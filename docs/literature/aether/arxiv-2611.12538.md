## What they wanted, and why
A multilingual model generating text has to settle two things at once: what to say and which language to say it in. The final string entangles both, so a behavioural comparison across languages cannot say where either decision is made or in what order. The authors want a layer-by-layer causal account of which of the two a given residual-stream state actually supplies, and they want it to hold up when the *test item* differs in a known way. The larger ambition is stated plainly: if low-resource failures are as much about when language selection is established inside the forward pass as about missing knowledge, then the timing is itself an object worth measuring — and the method they build is meant to be a template for any task where an output conflates content with a task-level commitment.

## What they studied
Three pretrained (not instruction-tuned) decoder-only models: gemma-3-4b-pt as the primary subject, replicated on Qwen3.5-9B-Base and Llama-3.1-8B — so three scales, three tokenizer families, three SAE families and two attention designs (Qwen interleaves linear-attention layers). Data is multi-parallel: FLORES+ devtest for translation, topped up from NTREX-128 through the same pipeline, and Belebele for reading comprehension, whose passages are drawn from the same FLORES material. The unit of analysis is a single residual-stream vector at one position — the last token of the prompt, which in both templates is a fixed token (the trailing target-language marker for translation, `Answer:` for multiple choice) — swept one layer at a time. The SAE layer uses Gemma Scope 2 residual dictionaries for the primary model and the Qwen-Scope and Llama-Scope TopK dictionaries for the replications.

## How they did it
The core construction is a *success–failure contrast on identical content*: a segment that the model translates well in one direction (HIGH) and badly in another (LOW), with pairs filtered per language pair by an xCOMET-XL margin so that a global cutoff does not either destroy the LOW side or admit near-ties. Patching then copies the HIGH last-token state into the LOW run at one layer and reads three things: segment quality against the LOW reference, which language the output comes out in (fastText on the generated text, cross-checked by counting characters exclusive to the target language, with a *flip onset layer* defined as the first layer where the target-exclusive share crosses a threshold), and, on Belebele, whether the patched run reproduces the HIGH pass's own answer. The design's key move is the donor panel: **matched** (the same instance's HIGH state), **shuffled** (another instance's HIGH state under a seeded derangement), **mean** (the per-layer average HIGH state rescaled to the average per-instance norm, so a null cannot be blamed on a shrunken donor), and **mean_diff** (the HIGH-minus-LOW mean difference added as a steering vector at the LOW state, i.e. the only additive donor). Since only the first sees the test segment, the four together separate content-carrying from content-free information. Pairs are grouped by what a patch *can* transfer: same-target pairs (no language change possible, so any gain is content), different-target-same-source pairs (content transfer uninformative, isolating the target-language decision), and disjoint pairs (both differ, donor controls needed). For the feature arm they encode the HIGH/LOW last-token residuals, rank features per pair, measure how much of the flip direction a top-k feature subspace spans by least squares (not by raw activation differences, so scale cannot understate the span), and then run three sparse interventions — ablate the LOW-associated features, add the HIGH-associated ones, both — as extra conditions in the same layer sweep, after manually checking the feature sets against a max-activating-feature dashboard.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **causal layer map with crosses**: for each layer, which kind of information that layer's last-token state causally supplies, established by what the donor panel reproduces and what it fails to reproduce, plus a feature-level verdict for each signal — sparse or not, and causally effective at this position or not. It is explicitly partial and the paper says so twice: the curves identify what is *available* at a state, "not necessarily where the underlying computation is performed", and a null under one position and one layer is not evidence of absence. The predictive content is ordinal and structural rather than numeric: a three-stage ordering (a content-independent English/high-resource default, then a content channel, then the specific target language), with onset layers that move between models.

## How they judged success
Paired per-instance differences averaged over instances, with a two-sided bootstrap test over resamples, for every chrF++ and xCOMET quality contrast; lateral language identification (fastText plus an exclusive-character count) for the flip fraction and the median flip onset layer; Belebele accuracy and exact-agreement with the HIGH pass for content transfer. The decisive comparisons are *within* the donor panel (matched against shuffled and against the rescaled mean), plus three controls on the sparse arm: two SAE widths, two steering weights, and an intervention that removes an equal amount of mass from random active features rather than from instance-specific ones. An intervention-free logit lens over three token masks (script blocks, sister-exclusive characters, English function words) is used as an independent check on the onset map.

## What they found
Three stages, replicated in all three models though not at the same depths. **(i) A content-independent English default.** Between the two layers in the middle of Gemma's stack all four donors jump together — matched, shuffled, the rescaled mean and the additive direction — so whether the donor comes from the matching segment or a random one makes no measurable difference, and every donor switches most instances into English. The median onset layer agrees across conditions, and the logit-lens appendix shows Latin-script mass stepping up at the same layers while English function-word mass rises and then decays. **(ii) A content channel opens one layer later.** From that layer matched separates from shuffled and stays separated to the last layer; shuffled becomes actively *worse* than the clean LOW run, which is what a content channel predicts. Belebele places its onset at the same layer with a different template and a different metric — matched lifts accuracy off chance while shuffled and the mean sit at chance, and the share of patched runs reproducing the HIGH pass's own answer jumps at that same layer, which is the paper's argument that the onset is a property of the model rather than of translation. **(iii) The specific target resolves late, and only for non-English targets.** English or high-resource Latin targets cluster at the middle onset with high flip fractions; sister languages (Nynorsk against Swedish, Ukrainian against Russian) sit many layers later with near-zero flip fraction, with cross-script targets between the two. Depth is *not* stable across models: the English flip onset is given in Table 1 as layer 16 for Gemma, 18 for Qwen and 13 for Llama, with content onset one layer later in each; Llama's transitions are ramps rather than steps and its donor identity makes a small but measurable difference at the flip layer.
**The feature-level dissociation is the sharpest result.** The cumulative R² with which the flip direction is spanned by its top SAE features sits high early, *dips exactly at the layers where the flip is causal*, and recovers afterwards; doubling the feature budget barely moves the dipped value. Rebuilding the same effect from the dictionary instead of from dense vectors fails on every English-target pair at both widths and both steering weights, while all three dense donors flip the outputs. The late sister-language distinction shows the converse: its direction is spanned well by top features, yet no identified feature set flips the language, and matched itself flips only a small fraction of instances at that layer. So "sparsely represented" and "causally effective at this position" come apart in both directions.
**Two further results worth keeping.** First, the late-layer anomaly where the *mean* donor overtakes matched on the character-level surface metric does not reproduce under the model-based metric, and the authors downgrade it to a surface-form effect — but the de-noising experiment contradicts their own interference story: removing instance-specific feature mass from the HIGH vector helps at the flip layer and then hurts progressively at later layers, and renormalising changes nothing while removing the same mass from random features is far worse, so it is the identity of the removed features, not their quantity, that carries the effect. Second, the whole feature arm is bounded by reconstruction fidelity, and the replication makes that concrete: Gemma's dictionaries reconstruct well enough for the shared/instance-specific split to be interpretable, Qwen's are much lower and the de-noised vector is dominated by reconstruction error, and Llama's are lower still, where the authors decline to run the sparse test at all and read the outcome as an instrument limit rather than a contradiction. The patching-level account, which needs no dictionary, transfers to all three.

## Limits and open problems they name
The intervention is deliberately narrow — one vector, last token, one layer at a time — so the curves describe availability at a state and not the site of the computation, and they warn that their negative results must not be read as evidence that a signal is absent, since it may live at other positions, be distributed across components, or need a multi-layer edit. Feature-level nulls are relative to the dictionaries studied and to their fidelity, so they are hard to interpret exactly where fidelity is low. Contrast instances are selected by xCOMET-XL, whose reliability varies across languages and may favour higher-resource settings. Scope is three pretrained models, a limited set of pairs and two tasks; they claim the *ordering*, not the layer locations or magnitudes. What they do not name, and what the delivery makes visible, is that the sparse intervention is tested at a single position, so "no feature flips the language" is a statement about last-token feature addition — the same caveat they give the patching negatives applies to the feature negatives and is not repeated there.

## What we take from it
Four things, in order of portability. **The donor panel is the best control ladder in this folder for "what did the copy actually carry"**: matched, deranged-shuffle, norm-rescaled mean, and an additive difference vector, each isolating a different claim, and the shuffled donor doubling as an anti-donor whose *harm* is evidence that a content channel is real rather than that patching is noisy. Any intervention of ours that copies a state should ship this four-way comparison, because matched-versus-clean alone cannot distinguish content from a task-level default. **Sparse representability and causal effectiveness are independent measurements, and this is the cleanest two-sided demonstration we have**: a direction can be spanned well by decoder vectors and still not move the model at that position, or move the model while being spanned poorly — the dip in span at exactly the causal layers is the shape to look for, because it says the effective direction is *distributed* rather than missing. That is a direct warning for our own "the dictionary recovered direction X" claims: reporting span alone certifies nothing about causality, and reporting a failed sparse intervention certifies nothing about the dictionary without a fidelity number beside it. **Onset order is a testable prediction and the cheap way to falsify it is a second task with a different metric**: the content onset appearing at the same layer under a translation template scored by chrF and a multiple-choice template scored by accuracy is what makes "the model has a layer" a claim rather than a coincidence, and the same design lets us separate a model-level schedule from a task artefact. **The replication is reported by its failure point, not its success**: the authors print the reconstruction fidelity of all three dictionary families, say at which fidelity the analysis stops being interpretable, and decline to run the test where it would be meaningless, while keeping the account that needs no dictionary. For our reading of weights, that is the model to copy — publish the instrument's resolution limit next to the result, and carry the argument at whichever level survives the instrument.
