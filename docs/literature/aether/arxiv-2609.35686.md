# Rethinking Circuit Evaluation: Do Circuits Explain Model Errors?

- **Key:** arxiv-2609.35686
- **Authors / organisation:** Li Zhang, Chuqin Geng (equal contribution), Mark Zhang, Chen Yang, Luke Zhang, Haolin Ye, Xujie Si; University of Toronto / McGill University / Tsinghua University
- **Date:** 2026-09-28
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.35686
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (arXiv HTML v1; abstract, §1–§7, references, Appendices A–D including the discovery implementations for ACDC/EAP-IG/Edge Pruning, both ablation-procedure appendices, Table 4 with every recovery attempt, Table 5's per-setting correct/error counts, Table 7's margin-matched table and Table 8's reference head list)
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

Circuits are validated by keeping a subnetwork and ablating everything else, and the standard faithfulness numbers describe how much of the *task score* survives. The authors ask a different question: does the circuit also reproduce the model's **mistakes**? Their point is that an account which regenerates every correct answer while silently producing the right answer on inputs the model gets wrong is not an account of the model's real decision — it is an account of the task the model is mostly solving. They argue the omission has a direct cost: an explanation that cannot reproduce a failure cannot explain why the failure happened. This matters to us because our project's target is exactly the thing their metric isolates — a white-box predictor should predict *what the network will do*, including where it goes wrong, not only where it succeeds, and the field's standard evaluation would score the former as perfect.

## What they studied

Five model–task settings and three task families. IOI on GPT-2 small (12 layers, 12 heads per layer; the published 26-head reference circuit of Wang et al.); the Docstring task on a four-layer, eight-head attention-only transformer (`NeelNanda/Attn_Only_4L512W_C4_Code`); and six settings from the Mechanistic Interpretability Benchmark (MIB) — ARC-Easy and ARC-Challenge on Llama-3.1 8B, Gemma-2 2B and Qwen-2.5 0.5B, plus subtraction on Llama-3.1 8B. The unit of analysis is the **circuit plus its ablation rule**: a subgraph of heads/edges is kept and everything else is replaced by mean, resampled, or learned constants. The object being measured is the joint distribution of (full-model prediction, circuit prediction) split into two strata — inputs the model answers correctly and inputs it answers wrongly.

## How they did it

Every task is scored as an argmax over a small candidate set (two names for IOI, fourteen argument tokens for Docstring, answer letters for ARC, integers 0–99 for subtraction), for both the full model and the circuit, with a fixed tie rule; a task instance is an error if the full model's choice disagrees with the label. Two numbers are then computed on disjoint strata: correct agreement (circuits reproducing the model on prompts it got right) and error agreement (circuits reproducing *the same wrong answer* on prompts it got wrong), plus their difference Δ and a companion statistic B_err for the share of error prompts where the circuit is wrong at all. Circuits came from four sources — the published manual reference, ACDC at two thresholds, EAP-IG with a full-vocabulary KL objective, and Edge Pruning at three seeds — each evaluated under mean, resample (same-template ABC counterfactuals for IOI, the generator's random-random corruption for Docstring, symbol or operand counterfactuals for MIB) and, for the fixed circuits, optimal ablation with learned per-component constants. Discovery, validation and test prompts were made disjoint and the IOI test sample retained all 884 model errors from a 100,000-prompt census, so the error stratum is not under-sampled. MIB circuits were swept across ten edge budgets and agreement was plotted per stratum against size. They then tried seven ways to close the gap (more discovery errors, upweighting errors in the discovery loss, error-enriched discovery sets, error-conditioned replacement values, output calibration, threshold calibration, and targeted circuit expansion) and measured each one's cost on correct agreement. Finally, an IOI case study searched for a compact extension of the reference circuit: a 3,574-candidate space of head–position additions was screened by gradients on relaxable gates and then evaluated exactly with binary gates, under an objective that rewards error agreement and penalises any drop in correct agreement beyond a 0.5-point tolerance, with a size penalty.

## What counts as an explanation here

A circuit plus a stated ablation rule, together with stratified numbers describing what it reproduces. The paper's claim is deliberately about the *evaluation*, not a new explanation formalism: it says the form the field already uses is admissible, but its success criterion is too weak by one stratum. The explanatory object is explicitly partial and adversarial to the usual one — their recommendation is a *necessary but not sufficient* test, and they say plainly that the metric does not establish that the circuit recovers the model's mechanism. The predictive content is narrow and concrete: the recovery analysis shows that omitted computations produce specific wrong answers on a reproducible subset of errors, and that a 63-node extension reproduces those wrong answers on held-out prompts.

## How they judged success

Exact answer agreement conditioned on model-correct versus model-error strata, with 10,000 percentile bootstrap intervals that resample within IOI sampling cells and condition on fitted masks; normalized task-score faithfulness F = (mean margin of circuit − mean margin of empty control)/(mean margin of model − mean margin of empty control), with graph- and ablation-specific empty controls; MIB's CPR and CMD aggregates over the budget sweep; a margin-matching control that pairs error and correct examples within 0.1 logit of top1-minus-top2 margin; structurally matched random circuit extensions as the null for the recovery claim; and a fitted scalar subject-logit bias calibrated to the extension's correct agreement as the control for "any nudge toward the wrong name would have done as well".

## What they found

Under mean ablation on IOI, the manual circuit and the automated circuits reproduce 97.3–99.5% of correct choices but only 11.4–41.7% of errors — the manual circuit itself is at 15.2% error agreement with F = 0.956, and ACDC's Δ is 85.5 percentage points. Under optimal ablation the gap narrows but does not close (manual 99.9 correct vs 10.0 error; ACDC 8.1). Circuits discovered with a full-output-distribution KL objective still show the gap, so matching the model's distribution is not sufficient. Docstring is more heterogeneous and shows the trap in the metric: the manual circuit's gap falls from 41.3 to 8.2 points under resampling mostly because correct agreement collapses from 77.1% to 47.2%, while EAP-IG under mean ablation reaches 69.4% error agreement against 65.6% correct (Δ = −3.8). On MIB the gap persists on Gemma ARC-Challenge even though 430 of 1,119 discovery examples (38.4%) are errors, so it is not a rarity artefact. Of the seven remedies, error upweighting lifts IOI ACDC error agreement from 9.3% to 37.0% while correct agreement falls 98.7% to 91.0%; error-enriched discovery reaches 44.2% with the same roughly 7-point correct loss; and error-conditioned replacement values help but a plain scalar shift toward the subject name recovers at least as many errors, which they flag as the decisive control. A candidate-specific logit calibration on Qwen ARC-Challenge raises error agreement 38.5% → 62.8% and correct agreement 59.3% → 70.3%, which they note does not make the underlying circuit causally faithful. Margin matching shrinks several gaps but leaves positive matched gaps in all twelve IOI configurations (e.g. manual mean ablation 94.9 vs 14.8). In the case study, 63 added head–position rules (about 61.5 extra head outputs per prompt) raise held-out error agreement from 14.22% to 75.11% at a cost of 0.41 points of correct agreement (99.34% → 98.93%), beating matched random extensions (17.33–41.33%, median 29.33%) and the calibrated scalar bias (20.44%); on a fresh 5,200-prompt check the correct loss was 0.85 points, exceeding their own 0.5-point tolerance. Mechanistically, the recovered errors come from two routes: restored contextual inputs change the S-inhibition heads' queries (path-patching the S2 inhibition contribution into the reference circuit's name-mover queries reproduces 120 of the 137 newly recovered errors), and restored early name-position heads change the keys through which name movers retrieve the correct name (patching those keys reproduces 35 of 137, and none when patched only at the subject positions). Recovery survives a change of ablation rule: under resampling, adding the extension moves error agreement 40.89% → 60.89% and correct agreement 91.41% → 97.67%.

## Limits and open problems they name

Their conclusions are scoped to the tested tasks, models, circuits and ablation settings; the case study is a single IOI reference circuit and does not establish that the recovered mechanisms generalise; and while several recovery strategies worked partially, they neither exhausted the remedies nor produced a general method that improves error reproduction while consistently preserving correct behaviour. They also note that the discrepancy can be masked by a collapsing correct stratum, and that their attempts at output calibration are fitted on evaluation correct cases. Mine, logged as mine: the recovered-error sample is small (137 errors from a 60,000-prompt base, 225 errors in the final 26,000-prompt hold-out), and the extension's correct-agreement slack was exceeded on fresh data — a small but real indication that the 0.5-point tolerance was tuned on validation. No figure was inspected: Figures 1–3 are images, so the stratified agreement curves by circuit size are recorded as the authors describe them and every number here is a table cell or an explicit sentence.

## What we take from it

Four concrete things. (i) **A stratification requirement for every evaluation we publish**: the folder's circuit, probe, dictionary and steering evaluations all report a single pooled score, and this is the cheapest demonstration that a pooled score can be almost perfect on the half of the distribution the model gets right. Any predictor of ours should be scored on model-success and model-failure strata separately, with the identical-answer requirement on the failure stratum (a surrogate that emits the *correct* answer where the model is wrong is a disagreement, not a bonus). (ii) **A discovery-budget caveat**: exposing the extraction to errors does not make it explain them, and the controls matter more than the fix — the scalar-bias control is the one that shows most of the "error-conditioned" recovery was direction, not mechanism, and it is exactly the control this folder habitually omits. (iii) **A composition warning**: the agreement gap is not one phenomenon but a bundle of siting, margin and objective effects, and it can be narrowed by losing the correct stratum, so the two numbers must be reported as a pair, never as a single "gap improved". (iv) **A positive existence proof**: a compact targeted extension of a published circuit can move error reproduction from 14% to 75% with less than half a point of correct loss, which is the strongest evidence in the folder that published circuits are genuinely missing load-bearing computation rather than merely being under-tuned.
