# Navigating by Old Maps: The Pitfalls of Static Mechanistic Localization in LLM Post-Training

- **Key:** arxiv-2605.06076
- **Authors / organisation:** Hang Chen, Jiaying Zhu, Hongyang Chen, Hongxu Liu, Xinyu Yang, Wenya Wang; Xi'an Jiaotong University / Chinese University of Hong Kong / China Mobile / Nanyang Technological University

- **Read from:** full text (arXiv HTML v1, all 96,104 extracted chars read in five slices: abstract and §1, §2–§3 with the three metrics, §4.1–§4.2.2 and §5–§6, references, and Appendices A–G including the logical-circuit construction, the Ns+Dn objective, Tables 3–14)
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
A large family of post-training methods locates a computation with interpretability tools, freezes everything else, and trains only the located part: unlearning, knowledge editing, targeted fine-tuning. The whole approach assumes that a component set identified on the current weights is still the relevant set after the weights move. The authors ask whether that assumption holds — whether a static mechanistic map describes the terrain the optimiser is about to walk into. Their framing is deliberately adversarial to their own field: if the map is stale, then the success of the method may be a property of the *tasks* it was tested on rather than of the localisation. The larger goal is a form of localisation with foresight, i.e. a prediction of the post-update circuit rather than a description of the current one — which is the same object our project wants, restricted to the training axis instead of the input axis.

## What they studied
Mistral-7B-v0.3 and LLaMA-3-8B under full-parameter SFT, over 15 standard interpretability tasks split into target and "pervasiveness" (must-not-degrade) roles: Arithmetic, Bool, Gender, SST2, Winogrande and Induction as target/skill tasks, FEVER and zsRE as factual-knowledge tasks, OpenBookQA as the usual pervasiveness task, plus IOI, Docstring, RTE, Reverse, Greater Than and SA. Unit of analysis: the smallest update unit is an individual parameter matrix (W_q, W_k, W_v, W_o, W_up, W_down), and the object tracked is an edge-weight *distribution* over the computational graph, re-extracted at 20 observation steps inside each of 10 epochs. A circuit is a subgraph, and the paper treats the causal-effect vector over all edges as the circuit's identity rather than the set of selected nodes.

## How they did it
Three quantities are defined and then tracked through SFT. Circuit distance is a normalised Manhattan distance between the causal-effect vectors of two parameter states, summed over every edge in the graph, so a change in any edge's measured importance counts even if the selected set does not change. Circuit stability is the expected Spearman rank correlation between circuits extracted from two independent 20% subsets of the target data at the same checkpoint — a check on whether the mechanism is consolidated enough to be sampled reliably at all. Circuit conflict maps the target and pervasiveness circuits into conjunctive normal form using a logical-circuit construction (AND, OR and ADDER gates separated by running circuit discovery under both noising and denoising interventions) and reports the size of the UNSAT core of their conjunction via a SAT solver, so redundancy is handled properly rather than by taking a naive intersection. Edges are discovered by activation-patching effects (default interchange ablation) with three algorithms compared — ACDC, EAP, and a differentiable hard-concrete mask they call EdgePruning. The main comparison is three training conditions at matched compute: free full-parameter SFT, mechanistic localisation (freeze the complement of the discovered circuit, keeping 800 critical components), and random localisation (freeze the same complement shape but keep 800 randomly chosen components with the same attention/MLP ratio). The localised runs are then repeated at circuit scales from 500 to 4,000 components. Surveys over the pervasiveness degree (0–10 stacked tasks), dataset size (500 to 100,000), the proportion of components shared between target and pervasiveness circuits (5.86% to 15.63%), and initial task mastery (including a curated 100%-accuracy subset) decompose what drives the movement. Finally, they test the one thing their own diagnosis implies: a "future" localisation that uses the circuit obtained *after* free evolution as if it were the current one, and compare gradient-based and intervention-based localisers (WAGLE, DEPN, MEMIT, knowledge neurons) against circuit-based ones.

## What counts as an explanation here
A circuit plus a trajectory, not a snapshot: the explanation is a claim about which components carry a task, restated as a claim about how that set moves under optimisation. The three metrics are the descriptive vocabulary — distance (where did the computation go), stability (how well is it pinned down), conflict (does it collide with another task's computation) — and they are meant to be predictive instruments rather than faithfulness scores. The paper's constructive proposal is explicitly predictive: an explanation worth having is one that says what the circuit will be after the update, so a map is graded by how well it anticipates dynamics. No complete account of any task's computation is offered or claimed; the unit is partial and set-valued throughout.

## How they judged success
Primary: target-task accuracy and pervasiveness accuracy after SFT, against the pre-SFT baseline, across three localisation strategies. Secondary: the three circuit metrics over training, with per-task trajectories for the five main target tasks. Controls and ablations: random localisation matched on component count and attention/MLP ratio (the paper's load-bearing null); circuit-scale sweep; discovery-algorithm comparison; single-objective reruns with the pervasiveness task removed to rule out the multi-task setting as the cause; five seeds with a reported narrow 95% interval (t = 2.776) though only representative runs are plotted. No human ratings, no benchmark beyond the 15 tasks, no significance testing of the main localisation contrast beyond the seed intervals.

## What they found
Circuits are not static. Without localisation, critical components migrate during SFT, and the migration splits along a well-known axis: attention components move far more than MLP components, and attention-dominated "skill" tasks (Induction, Reverse) show circuit movements roughly twice those of MLP-dominated factual tasks (FEVER, zsRE), while the factual tasks instead consolidate in place, with MLP stability around 0.93–0.95 against attention stability around 0.85 among the skill tasks. Even tasks the model already answers correctly keep moving — a 100%-accuracy subset still shows attention circuit distance near 0.8–1.06 in the units used — and larger datasets do not stop the migration, they only improve consolidation. More pervasiveness tasks, and more conflict between the target and pervasiveness components, both increase migration.

The decisive comparison is that mechanistic localisation beats free SFT but does not beat random localisation of the same size. Both localised conditions improve target accuracy and reduce conflict over the free baseline, and both are then indistinguishable from each other; the mechanistic condition even shows *larger* circuit distance and lower stability than free training, which the authors read as evidence that it freezes components the future computation needs. Mechanistic localisation only separates from random once the kept set is expanded to roughly 3,000–3,500 components, at which point it contains about 80% of the model's components and the whole exercise has collapsed back toward full-parameter training. The "illusion of effectiveness" in the literature is then explained by task choice: on MLP-dominated knowledge tasks such as bio-hazard unlearning, mechanistic localisation does clearly beat both random and free training on forget efficacy (0.736 vs 0.716 vs 0.685) at comparable retain utility (0.457 vs 0.441 vs 0.467), precisely because those circuits barely move; on the attention-dominated Induction task it is worse than random (forget efficacy 0.955 vs 0.952 and retain utility 0.367 vs 0.359) with much higher circuit distance. Both patterns survive removing the pervasiveness task. Gradient-based localisers (WAGLE, DEPN) get the lowest circuit distance and highest stability of all localisers tested on Arithmetic, which the authors take as evidence that a method which already knows the gradient direction has some foresight, and EAP-derived circuits beat ACDC and EdgePruning for the same reason.

## Limits and open problems they name
Circuit discovery does not scale to very large models or very large data, which caps how far the analysis can be pushed. Localisation and update method are often co-designed (their own baseline uses vanilla SFT to keep them separable), so the effect of coupling specialised updaters remains unmeasured. The paper's "future localisation" proof of concept uses the post-hoc circuit as a stand-in for a prediction, which is an oracle, not a method: nothing in it could be run before the update. Mine, marked: there are no error bars on the main localisation comparison, only seed intervals on accuracy, and the differences that carry the "beats random" verdict at the 3,000–3,500-component scale are small enough that a proper paired test is needed before reading them as real. The claim that MLP dominance is the reason localisation works on knowledge tasks is correlational — the paper does not run localisation on an MLP-dominated task whose circuit nevertheless moves. And the metrics are not commensurable across papers: circuit distance is normalised by the empirical range of the causal effect, so the numbers are comparable within this paper's tables and not across studies.

## What we take from it
Method: a circuit's identity should be the causal-effect vector over all edges, not the selected set, because the selected set can look identical while the underlying effects drift; that is the cheapest possible instrument for any read-out we track over time (training steps, checkpoints, fine-tuning stages). Method: stability as a *sampling* property — expected rank correlation between two data halves at the same checkpoint — is a direct test of whether a mechanism is consolidated, and it needs no ground truth, so it belongs in our eval set beside the resample-Jaccard statistic the variance entry already gave us. Framing: the paper's central question is ours stated on the training axis — does a description of the current network predict the network after intervention — and its answer (only where the description is dynamic, i.e. built from gradients) argues that any white-box model we ship should be scored under weight updates, not only under input changes. Measure: the matched random localisation is the control the whole field of "our localisation improves downstream performance" owes, and it costs one extra run. Warning: the convergence of a localised subset to 80% of the network at the scale where it finally wins is the compactness-versus-coverage trade we have logged before, now with an explicit saturation point. Shortcut: the UNSAT-core conflict measure is a cheap way to ask two mechanisms whether they share computation, and the noising-plus-denoising pair is a principled way to say whether an edge is redundant (OR) or essential (AND) without assuming either.
