## What they wanted, and why
Sparse autoencoders are used as a read-out interface into a network's internals, and the field has quietly assumed that the atoms they learn are real structure rather than one run's accident. Two independent trainings on the same activations can return different dictionaries and, more importantly, different codes for the same input. The authors refuse to assume the atoms are interpretable objects and instead treat the SAE as a statistical estimator of the classical dictionary-learning problem, then ask the compressed-sensing question: under what conditions is that estimator consistent? The larger goal is a guarantee rather than a hope — a white-box read-out whose units are recoverable run to run, and therefore reusable as the vocabulary in which a model is described.

## What they studied
Units are dictionary atoms (4096 per dictionary) and the amortised sparse codes (sparsity k = 40) that a TopK-style encoder produces. Four data regimes: two synthetic (768-dimensional, atoms drawn i.i.d. or from a mixture of Gaussians projected to the unit sphere, codes unit-Gaussian then sparsified), and two real (layer 12 of Pythia-160M on The Pile; DINOv2-Base patch tokens on ImageNet-1k), both 768-dimensional. Four model variants are compared: plain TopK; AbsTopK (top-k by magnitude, so coefficients may be negative); iSAE (AbsTopK plus their aRIP regulariser); iSAE-ME (iSAE plus a multi-step encoder borrowed from LISTA). All variants share an identical training recipe — unit-norm inputs and atoms, tied pre/post bias initialised at the data's geometric median, encoder initialised as the decoder's approximate left inverse, auxiliary loss against dead atoms, no repeated tokens, 512M tokens for the LLM run.

## How they did it
They first state what identifiability should mean for an SAE: two independent trainings on the same distribution must agree on the dictionary and on the codes up to a signed permutation. Then they prove an impossibility result — there exists a normalised dictionary for which two different continuous k-sparse encoders reach the same reconstruction accuracy while disagreeing on a positive-measure set of inputs and on the sparsity patterns there. So dictionary agreement cannot imply code agreement. The repair is a relaxation of the restricted isometry property: rather than demanding near-orthogonality of every k-subset of atoms, they demand it only on unions of two observed support sets, and they turn that into a per-sample scalar by taking the mean squared deviation of the eigenvalues of the Gram submatrix from 1. That scalar is estimated with Hutchinson-style random probes inside the training loop and used as a regulariser at weight 0.1. Separately they make the encoder multi-step (five iterations of a step-size matrix, a threshold and the linear map, borrowed from the learned-ISTA line of work), because a near-linear encoder may not be expressive enough to amortise the codes the well-conditioned dictionary admits. Agreement is measured two ways: dictionary cosine similarity after Hungarian matching, and — the part the folder should notice — code intersection-over-union and normalised l2 error on the matched supports. An orthogonal-matching-pursuit solver run on the learned dictionary is included as an oracle, so the encoder can be compared against the best code recovery that dictionary allows.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not a description of what the atoms mean. The explanation is a *sufficient condition plus a measurable diagnostic*: a dictionary that satisfies approximate RIP on observed support unions, with the codes near-identifiable up to permutation, and a learnable proxy (the eigenvalue-deviation scalar) that can be regularised during training. It is explicitly partial — it says nothing about whether the atoms correspond to the data-generating process, and the authors warn that verifying true RIP remains NP-hard, so nothing here recovers ground-truth concepts. It does predict: a dictionary whose aRIP constant is small should yield matching codes across seeds, and the paper tests exactly that ordering.

## How they judged success
Reconstruction MSE; pairwise dictionary cosine similarity after Hungarian matching; pairwise code IoU and normalised l2 error after the same matching and sign alignment; the measured aRIP scalar; the OMP oracle on the same dictionary; and SAEBench on Pythia-160M (CE-loss inflation, sparse probing, spurious-correlation removal, targeted probe perturbation). Identifiability is estimated across independently seeded trainings on identical data. No confidence intervals or significance tests are reported for the pairwise identifiability numbers, and no sweep over the regularisation weight or the encoder iteration count (only a remark that 3–10 iterations behave similarly).

## What they found
On synthetic data the ordering is clean and the mechanism separates. AbsTopK cuts reconstruction error by roughly 30–40% and moves dictionary cosine similarity from about 0.60 to above 0.91 in the i.i.d. case; the oracle already recovers codes well from that dictionary while the encoder still does not — dictionary agreement bought, code agreement not. The multi-step encoder closes that: iSAE-ME reaches the lowest MSE (0.001 i.i.d., 0.040 mixture) with 0.997 and 0.873 dictionary cosine similarity. On real activations the picture flips in two instructive ways. AbsTopK *hurts* reconstruction there (Pythia MSE 0.177 against 0.166) while giving the best dictionary similarity (0.631 against 0.398) and the best oracle code scores; and the expressive encoder, which attains the paper's lowest LLM reconstruction error (0.148), *worsens* both dictionary and code agreement (0.375 similarity, 0.297 code IoU against iSAE's 0.526 and 0.256 — with iSAE the better of the two on codes). On SAEBench iSAE-ME wins CE loss (3.906 against 3.974) and targeted probe perturbation, but plain TopK wins sparse probing (0.909) and spurious-correlation removal (0.381), which the authors attribute to zero having a natural meaning as "absence" under a one-sided activation.

## Limits and open problems they name
Only TopK-family SAEs; the LISTA-style encoder was built for ReLU-style activations and its behaviour there is untested. Agreement on real activations remains far from perfect, and the authors hypothesise the remaining gap is optimisation, not capacity — an explanation rather than a demonstrated cause. One synthetic generator and two real layers; no scale study. The aRIP guarantee holds only on the training distribution, and the impossibility theorem shows that good reconstruction is not by itself enough. Notably, the version we read carries a self-audit: the proof of the main theorem was formalised in Lean after acceptance, the original diversity assumption was found too weak to carry the argument for a *pair* of models, and Appendix A replaces it and says the earlier version, with errors, remains on arXiv — the corrected assumption now applies to a single model's code distribution over a set of positive measure.

## What we take from it
Three things, all instruments. **First, a metric to copy**: if our account claims a per-unit structure is "recovered", we report agreement on *what fires where* (support IoU) beside agreement on the direction (cosine), because the paper's impossibility theorem says the first can fail while the second holds, and its tables show both moving independently in real models. **Second, a framing**: identifiability defined as agreement of two independent runs up to the trivially irrelevant symmetries (permutation and sign) is a cheap, checkable target that needs no ground truth — and it is a *pre-requisite* for any stronger claim, so it belongs at the bottom of our validation ladder rather than at the top. **Third, an oracle control we have not been using**: solve the same inference problem with an expensive exact solver on the structure our account recovered, and compare the cheap read-out against it. When the oracle wins, the structure was fine and the read-out is the bottleneck; when the cheap read-out wins, the structure is not well-conditioned. That two-by-two is a diagnosis, not a score, and it is the cheapest way to tell "our units are wrong" from "our way of finding the code is wrong".
