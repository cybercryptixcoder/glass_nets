# Negated and Misprimed Probes for Pretrained Language Models: Birds Can Talk, But Cannot Fly

- **Key:** 2020.acl-main.698
- **Authors / organisation:** Nora Kassner, Hinrich Schütze — Center for Information and Language Processing (CIS), LMU Munich
- **Date:** 2020-07 (ACL 2020, July 5-10, Online)
- **Link:** https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.698/
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 1 (background)

## What they wanted, and why
Fact-recall benchmarks for language models (LAMA and its descendants) turn knowledge-base triples into cloze sentences and score the model by whether the right object fills the mask. A high score is habitually read as evidence that the model has *stored the fact*. This paper asks whether that reading survives two manipulations that are trivial for a human reader: putting the sentence in the negative, and prefixing it with a short, plainly wrong hint. The larger goal is a test that distinguishes pattern completion from knowledge, since a model that recalls "Birds can fly" and also happily completes "Birds cannot fly" with *fly* is not representing the fact at all but the co-occurrence of subject and answer.

## What they studied
Five pretrained language models of that era — Transformer-XL large, ELMo (original and 5.5B) and BERT-base/BERT-large — evaluated on LAMA's four sources (Google-RE, T-REx, ConceptNet, SQuAD). The unit of analysis is the model's full ranked prediction over the mask, compared between an original sentence and a minimally altered one. A second experiment swaps the real corpus for a small synthetic one where negation is balanced by construction, isolating what pretraining can learn about negation with no frequency imbalance.

## How they did it
Two derived datasets are built on top of LAMA, with no retraining of the evaluated models. **Negated LAMA**: a negation element is inserted by hand into each template or question, so every positive sentence has a paired negative one ("The theory of relativity was not developed by [MASK]"); for ConceptNet only a subset that negates cleanly (short sentences or a fixed list of patterns) is kept, negated via a hand-built verb dictionary, leaving 2,996 of 11,458 items. **Misprimed LAMA**: an incorrect word plus a question mark is prepended ("Talk? Birds can [MASK]"), with four generation modes — random words (A), a correct filler belonging to a *different* instance of the same relation (B), the model's own highly ranked but wrong filler for the same question (C), and C with twenty neutral sentences inserted between prime and mask (D). For every mode the harness first restricts to questions BERT-large already answers correctly, so any drop is attributable to the prime. For the negation question they additionally pretrain BERT-base from scratch on a tiny synthetic corpus of 200 subjects, 20 adjectives forming 10 antonym pairs, and a hidden knowledge base that fixes which adjective fits which group of subjects; training covers all positive and negative sentences for 70% of subjects and only one polarity for the rest, which is moved to test. They then check memorisation of seen sentences, generalisation to unseen subjects, and finally finetuning on a true/false classification of the same sentences.

## What counts as an explanation here
There is no mechanistic explanation and none is attempted — no probe, no ablation, no direction. The claim is behavioural and, in the authors' framing, a *diagnostic*: two perturbations that should not change a knowledge-bearing representation are shown to change the answer, therefore the benchmark score measures surface matching rather than stored facts. The evidence form is a table of prediction agreements plus worked prediction examples with log-probabilities.

## How they judged success
Two complementary statistics over the positive/negative pair: the Spearman rank correlation of the two prediction distributions, and the percentage overlap of the rank-1 fillers. The reasoning is that a model which genuinely handles negation should produce *unrelated* top answers for the pair, so high correlation and high overlap both indicate failure. For mispriming the statistic is absolute precision drop from the subset the model already answered correctly. Two controls are present: a positive-positive correlation baseline (pairs of different questions from the same template, whose agreement is significantly lower), and mode A as a sanity check that the mere presence of a prefix is not itself harmful.

## What they found
Across nearly all sources and models the positive and negative prediction distributions are close: mean Spearman above 85% in most cells, with rank-1 overlap often large — the clearest single illustration being that "Birds can [MASK]" and "Birds cannot [MASK]" both return *fly* as the top word. BERT is the least bad of the five, and nearly all models are near-identical between the two polarities on ConceptNet. The outliers are interpretable rather than fatal: Google-RE birth-date is anomalous because the negated template is rare, and on T-REx 1-1 the models do change their answer, but to words like *listed* or *known*, i.e. they treat the negated sentence as a different, plausible sentence rather than as a truth claim. In the synthetic corpus, the model memorises both polarities of seen sentences at high accuracy but sits at chance (0.2) on the unseen test sets for both, with test accuracy plateauing while training loss falls, so failure is not overfitting; finetuned on true/false labels it reaches 1.0, showing the information is learnable when supervision names it. For mispriming, a single distracting word from the model's own top candidates costs more than 60% precision in most relations, and inserting twenty sentences between prime and mask barely helps. Random misprimes (A) are far less harmful, which is the crucial contrast: implausible prefixes are handled, plausibly-wrong ones are not.

## Limits and open problems they name
The misprimes are automatic and less natural than a human would write, a trade the authors accept to get scale. There is no way for the model to abstain — not answering is not an option in a cloze task — and while probabilities are generally lower for negated answers, no threshold separates valid positives from invalid negatives across questions. They offer an explanation only as a suspicion: negated sentences are rarer in pretraining data, so the nearest neighbour of a negative sentence is usually a positive one. They name three openings: facts and truth values are discrete while current representations are not, confidence must distinguish informed from random guesses, and human-easy tests are a good source of probes.

## What we take from it
A **minimal-pair battery** as the cheapest probe of whether a read-out encodes a proposition or a string: take the sentence, flip one truth-relevant element, and measure the agreement of the two prediction distributions rather than the accuracy of either. Agreement-as-failure is the inversion worth copying — the usual battery asks "did the intervention break the answer", and here the correct answer to "did it not break the answer" is itself the finding. The misprime ladder (random, same-type, the model's own near-miss, the model's own near-miss at distance) is a **dose-response over distractor plausibility**, and its headline result is the warning: the intervention that a model resists is the implausible one, so random perturbation is the weakest possible stress test and a battery built only on random controls will overstate robustness of a read-out. The finetuned-versus-pretrained gap is a **separate-decodability-from-use** signature in its purest form — the information is fully present and fully unusable, which is exactly the distinction the folder's probing entries keep circling. Finally the honesty pattern is reusable: they report where the manipulation *fails* to bite (T-REx 1-1, Google-RE birth-date) and interpret it, which prevents the aggregate average from being read as a uniform effect.
