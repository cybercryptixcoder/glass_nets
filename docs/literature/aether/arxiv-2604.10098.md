## What they wanted, and why
The sink literature grew fast and in incompatible vocabularies: efficiency papers talk about which cache slots to keep, mechanism papers talk about logits and outliers, safety papers talk about hallucination and backdoors, and each line reports its own evidence for what a sink *is*. This survey sets out to make the field legible as one research programme, sorting some two hundred papers into three dimensions it treats as a historical progression — utilization first (2023 onward), then mechanistic interpretation (2024 onward), then mitigation (2025 onward) — and asks one question per dimension: how sinks are exploited, why they exist, and how they could be designed away. The stated ambition is to be the reference that a researcher entering the area reads instead of re-deriving the taxonomy, and to point out which of the three dimensions is failing to deliver. The larger goal, visible in its framing, is to move the field from exploiting a phenomenon to engineering against it.

## What they studied
Nine transformer families, each given its own architecture sketch and its own sink signature: encoder-only classical models (BERT/RoBERTa, sinks on `[CLS]`, `[SEP]`, and later punctuation), decoder-only LLMs (sinks on initial tokens, strong delimiters and weak-semantic tokens, with layer-wise escalation), mixture-of-experts LLMs (sink tokens getting high router scores in a very small expert subset), multimodal LLMs (text-side sinks inherited from the backbone plus visual sinks on background patches), vision transformers (sinks on high-norm background patches, spatially concentrated near image boundaries), diffusion transformers (sinks concentrated in the *central* layers and carrying global scene information), diffusion language models (sinks that *move* during generation rather than sitting at the start), linear and hybrid-linear attention models, vision-language-action models, omni-modal LLMs, autoregressive video diffusion, and streaming 3D transformers. The unit of analysis shifts with the dimension: attention columns and value norms for utilization, weight/activation/attention outliers and geometric positions for interpretation, and architectural slots — gates, softmax variants, bias parameters, optimizers, prefix tokens — for mitigation. No experiments are run; the paper surveys.

## How they did it
The method is taxonomic sorting plus a load-bearing convention: every mechanistic claim is grouped by the *kind* of explanation it is, and then re-read through one question — does an intervention that implements this explanation remove the sink? §4.1 collects the softmax-limitation and no-op family and marshals two evidence classes for it, observational (sink tokens have conspicuously small value states, across BERT, ViT, LLaMA, OLMo, and KV-quantization work) and causal (relax the sum-to-one constraint and the sink goes away: gating after or before the attention output, clipped softmax, softmax with a `+1` denominator, an unnormalized rectified softmax, plain sigmoid attention). §4.2 then layers a numerically specific account on top of it — a chain in which up/gate weight columns produce down-projection input spikes, down-projection weight columns turn those into layer-output spikes, and the spiked tokens' query/key alignment produces the attention outlier — and validates it with interventions at each link. §4.3 re-reads the same facts functionally, showing that the sink's contribution to every query's attention output is nearly constant across queries and inputs, hence a bias term, and that supplying an explicit key/value bias removes the need for it. §4.4 collects geometric readings (positional-vector decomposition, orthogonality-to-the-sink as an importance measure, near-zero key similarity to the mean key). §4.5 lists six further framings and then produces the survey's one synthesizing move: a table of all eleven interpretations arranged on five analytical levels — mathematical origin, training dynamics, numerical mechanism, geometric structure, functional role — with a note on what each level does and does not explain. Mitigation is then read along a different cut: methods that supply an explicit substitute for the implicit sink (gating, learnable biases) versus methods that cut the causal chain (modified softmax, pre-training interventions, norm-layer changes), each with a named cost.

## What counts as an explanation here
Five kinds, deliberately not unified: a *mathematical necessity* argument (the softmax constraint admits no null option, so a head wanting to do nothing must spend its mass somewhere and learn a near-zero value there), a *numerical circuit* (named weight objects producing named activation outliers producing the attention column), a *functional role* (a constant bias added to every token's attention output), a *geometric* reading (a stable reference point that other tokens' representations move toward), and a *residual* category of six further framings. Each is explicitly partial. The survey's own admission, in its future-directions section, is that no coherent framework integrates them, and that the missing piece across almost all of them is training dynamics: the softmax constraint says why a no-op is *possible*, none of the accounts says why optimization *chose* one, when, or driven by what. There is no predictive model in the survey — it offers a taxonomy of explanations, not an explanation that forecasts.

## How they judged success
Success criteria are per-dimension and mostly inherited from the surveyed work rather than imposed: a sink *rate* or fraction of attention mass on the sink column before and after an intervention (reported as percentages of first-token attention, e.g. roughly two-thirds down to a few percent for a denominator change, and about 47% down to under 5% for gated attention), activation *kurtosis* or maximum magnitude before and after (four-figure to single-figure reductions under softmax and optimizer changes), quantization fidelity (whether 4-bit or 8-bit deployment survives), downstream benchmark and perplexity retention, and causal tests where a source of outliers is pruned and the sink collapses. The survey's own evaluative contribution is a repeated complaint rather than a metric: it states that there is no standardized benchmark for sink mitigation, so effectiveness, computational overhead and parameter count cannot be compared fairly across methods, and that this absence is a principal obstacle. Its "Key Takeaways" boxes — concept, evidence, discussion — are the closest thing to a rubric, and the discussion text is where it grades each family.

## What they found
The taxonomy's central empirical claim is that across every architecture surveyed the sink token occupies an unusual position on a *pair* of axes, not one: an extremely large share of attention mass and a conspicuously small value-side write. The clearest quantitative anchors it quotes are that the first token takes the maximum attention in the overwhelming majority of heads in LLM checkpoints (98% of heads is the figure it gives for LLaMA), that the same phenomenon appears in non-causal models on delimiters and background patches, and that sinks are not confined to position zero — decoders sink on strong delimiters, diffusion language models sink on *moving* positions, and diffusion transformers sink in the middle of the stack carrying semantic content rather than on a null token. Its most useful and most uncomfortable finding for mechanism work is differential: ablation damage is architecture-dependent and sometimes anti-correlated with how much the sink looks like a sink. Removing sinks costs almost nothing in some diffusion-transformer configurations with respect to text alignment but shifts perceptual detail by a large multiple of a random control; in diffusion language models sink removal causes only minor degradation because the sink moves; yet in ViTs the same removal causes severe degradation, and in MoE LLMs pruning three of several thousand experts collapses the sink and with it coherence and reasoning. The mitigation section's honest negative is that essentially every effective method — gating, modified softmax, explicit bias, pre-training intervention — requires training from scratch, so none of it can be applied to an existing checkpoint, and the survey names lightweight post-hoc adaptation as an unexplored gap rather than a solved problem. Its scattering of mechanism-specific numbers all point the same way: the interventions that remove the sink are those that give the model a way to be silent *without* extreme logits.

## Limits and open problems they name
Their own limitations paragraph confines the survey to mature transformer families and excludes the emerging ones (hybrid-linear, 3D-geometry transformers) for lack of material — the same families the rest of the document then names repeatedly in its future-work section, which is a circularity worth noting. Beyond that paragraph the open problems are stated bluntly and are the document's most valuable content: training dynamics are unformalized in *all four* mechanistic accounts (when circuits emerge, how a mutual reinforcement between attention scores and value states locks in, why value norms get suppressed in the first place); causal validation is partial everywhere, with the outlier-circuit account in particular resting on a few interventions rather than a systematic component-wise sweep; the geometric account is largely correlational; practical methods are built for static sink positions although sinks demonstrably move; most interventions break kernel compatibility or run only after softmax; and no benchmark exists to compare mitigations. It also flags that the explanations are not commensurable — the five-level table organizes them but does not test them against each other.

## What we take from it
Three things. First, an **audit of our own vocabulary**: this folder's sink entries from run 704 onward align almost one-to-one with the survey's named families (no-op and near-zero writes; the weight→activation→attention outlier chain; the constant-bias reading; the anti-overmixing and spectral readings; the developmental account), which means the survey's five-level table is a ready-made organizing scheme for what we have already collected, and its eleven categories a checklist of framings not yet represented. Second, a **null hypothesis we must report against**: because the sink's attention column and the sink's value-side write can dissociate — and because removing a sink is benign in one architecture and fatal in another — every sink statistic we publish should be the pair (attention mass on the destination, magnitude of what the destination writes), with an ablation damage figure attached, or it is a single-axis measurement in a literature the survey itself shows is full of them. Third, a **warning about mitigation-as-evidence**: the survey treats "relax the softmax constraint and the sink disappears" as causal support for the no-op account, which is exactly the move our own entries (runs 705-711) showed can be a relocation rather than a removal — an architectural change that makes the sink unnecessary is compatible with several mechanism stories, so we should treat mitigation success as a necessary condition on any account we adopt, never as sufficient. Also worth carrying: the survey's own numbers are quoted from the papers it cites, not re-measured, and its study count is given as "over 210" in one place, "over 200" in another and "over 180" in the conclusion — a reminder that survey-level counts and magnitudes are second-hand in a way that our entries should never be.
