## What they wanted, and why
The ranking of out-of-distribution detectors is famously unstable: the same scoring rule is competitive on one benchmark and mediocre on the next, and the field's habit of picking one detector by average rank hides that. The authors want to know whether the instability is explainable rather than arbitrary, and they name the explanatory variable: the geometry of the trained model's last-layer representation. Their two-step ambition is first to show that which detector family is competitive can be *read off* measurable geometric properties, and then to show those properties are enough to *predict* a good shortlist for a model the predictor has never seen — with no out-of-distribution data from that model and no knowledge of which kind of distribution shift it will face. The larger goal is detector selection without test-time OOD labels, which is what a deployment actually has to do. Read from full text.

## What they studied
Image classifiers only: VGG-13 trained from scratch and a ViT fine-tuned from pretrained weights (5 seeds per backbone × paradigm), plus two held-out pools used for transfer (a ResNet-18 pool with one seed per cell, 56 checkpoints; 47 pretrained ImageNet-1k classifiers from timm; and 40 linear probes on frozen DINOv2 and CLIP encoders). Source datasets are CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, SuperCIFAR-100 (CIFAR-100 folded into 20 superclasses) and TinyImageNet; OOD sets are iSUN, LSUN-crop, LSUN-resize, SVHN, Places365 and DTD. The unit of analysis is two-sided: **22 confidence scoring functions** (head-side scores from logits and softmax, feature-side scores from penultimate features including Mahalanobis, CTM, fDBD, NNGuide, ViM, NCI) and **eight Neural Collapse metrics on the trained weights and penultimate features**. Read from full text.

## How they did it
1. **Build a factorial benchmark.** Vary backbone, training paradigm (vanilla cross-entropy with a confidence head, label-interpolated, and a gambling-loss head), dropout, reward and source dataset, and score every model with all 22 detectors on every OOD set using two threshold-free ranking metrics, AURC and AUGRC, where only correctly classified in-distribution samples are compared against OOD samples.
2. **Stratify shifts by a measured distance instead of a heuristic.** Embed all ID and OOD sets with CLIP, compute two distribution distances (Fréchet, kernel) and two class-aware angular distances, z-score, and k-means into near / mid / far. Three CLIP encoders agree on the ordering; leaving out any one metric keeps the rank agreement high, and both clustering algorithms give identical groups.
3. **Replace single-winner tables with cliques.** Per cell, run a Friedman test on per-block ranks, Conover pairwise comparisons at 0.05, then Bron–Kerbosch maximal cliques of the indifference graph, so the output is the set of detectors statistically indistinguishable from the best, not a rank list.
4. **Summarise the geometry.** Eight NC metrics: equinormness and equiangularity of class means and of classifier weights, their maximal-equiangularity versions, the within/between covariance ratio of Papyan et al. (reported divided by the number of classes), and the self-duality gap between the classifier weight matrix and the centred class-mean matrix. All are computed **on training data**, on unfiltered features and after two PCA projection filters.
5. **Test association.** A Mantel test on pairwise NC distances against pairwise detector-rank distances, with partial Mantel variants controlling for in-distribution accuracy and then for source identity.
6. **Turn association into prediction.** One class-balanced L2 logistic regression per detector predicts clique membership from the eight z-scored NC metrics. Heads are fitted on the whole VGG-13 pool and applied without retraining to ResNet-18, then to ViTs, to 47 ImageNet models and to 40 self-supervised probes. The evaluation is **set-regret**: the performance of the best detector in the predicted shortlist minus the per-cell oracle, always against a fixed-detector baseline and against a random shortlist of the same size.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **predictive instrument, not a mechanistic account**. The explanation is a mapping from eight weight- and activation-derived scalars to the probability that each of 22 named detectors is competitive, plus a sign-level reading of the logistic coefficients (collapsed and self-dual representations favour prototype-based scores; weak self-duality favours boundary-based and residual-based scores). It is deliberately partial: the authors call the clique structure and the transfer established and the geometric interpretation exploratory, and they state that the associations are not causal. It predicts a set, not a value, and it cannot say by how much a detector will win — only that it would be within the statistically indistinguishable group. Read from full text.

## How they judged success
Friedman/Conover cliques with a Holm-adjusted check at the clique level; Mantel tests with 9,999 permutations; mean set-regret against AUGRC with 95% bootstrap intervals (2,000 resamples, fixed seed); one-sided paired Wilcoxon tests Holm-corrected over 66 (regime, side, baseline) comparisons, plus a model-clustered sign-flip test of the mean difference; and three NC-free baselines — always use the strongest fixed detector, use the best detector on the training pool universally, and draw a random shortlist of the same size. The random shortlist is the decisive control and is run in every transfer pool. Read from full text.

## What they found
The competitive family moves with shift severity and does so differently by architecture: on CNNs the progression is probabilistic scores in-distribution, margin-based scores in near-shift, geometry-aware scores (CTM, fDBD) in mid and far; on fine-tuned ViTs the shift is abrupt and reconstruction- and residual-based scores take the middle and far columns. NC metrics and detector rankings are associated in all four main strata (Mantel between 0.37 and 0.70, p ≤ 0.002), and the association survives an accuracy control in all four, but with a source-identity control it survives in three of four and vanishes for the weakest paradigm. Under VGG-13 → ResNet-18 transfer the NC-only predictor cuts mean regret against the strongest fixed detector by 55%, 75% and 80% in the near, mid and far cells respectively, and wins 65 of 66 comparisons. Under transfer to ImageNet models it still beats the best fixed detector chosen with hindsight by a wide margin — but a random shortlist of the same size reaches almost the same regret, and the predictor's targeting advantage (it contains the oracle detector on 57% of rows against 37% expected by chance) is only significant under the rank test, not under the mean test. On the 40 self-supervised probes it fails at far shift outright. Two further results: projection filtering helps differently by architecture (global PCA subspaces on CNNs, class-predicted subspaces on ViTs), and the within/between covariance ratio for VGG-13 averages around 0.002–0.010 across sources against roughly 0.012–0.037 for the ViT — the ratio is measured and reported, but it is not the metric that carries the prediction. ## Limits and open problems they name
The authors' own limitations section is unusually explicit. The predictor works **only between closely related CNN families**; on ViTs and on ImageNet models a random shortlist of the same size does about as well, and on self-supervised probes it fails at far shift. The association is correlational and its size depends on how the collapse metric is normalised; with an added learning-rate control on the ViT stratum the correlation drops and loses significance. There is one training seed per ResNet-18 cell, so no clique can exist on the transfer pool and every transfer number is regret against a single-run oracle. The eight-metric vector is coarse; the pairwise clique tests are unadjusted (only the clique membership is checked under Holm); hyperparameters were tuned on AUGRC. Scope is small and medium image classification with three paradigms; class imbalance, multi-label training, segmentation and temporal drift are untested. Read from full text.

## What we take from it
- **Method — controlling the control.** The random shortlist of matched size is the only baseline that tells you whether a representation-derived predictor is doing work at all, and here it dismantles most of the ImageNet result. We adopt it: any selector, router or probe-set built from white-box statistics is scored against a random draw of the same cardinality, reported in the same table.
- **Method — cliques instead of winners.** Reporting the statistically indistinguishable set rather than a single best makes an unstable ranking a measurable object. Our evaluation tables should report the set of our own methods that are indistinguishable from the best, not a rank order that reverses under a metric change.
- **Measure — collapse geometry as covariates.** The eight metrics are computable from a checkpoint alone, cheaply and without data labels, which is exactly the shape of a white-box quantity we want: available before any behaviour is observed. The paper also demonstrates the honest use — as *covariates that predict a set*, not as a mechanism.
- **Warning — transfer decay.** A predictor fitted on one architecture family transferred inside that family, degraded to chance on a second family, and collapsed on a third. Any predictor we build gets its transfer boundary mapped, not assumed, and the boundary is reported as part of the claim.
- **Warning — interpretable coefficients, uninterpretable magnitudes.** Two of the logistic heads picked almost no regularisation and their coefficients are an order of magnitude larger than the rest, and the authors say to read the signs, not the magnitudes. A linear readout of a geometry is evidence about direction and not about magnitude, and our own readouts should be reported the same way.
