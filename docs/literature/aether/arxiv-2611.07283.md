# Encoding and Propagation of Grammatical Number in Large Language Models

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.07283
- **Authors / organisation:** Jacob Solinsky, Serguei Pakhomov (University of Minnesota)
- **Date:** 2026-11-05
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07283
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
The paper asks what it would mean for a sparse-autoencoder feature to *mean* something, and answers with a behavioural criterion rather than a descriptive one: a feature earns a semantics only if interventions on it predict a change in the model's output distribution, organised as a causal graph in Pearl's sense. The motivation is the usual one for mechanistic work — hidden state is opaque, so debugging degrades into retraining and prompt-tinkering — but the specific target is linguistic rather than safety-oriented: models are grammatical long before they are reliable, so grammar is a place where a complete circuit could in principle be recovered. The authors position this inside theoretical syntax, arguing that a machine which generates fluent language can be interrogated for a grammar in a way that behavioural observation of humans cannot supply. The study is a single behaviour (subject–verb number agreement) pursued to exhaustion on a small model, then spot-checked on two larger ones.

## What they studied
Pythia-70M as the primary object, with embedding-matrix replication on Gemma-2-2B and Llama-3.1-8B. The unit of analysis is an SAE feature on a named subcomponent: the embedding matrix, the unembedding matrix, each MLP sublayer, each attention sublayer output, and separately the value, key and query vectors of individual heads. Several thousand TopK SAEs and Matryoshka SAEs are trained — 8,192 features for Pythia's 512-dimensional states, 16,384 on Gemma's embeddings, 32,768 on Llama's, and 1,024-feature SAEs on individual query/key vectors — plus one SAE on the step-0 (untrained) checkpoint for control. Stimuli are matched singular/plural sentence pairs in four syntactic templates: a simple frame, a relative clause with a long subject, a relative clause with the target noun inside it, and a prepositional-phrase frame.

## How they did it
The behaviour is turned into a scalar first, because everything downstream filters on it. They define a "singular conjugation preference" over a curated set of 367 verb pairs whose two forms are each a single lowercase leading-space token, and 1,700 noun pairs gathered the same way; the raw per-verb effect is the log ratio of the patched probabilities of the singular and plural forms, and the total score sums that over the vocabulary after attenuating with three penalties — one for changing the pair's *total* probability, one for shifting probability onto non-verbs, and one that downweights verbs the model would never emit for that prompt. Candidate features are then screened by Cliff's delta between the singular and plural activation distributions, with a 0.7 threshold, and by a gradient-based sensitivity prefilter. Edges are established by activation patching: swap the cause feature's value from the singular sentence into the plural sentence and back, and measure what fraction of the effect feature's (or the behaviour score's) singular/plural gap is closed, averaged over the corpus with per-direction "singularisation" and "pluralisation" strengths. Circuits are then drawn by pruning features below the thresholds and grouping features that share more than 60% of their in- and outgoing edges into boxes.

## What counts as an explanation here
A feature dictionary that is deliberately *not* claimed to be self-describing, plus a causal graph over those features terminating in a behaviour. Interpretations are attached to features post hoc by inspecting top-activating tokens, but the paper's evidential weight rests on the graph: an in-edge means the feature's value is caused by an earlier feature, an out-edge means it causes a later one or the measured behaviour. It is explicitly partial — the authors say most features are left out, that some "mystery" features connect to behaviour without appearing in the feature-to-feature graph, and that the graph's meaning is behaviourist: prompts are stimuli, continuations are responses, and the model's language "denotes" nothing in the linguistic sense. It predicts responses to single-feature interventions at single token positions in the templates studied, and the authors are candid that it does not extend to behaviours requiring multi-token changes.

## How they judged success
Variance explained by each SAE on held-out hidden states (80–90% across components, with the final-layer attention value component the stated exception; embedding SAEs 95.84% on Gemma and 94.31% on Llama). Effect sizes from Cliff's delta between singular and plural distributions. Two normalised edge strengths bounded near 0–1, reporting the fraction of a gap closed by patching. Per-verb effect stability across the verb set, used to justify the log-space scoring. Controls: the untrained step-0 checkpoint analysed with the identical pipeline; the SAE-quality check against a theoretical upper bound for a Gaussian-input reconstruction; a random-feature ablation arm for the query/key SAEs; and cross-model replication of the noun-number result.

## What they found
Verb conjugation in Pythia-70M is a short circuit and mostly a suppression story. The most important mechanism in every template is a plural-noun feature acting through one route or another to suppress an unembedding feature that by default selects singular verbs; plural-promoting features exist but matter less than singular suppression. Plural marking on nouns is unified across templates around one feature that has a strong effect size in all four datasets, while singular-marking features turned up in only one template — which the authors read as the grammatical singular being the *unmarked* number for the model, an asymmetry they reproduce in both larger models (all three busy mostly with privative plural marking, a single singular positive feature appearing in one Llama condition). Long-distance agreement splits into a direct one-hop route from subject to final token and a two-hop route in which competing singular/plural attention features drive a shared plural-verb-selection feature; a double-negative chain (plural noun → suppressing feature → suppressing feature → plural verb) recurs across three templates, and the two-hop route carries much of the number-distraction effect because the plural number is sprayed onto the whole embedded clause. The larger models need *fewer* conjugation-relevant number features each, with one dominant feature per model across templates, which the authors take as a sign the method will scale. Plural features turn out to have distinct semantic fields rather than one shared meaning — one indexes animate plurals and occupations, another concrete inanimate objects, another arts/faith/communication vocabulary. Finally, the paper reports that the untrained model's hidden states are reconstructed about as well as the trained model's, which they read as evidence that training does not make states more statistically regular, merely more useful for generation.

## Limits and open problems they name
The query/key SAEs failed: ablating one feature at a time from a query vector reduced the target edge by over half for about the same fraction of features as a random-ablation control (roughly 11% against 12%), so attention edges could not be decomposed symbolically and that line was abandoned. The gradient-based prefilter failed on the larger models through gradient shrinking in early layers, forcing a brute-force sweep. Non-orthogonality of the overcomplete dictionary means many found features are probably redundant, and features they suspect should be one are routinely split into near-identical bundles, complicating every diagram. The authors also flag that features belonging to the unembedding matrix are mis-attributed to whatever layer they were observed in, and that their framework cannot address behaviours that appear only across multiple tokens.

## What we take from it
Three transfers. First, a **behaviour-first filter**: the paper refuses to call a feature "the plural marker" on activation evidence alone and instead ranks candidates by a scalarised behaviour metric that is itself defended penalty-by-penalty — a template for turning a named property into an estimand before wiring anything, which is what our predictive model needs in order to be scored at all. Second, the **two-term effect decomposition** (how much of a gap the patch closes, computed separately when pushing singular→plural and plural→singular) is a cheap habit that caught this paper's own headline asymmetry; a symmetric-looking average would have hidden the markedness result. Third, the **untrained-checkpoint control** on identical inputs, which here produced a genuinely useful negative: reconstruction quality is not evidence of learned structure. Warning to carry: the failed query/key arm is the design lesson that an SAE whose ablation effect does not exceed random features has measured nothing, however good its variance explained.
