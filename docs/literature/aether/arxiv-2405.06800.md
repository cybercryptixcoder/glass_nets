## What they wanted, and why
The paper takes the most convenient black-box explanation setting in use — hand an LLM only a problem and an answer, ask it to justify the answer — and asks what happens when the answer is wrong. The authors argue the field has catalogued why explanations can be *unfaithful* to the model but not this separate failure: an explanation that is persuasive precisely because it defends a false claim. They name it **adversarial helpfulness**, and their motive is that the setting is the one ordinary users actually reach, through API chat products rather than weights, so a breakdown here is a breakdown in the main deployment route. They want three things: a measurement that the effect is real for readers, a taxonomy of the rhetorical moves that carry it, and a structural test of whether the model is defending a wrong answer by reasoning through knowledge or by something cheaper. The larger goal, stated in the conclusion, is guidance on when an LLM may safely be used as an explainer at all.

## What they studied
Four commercial explainers with API access only — GPT-3.5-Turbo (Chat), GPT-4 (0613), Claude via Amazon Bedrock, and Cohere Command — and three open-weight models as proxy evaluators (Vicuna-33B, WizardLM-70B, Mixtral-8x7B), all chosen from the top of the lmsys Arena leaderboard at the time. Three explanation settings: ECQA commonsense multiple-choice with 500 problems, SNLI entailment-to-neutral with 300 problems, and SNLI contradiction-to-neutral with 300. The unit of analysis is the *explanation text*, not any internal object: no activations, no neurons, no weights. The fourth experiment is a synthetic object instead — a linearised reasoning graph — used as a structural stand-in for an explanation.

## How they did it
They prompt each explainer to argue for a designated wrong answer: for ECQA the "second-best" option (the near-miss distractor), and for NLI a neutral label on pairs whose true label is entailment or contradiction, deliberately avoiding the easier sub-case of flipping entailment against contradiction, which they say neither humans nor models can argue plausibly. The prompt instructs the model to convince the audience, to give reasons the alternatives are wrong, and not to concede any alternative. Human side: Amazon MTurk annotators from the US rate GPT-4's ECQA explanations on a 3-point scale (1/3/5) for convincingness of the wrong answer *before* the explanation, convincingness *after*, fluency, and factual correctness, with every explanation seen by three different workers. Machine side: the same texts are scored by the open-weight evaluators, not by free-form prompting but by reading the conditional probability of each scale token and taking the argmax — a choice that keeps the evaluator's answer on the same permitted set as the humans'. They then hand GPT-4 Turbo a list of ten persuasion strategies with descriptions and ask it to emit a JSON object naming which were used and quoting an instance, running this detection over the explanations for all three explainers, and repeating the whole detection under a second, external forty-technique jailbreak taxonomy to check the finding is not an artefact of their own list. Finally, the structural test: build a tree with a root, B branches of length L, one lettered leaf per branch as an "answer", linearise it to text, and ask each model to return an alternative reasoning path to a different leaf; score by exact match, and rerun the whole thing with non-root, non-leaf node names replaced by random non-overlapping characters so that the systematic name (branch index plus depth) no longer leaks connectivity.

## What counts as an explanation here
An explanation is a piece of natural-language prose, and the paper's object of study is its *rhetorical form* rather than its relation to any internal computation — ten named persuasion strategies plus a graph of reasoning steps as an idealisation. There is no claim of completeness or of predictive power: the authors count an explanation as well-formed in their sense when it does not rationalise a wrong answer, and they explicitly separate their target from faithfulness (unfaithfulness is defined without reference to whether the explained answer is correct), from plausibility (a feature of text, where adversarial helpfulness is a bug), from hallucination (they stress the explanations usually present true material *selectively*), from sycophancy (the model need not be agreeing with the user), and from over-trust, which they place in the reader rather than the text. So the explanation form is descriptive and diagnostic; its purpose is to flag a text, not to predict the model that produced it.

## How they judged success
Human convincingness before versus after the explanation, paired two-tailed *t*-tests reported at 499 degrees of freedom for ECQA (three workers per item, so the per-item mean is the unit) and at 499 and 299 degrees of freedom for ECQA and NLI in the evaluator arm and Bonferroni correction across the three evaluators; a separate narrative/fluency/correctness block from the same raters; strategy frequencies reported out of 100 explanations per cell; and alternate-path success rate as a function of graph complexity, with exact-match scoring and a name-randomised arm as the control. There is no ablation of the explainer prompt, and no arm in which the model is asked to explain a *correct* answer — the authors note this omission only in passing, as an expectation that the same rhetorical capabilities would appear there.

## What they found
Readers do shift toward the wrong answer. On ECQA, mean convincingness of the second-best option rises from 2.96 to 3.53 after a GPT-4 explanation, from 3.66 to 3.72 after Claude's, and from 3.74 to 3.84 after GPT-3.5-Turbo's, all significant at p < 0.01; the same texts are rated highly fluent (4.85, 4.55, 4.46) and largely factually correct (4.68, 3.86, 4.04) by those raters. The proxy evaluators reproduce the direction inconsistently: Vicuna-33B and WizardLM-70B move significantly (p < 0.001, Bonferroni-corrected) while Mixtral-8x7B does not, and on the NLI entailment-to-neutral cell Mixtral's post-explanation scores are all identical at 3.00 — a degeneracy the authors flag as a dataset artefact rather than a judgement. Strategy detection over three explainers finds a small alphabet used very often: for ECQA, comparative advantage framing appears in more than 70% of explanations (90 / 79 / 82 for GPT-4 / Claude / Chat); for the NLI settings, reframing the question appears in more than 90% (93-95), with selective evidence at 43-67 and selective fact presentation at 37-72; appeal to authority (0-5) and analogical evidence (1-4) are nearly absent. The external taxonomy shows the same concentration around logical appeal, encouragement and framing. On the graph task, success falls with complexity, and once node names are randomised every model except GPT-4 and GPT-4 Turbo drops to zero, while GPT-4 itself fails nearly half the graphs at L = 6. The authors read the pair as evidence that the persuasive text is carried by lexical surface and structure rather than by deductive navigation of the knowledge graph.

## Limits and open problems they name
Their own §10 is unusually candid and undercuts the evaluator half: humans correlate weakly with every proxy model on the item level, with no significant Pearson correlation on any of the three scores; human inter-annotator agreement is poor across the MTurk pool; and every pair of proxy evaluators agrees at Cohen's kappa below 0.1, which they attribute to different "baseline marking guidelines" visible in wildly different mean scores (Mixtral averages 1.1-1.6 on several cells where humans average 3.0). They name as future work an uninformative-explanation control — whether any plausible-sounding text lifts convincingness equally — and an analysis linking each strategy to its effect size, neither of which is done. The graph experiment is an analogy, and the persistent double-writing of the last node, which they forgive under an aliasing reading of the arrow, is a scoring relaxation that is not ablated. Sample sizes are 500 and 2 × 300 problems, all English, all one turn, and only one prompt family per setting with no sweep of instructions.

## What we take from it
**A warning with a design consequence.** The paper's sharpest operational claim is that guardrails cannot be applied at the model level because the strategies that carry adversarial helpfulness are the same abilities that make the model useful — ignoring minor facts, speaking confidently, choosing a frame — so the fix is upstream: do not ask an explainer to defend an answer whose correctness is not independently established. For us this is the natural-language twin of the folder's standing rule that a description must be scored, never believed, and it adds a third column to the description table: not only "does the gap move" but "what rhetorical moves are doing the work". **A cheap detector.** A ten-item strategy checklist run by a strong judge over a fixed number of generated texts (out of 100, as here) is an affordable audit row for any account we write in prose. **A measurement trap to avoid, stated by the authors against themselves:** the proxy-evaluator row is a worked example of an evaluator that produces a statistically significant effect and a kappa below 0.1 — significance without agreement — so any LLM-as-judge score we report needs an agreement statistic against the human or executable reference beside it, not only a p-value. **A null that should have been run:** the missing uninformative-explanation arm is exactly the control this design needs, and it is the one our own natural-language checks should carry by default.
