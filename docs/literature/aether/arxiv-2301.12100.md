# Reachability Analysis of Neural Network Control Systems (DeepNNC)

- **Key:** arxiv-2301.12100
- **Authors / organisation:** Chi Zhang, Wenjie Ruan (corresponding), Peipei Xu; University of Exeter and University of Liverpool, UK
- **Date:** 2023-01-28
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.12100
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Every tool in the neural-network-control verification family pays for its soundness in a particular currency: sign patterns, Taylor-model coefficients, generators, polytopes. Each such currency is defined only for certain activation functions and certain plant classes, and each accumulates error across control steps - the wrapping effect - so a reachable set that is tight at step one is useless by step ten. The authors want an analyser that escapes both limits at once: it should accept any plant and any activation the user happens to have, and it should not need to know the network's structure at all. Their answer is to stop bounding the network and start optimising a scalar function of the initial state. The larger goal is a verification tool that a control engineer can point at a black-box simulator, which is exactly the access class our own white-box account has to be benchmarked against.

## What they studied
Closed-loop systems of the form `x_dot = f(x,u)` with `u = sigma(h(x))`, where `sigma` is a k-layer feed-forward controller with sigmoid, tanh, ReLU, or mixed activations, and `f` is any Lipschitz-continuous plant, linear or not. The unit of analysis is the **whole closed-loop map** `phi(x0, t)` treated as one function of the initial state; no neuron, layer, head, or region inside the network is ever inspected. Six benchmarks plus an ARCH-COMP21 12-state aircraft case study.

## How they did it
Two halves. First, a proof that the closed loop is Lipschitz in its initial state, obtained by unrolling: within one control step the error between two trajectories is bounded by adding and subtracting `f(x, u_y)`, which yields an inequality with a linear growth term from the controller's Lipschitz constant and an integral term from the plant's; Grönwall's inequality then bounds that integral by an exponential in elapsed time. Repeating per step gives a global constant `K`, and the same argument restricted to a sub-interval gives a local constant `k_i`. Second, the reachability problem is rewritten as a box-constrained global minimisation: the lower end of the reachable interval at time `t` is `min` of `phi(x0,t)` over the initial box, the upper end a maximisation. They solve it by Lipschitzian optimisation: evaluate at interval endpoints, attach to each sub-interval a "characteristic value" computed from the endpoint outputs and a Lipschitz estimate, then repeatedly bisect the sub-interval with the smallest characteristic value and place the new point at the characteristic point rather than the midpoint. Multi-dimensional problems are handled by nested one-dimensional optimisation, recursing dimension by dimension. Because a black-box system gives no analytic constant, they estimate the Lipschitz constant from the sampled slopes themselves: a global variant that multiplies the largest observed secant by a safety factor above one, and a local variant that mixes the three neighbouring secants with a scaled global term, so small intervals use local information and large ones fall back to global.

## What counts as an explanation here
An **interval-valued enclosure produced by a converging optimiser**, not a description and not a certificate in the symbolic sense. The output is a box `S_t` per time point, plus two parameters that are themselves the accuracy statement: the tolerance epsilon at which optimisation stops and the iteration cap `k_max`. Two theorems state the epistemics: the returned bound is always an enclosure (soundness holds at any iteration, because the characteristic value is by construction a lower bound on the true minimum and the optimiser is only ever interrupted, never wrong), while completeness holds only in the limit where the tolerance goes to zero. So the object is offered as a partial explanation with an explicit dial, and it predicts exact reachable-interval endpoints up to a stated error - a predictive claim of the narrowest kind, about one scalar per time point.

## How they judged success
(a) Runtime against three named tools (ReachNN*, Sherlock, Verisig 2.0) on six benchmarks times four activation regimes, with blanks where a tool does not apply or times out. (b) Tightness as the **area of the polygon** formed by the estimated reachable set at a predefined time point, compared against the same area for the baselines and reported as a percentage reduction relative to each baseline; the reference for accuracy is a grid-based exhaustive search over 1000 initial points, wrapped in a convex hull, standing in for ground truth. (c) Two ablation sweeps: `k_max` in {3, 5, 10, 10^4} at fixed infinitesimal tolerance, and epsilon in {0.5, 0.05, 0.005, 0.0005} at fixed large `k_max`, both on one benchmark at eight time points, with the resulting boxes shown as figures rather than as areas. (d) A qualitative overlay check that simulated trajectories fall inside the estimated sets.

## What they found
The runtime table is theirs almost everywhere: sub-second to a few seconds across all six benchmarks and all four activation regimes, against tens to thousands of seconds for ReachNN* (one Sigmoid cell at 13,350 s), tens to 143 s for Sherlock, and tens to 190 s for Verisig 2.0, with many cells blank because those tools cannot handle that activation. The authors summarise this as averages of 768x, 37x and 56x. Tightness goes the same way but with much smaller margins: on the five benchmark/activation rows where a baseline area exists, DeepNNC's polygon area is larger than the exhaustive-search hull (the optimistic reference) yet 4.5% to 96% smaller than the loosest baseline, and notably only 4.8%-7.6% smaller than Verisig 2.0 on the three rows where that tool applies - the same tool that costs tens of seconds. The aircraft case study is a verdict rather than a measurement: over a narrowed initial subset and a 2-second horizon, two state variables leave the safe range, so the system is declared unsafe.

## Limits and open problems they name
Efficiency depends on two things they name openly - the quality of the Lipschitz estimate and the input dimension - and they concede that high-dimensional reachability is the open case, proposing a space-filling-curve transformation to collapse it into one dimension as future work. Not named but visible in the tables: the method's advantage over Verisig 2.0 is a factor of 4-8 on tightness at a factor of 30-60 on speed, so the tightness gain over a Taylor-model tool that also supports sigmoid and tanh is real but marginal; the correctness of the enclosures rests on a Lipschitz estimate that is a *sampled* secant multiplied by a heuristic factor above one, so the constant is only an upper bound in the limit of infinitely fine sampling; and the exhaustive-search reference is itself a lower bound on the true set, which the authors do not flag when they compute the percentage improvements against it.

## What we take from it
Three things. First, a **method**: Lipschitzian optimisation is a way to invert a black-box function's range at a chosen granularity with an anytime guarantee, and the characteristic-value/bisect step is cheap enough that the whole machinery never touches the network's internals - so it is the right baseline to run our white-box account against, since it holds zero structural information. Second, a **warning** about what that costs: because the constant `K` is estimated from secants on the sample set rather than read off the weights, every query enlarges `K`, and the enclosure widens with the sampled spread rather than with the network's actual gain - which is precisely the deficiency a white-box Lipschitz bound over the weight norms (the surviving thread's `prod ||W||` object) removes. Third, a **measure**: tightness reported as an area ratio against an exhaustive-search hull is a clean and cheap tightness metric, but the hull is a lower approximation, so the ratio is optimistic for the method that produced it and must be labelled. Recurring pattern worth recording: this is the last member of the closed-loop family in our thread, and it is the first one where the looseness number is printed beside the runtime on the same rows - the sequence of runs 626-646 arguing that the field omits it ends with two papers (this and the review tutorial) that do print it, both because their claims are trades rather than guarantees.
