# Dimensional Collapse in Transformer Attention Outputs: A Challenge for Sparse Dictionary Learning

- **Key:** arxiv-2508.16929
- **Authors / organisation:** Junxuan Wang, Xuyang Ge, Wentao Shu, Zhengfu He, Xipeng Qiu; Shanghai Innovation Institute and OpenMOSS Team, School of Computer Science, Fudan University
- **Date:** 2026-02-11 (v1 2025-08-23, v4 2026-02-11)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.16929
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (abstract, SS1-SS7, references, Appendices A-O with Tables 1-10). - **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

Sparse dictionary learning, and SAEs in particular, is the field's main route from activations to named features, but it has a persistent waste problem: at large dictionary sizes a large share of features never fire at all. Existing fixes treat this as an optimisation or regularisation issue - auxiliary reconstruction losses, resampling, momentum handling. The authors want to know whether the waste has a geometric cause instead: whether the activation space that the dictionary is being fit to is itself degenerate, so that a randomly initialised dictionary spends most of its capacity pointing at directions the data never occupies. If that is right, then no amount of loss-shaping fixes it, but the initialisation can. This is a step toward dictionaries whose feature count is an honest count of the model's representational capacity, which is the precondition for any claim that a dictionary gives us a complete inventory of what a network computes.

## What they studied

Models are GPT-2, Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen3-8B (plus Qwen3-4B, pythia-160m and pythia-2.8b in an appendix), on SlimPajama, its GitHub and arXiv subsets, and the Chinese CCI3-Data corpus. The unit of analysis is first the **whole activation matrix** at one site - its singular value spectrum and its effective rank - and then the **dictionary feature** (a decoder column, dead if it never activates across 10 million tokens). Three activation sites are compared throughout: the attention output (the concatenated head outputs Z passed through the output projection W_O), the MLP output, and the post-block residual stream. One SAE family is used for the main results (TopK, following Gao et al.), with JumpReLU SAEs, sparse replacement models (Lorsa) and an auxiliary-loss baseline (AuxK) as extensions.

## How they did it

Step one is a measurement pass with no training: collect 10 million activation vectors per site, mean-centre, take the SVD, and compute **effective rank** as the exponential of the Shannon entropy of the normalised singular value distribution, reported as a fraction of the ambient dimension. They corroborate the spectrum with two behavioural checks - the share of singular values above 1% of the maximum, and a loss-recovery curve in which activations are zero-ablated and then progressively restored through their top singular components, with recovered loss measured against the ablated baseline. Step two asks where the low rank comes from by decomposing the variance along each unit direction into a head-output term and an output-projection-norm term, so the spectrum of O can be compared against the spectra of Z and of W_O. Step three correlates: take the open LlamaScope dictionaries trained on each Llama-3.1-8B site and scatter each site's dead-feature proportion against that site's effective rank. Step four is a factorial robustness check - SAEs trained from scratch at three dictionary sizes and three sparsity levels, twelve cells, all reporting dead-feature share. Step five is the intervention: **Active Subspace Initialization (ASI)** takes the top d_init right singular vectors of the demeaned activations, randomly initialises the decoder, projects the decoder columns onto that subspace (encoder set to its transpose, both biases zero), and then trains normally for 800 million tokens, batch 4096, k = 50, 32768 features, d_init = 768 by default, against TopK and AuxK baselines at matched hyperparameters, three seeds per cell and 15 seeds for a significance run. Step six scales the feature count from 16K to 1M, adds SparseAdam (which updates momentum only for features that fired), and ports the same initialisation onto Lorsa heads, where each replacement head is seeded from the principal directions of the MHSA head it is meant to reproduce.

## What counts as an explanation here

The explanation is geometric and predictive rather than semantic: no feature is named and no circuit is drawn. What they offer is a **property of the space a dictionary operates in** - a spectrum, an effective rank, a subspace - plus a claim that this property predicts a dictionary-level failure mode (dead features) and a construction that removes it (initialise inside the active subspace). It is partial by construction: their own control on feature quality (a blinded 100-feature monosemanticity rating, and cosine alignment of features with the "dead subspace") is designed to show the intervention does not damage the dictionary's semantics, not that it improves them. The causal link between low rank and dead features is explicitly left open in the discussion - what is established is a cross-site correlation plus a successful intervention, not a mechanism.

## How they judged success

Five measures, all against matched baselines: effective rank (fraction of dimension) and the share of singular values above 1%; fraction of downstream loss recovered under progressive singular-component restoration with zero ablation as baseline; dead-feature count and proportion, with dead defined as never active over 10 million tokens; normalised reconstruction MSE; and delta LM loss (language-model loss with the SAE inserted minus the original, over 1 million tokens). Controls and ablations: random-subspace initialisation at the same d_init (reported as degrading every metric), a sweep of d_init from small to the full dimension, application to a near-full-rank site (the post-layer-15 residual) where gains are expected to vanish, a 15-seed Welch one-tailed t-test for each baseline-by-metric comparison, cross-layer and cross-model replication (Llama-3.1-8B layers 7 and 23; Qwen3-8B layers 8 and 26 on fineweb-edu), and a blinded monosemanticity rating on 100 sampled features per dictionary.

## What they found

**The low-rank claim.** Attention output has an effective rank around 60% of the ambient dimension, against about 90% for MLP outputs and the residual stream. In Llama-3.1-8B layer 15 the numbers are 2506 for attention output, 3611 for the residual stream, with the appendix printing 2351 (layer 7 attention), 2654 (layer 23 attention), 515 for GPT-2's middle layer attention output and 434 for pythia-160m's. The spectrum check is stark: 74.7% of the singular values exceed 1% of the maximum in attention output, against 100.0% for both MLP output and residual stream. Behaviourally, attention output needs 39.1% of dimensions to recover over 99% of the ablated downstream loss, against 95.3% for MLP output and 96.9% for the residual stream. A five-way repetition of the SVD on different 10-million-token draws puts the coefficient of variation of the effective rank at 1.5e-4 and the largest per-singular-value CV at 4.9e-3, so the spectrum is stable at this sample size. The projection decomposition locates the tail-thinning in W_O rather than in the head outputs: the O spectrum tracks Z for the leading components and falls away underneath them, consistent with the projection compressing the sum of overlapping head subspaces.

**The correlation.** Across Llama-3.1-8B's layers, the sites with the lowest effective rank have the most dead features, and that is every attention-output site plus the last two MLP-output sites. The from-scratch factorial shows the same gap and widens with everything: at L0 = 64, layer-7 attention runs 66.97%, 75.65%, 82.96% dead across dictionary sizes 16384, 32768 and 65536 while layer-7 residual runs 0.02%, 0.06%, 0.15%; at L0 = 32 layer-15 attention is 68.70%, 79.86%, 87.22% against 27.01%, 45.70%, 61.33% for the residual. Dead share rises with dictionary size and falls with L0 in every attention row, but the layer-15 residual moves the same way with much larger amplitude - 27.01% to 61.33% at L0 = 32 - so attention output is not the only site with a size problem, only the site where it starts worst.

**The intervention.** At L0 = 50, ASI at d_init = 768 goes from 16144.33 dead features (base) and 54.67 (AuxK) to 4.00, with normalised MSE 0.31680 against 0.33367 and 0.32241, and delta LM loss 7.847e-3 against 8.375e-3 and 7.958e-3, three seeds. The 15-seed Welch tests are all in the expected direction, the weakest p-value 1.27e-6 (delta LM loss versus AuxK) and the strongest 2.99e-40 (MSE versus TopK). Scaling 16K to 1M features, ASI holds the best reconstruction at every size; combined with SparseAdam it reaches the paper's headline of dead features falling from 87% to below 1% at 1M features. Two honest negatives are printed as negatives: at the largest scale ASI alone has **more** dead features than AuxK, and the paper says so while showing that the features AuxK revives contribute little reconstruction; and the layer-7 replication shows ASI at 5308 dead features against AuxK's 98, which they attribute to the smaller effective rank of that layer against a fixed d_init. Blinded rating of 100 features found no significant monosemanticity difference, and alive features under ASI are no more aligned with the dead subspace than under TopK (counts in the lowest cosine bucket 24576 versus 10176, but none at 0.15 or above in either), with dead-subspace reconstruction error essentially unchanged (0.00334 against 0.00350). On Lorsa and on JumpReLU SAEs with a consistently low L0 the initialisation transfers; on SAEs whose L0 starts high and is annealed down it fails, and TopK with K-anneal fails too, which the authors trace to feature directions drifting out of the active subspace while training sits in the high-L0 regime.

## Limits and open problems they name

Causality is unresolved: the correlation between low rank and dead features could come from optimisation dynamics or feature competition and they say so explicitly. ASI is a partial fix at extreme scale, where dead features remain without SparseAdam; it needs the site to be genuinely low-rank to help at all (minimal gains on the residual stream), it is sensitive to the training schedule (fails under L0 annealing), and it requires a fixed d_init per site, which the layer-7 result shows is mismatched when layers have different effective ranks. Feature quality is only checked for non-degradation. Not addressed: whether the low rank of attention output costs the model expressivity it could recover (raised only as a suggestion for future architectures), and whether a dictionary trained on attention output is therefore systematically impoverished relative to one trained on the residual stream.

## What we take from it

Three things, one methodological and two warnings. **Method:** the loss-recovery curve under progressive singular-component restoration with zero ablation as the baseline is the cheapest honest test of whether a low rank matters *behaviourally* rather than merely spectrally, and it is the number to quote instead of a single effective-rank scalar - their own comparison shows a site can be 60% of dimension on the spectrum but only need 39.1% of dimensions to recover over 99% of loss, and the difference between those two readings is where the collapse and the behaviour stop coinciding. **Warning one:** effective rank is a spectrum statistic with no units, and the option to report it as a fraction of dimension, as an absolute count, or as a recovery threshold is exactly the freedom that makes subspace claims unfalsifiable; the 2506 and the 3611 and the 39.1% are three different quantities and no two of them rank the sites the same way across all appendix rows (Qwen3-8B on SlimPajamaGitHub prints 2410 attention output against 2000 for the residual stream - attention is *higher*). Mechanical rule for us: any claim that a site is low-dimensional cites the recovery threshold and the dimension count, not the fraction, and the fraction is reported only beside the appendix rows that contradict it when they do. **Warning two, the one I would put in the paper:** fixing the instrument's geometry is not the same as fixing the instrument's coverage, and this paper's own dead-subspace analysis proves it - ASI takes the alive count from 10176 to 24576 in the lowest cosine bucket while no feature in either dictionary sits above 0.15 cosine with the dead subspace, and dead-subspace reconstruction error barely moves (0.00350 to 0.00334). So the recovered features are not entering the subspace that random initialisation ignored; they are mostly re-tiling the region already covered, which is consistent with the authors' two candidate explanations and with the fact that AuxK's extra alive features add nothing to reconstruction. For us the corollary is direct: **a higher alive-feature count is not evidence of broader coverage, and every dictionary we build reports the alive count beside the reconstruction error measured separately inside abandoned subspaces, because those two numbers moved in opposite directions here.**
