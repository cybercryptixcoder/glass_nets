## What they wanted, and why
Circuit discovery methods in the field are built to answer "which subgraph produced this prediction", and their output is a subgraph of the *computation graph for a particular input*. The authors are coming from the XAI side, where attribution has been used for years to prune vision models, and they notice that the same machinery, pointed at a different reference set, answers three practical questions at once: which parameters can be deleted without hurting general performance; which parameters carry a named task like indirect object identification; and which parameters carry an undesired behaviour like toxicity or degenerate repetition. The larger goal is a post-hoc tool that both shrinks a model and edits it, with no fine-tuning, on the grounds that retraining is unaffordable for the practitioner who just wants the toxic path removed.

## What they studied
Small models throughout: OPT-125M for all circuit and correction work, and TinyLlama, Llama-2-7B and Llama-3-8B for compression. The unit of analysis is a **linear-layer parameter**: either a whole neuron (a row of a weight matrix, for structured pruning), a whole attention head, or an individual weight element. Relevance scores come from Layer-wise Relevance Propagation, with AttnLRP used to decompose attention-head contributions through their softmax activations. Data: C4 for general-purpose reference samples (three sets of 128 sequences at 2048 tokens, different seeds), IOI-like sequences generated following ACDC's protocol (six reference sets of 128), RealToxicityPrompts for toxicity (93 prompts selected for highly toxic generations), and 53 repetition-triggering prompts plus C4 prompts as the benign contrast. Evaluation on WikiText2 perplexity and the lm-evaluation-harness zero-shot suite.

## How they did it
Four steps, and the whole framework is the first three plus a subtraction. (1) Run LRP on a set of reference samples, aggregate per-component relevance across samples and normalise, giving one score per neuron, head or weight element. (2) Rank components by score and prune the lowest-ranked by masking, at whatever granularity the application needs - globally structured, row-wise unstructured, or globally unstructured. (3) For compression, use a diverse general corpus as references and prune 50% row-wise, comparing against magnitude pruning and against Wanda (weight times activation, forward pass only) at the same sparsity. (4) For circuit discovery, use task-specific references (IOI) and compare LRP against Wanda, raw gradients and neuron activation as attribution sources, at both structured (neurons/heads) and unstructured (individual weights) granularity, under row-wise and global ranking; a circuit is judged good if the performance-sparsity curve stays flat until the task-critical components are reached and drops sharply once they are pruned. Model correction is the differential form: compute relevance on the harmful reference set and on the general set, take the difference, sort ascending, and prune components that are most relevant to the bad behaviour and least relevant to everything else. Nothing is fine-tuned at any stage, and every setting is run across seeds with mean and standard deviation or standard error reported.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **parameter-level subgraph**: a set of weights, neurons or heads that has been *deleted from the model*, sorted by attribution. It is explicitly input-independent by design - the authors argue that token-level computational subgraphs are example-bound and therefore hard to interpret - so the object is a claim about which parameters a behaviour depends on across a distribution, not about how a particular prediction was computed. It is mechanistic in the surgical sense (remove it and behaviour changes) but not in the descriptive sense: the paper never says what a retained component computes, only that relevance concentrates there. It predicts a curve (accuracy as a function of sparsity) rather than a mechanism, and the strongest predictive content is the ordering itself.

## How they judged success
Compression: perplexity on WikiText2 and mean zero-shot accuracy over seven tasks (BQ, RTE, HS, WG, ARC-e, ARC-c, OBQA), at 50% row-wise sparsity, with magnitude and Wanda as baselines and standard error reported (Table 1, Table 2). Circuit discovery: task accuracy along the pruning sweep - performance-sparsity curves, framed explicitly by analogy to the feature-perturbation paradigm for evaluating attribution, where a flat or rising curve means redundancy and a cliff means an essential component was hit - across six seeds. Correction: toxicity scored by the Perspective API, measured as the mean over responses to 93 prompts, and repetition quantified with a Response Uniqueness Ratio (distinct tokens over generated length, Eq. 11) on 53 prompts, each paired with WikiText2 perplexity as the "did general capability survive" gate. Contrastive attribution is itself the control: the differential set is what carries the claim that the pruned components are behaviour-specific rather than generally load-bearing.

## What they found
On compression the method loses narrowly to Wanda: at 50% sparsity, WikiText2 perplexity goes 11.85 (LRP) against 11.52 (Wanda) and 24.42 (magnitude) on TinyLlama, 7.14 against 6.94 on Llama-2-7B, and 9.82 against 9.86 on Llama-3-8B; mean zero-shot accuracy is 44.71 / 45.46 / 55.18 for LRP against 45.46 / 55.92 / 57.07 for Wanda on the three models. The gap closes with reference-set size - at 8,192 reference samples LRP reaches 11.35 perplexity, beating its own 128-sample result and every other method's - while Wanda's implementation could not exceed 1,024 samples for memory reasons, so the comparison is between a method at its sampled optimum and one beyond its competitor's feasible range. On circuit discovery, LRP extracts noticeably sparser parameter-level IOI circuits in OPT-125M than gradients or raw neuron activation, and sparser than Wanda under each method's own best configuration; but one result is easy to miss and is the most interesting: structured pruning of *attention heads* fails for every attribution method on IOI, which the authors read as the heads of OPT-125M all contributing together rather than any small subset being dispensable. On correction, pruning 100 neurons from fc1 (about 0.3% of them) lowers Perspective toxicity while WikiText2 perplexity is unchanged in the best configuration, and pruning roughly 7,000 weight elements from fc1 (about 0.03% of weights) raises response uniqueness on the repetition prompts; the per-sample scatter plots show the effect is an average shift with samples moving in both directions.

## Limits and open problems they name
Four, all theirs: row-wise unstructured pruning was inherited from Wanda and may not be the right sparsity geometry; the choice of granularity (structured vs unstructured, which layer type) changes results substantially and they do not know how to choose it in advance; the whole method stands on the quality of the reference sets, and they acknowledge that reliable correction needs references that isolate the behaviour without overlapping general capability, with no principled recipe offered; and they note the dual-use risk that the same procedure could be redirected to *amplify* an undesired behaviour. The engineering limits they report in the resources appendix are blunt: LRP costs a forward and a backward pass per reference sample (about 5 seconds per 2048-token sample on TinyLlama, against 1 second for Wanda) and needs about 60 GB of VRAM for a single sequence, so the method that wins on sparsity is the one that cannot run at the reference-set sizes where it wins.

## What we take from it
- **A warning about the baseline arms, and the reason this entry's compression numbers should not be read as a ranking: the method's best result is measured at a reference-set size the competitor could not run.** 8,192 samples for LRP against a 1,024-sample ceiling imposed by Wanda's implementation for memory is a capabilities comparison dressed as a quality comparison. The standing rule this buys us: when we compare a reading method against a cheaper one, the two arms get the *same* sample budget, and any result at a budget the cheap arm cannot reach is reported as an extrapolation.
- **A framing to borrow, precisely because it is a falsifiable curve rather than a score: judge a circuit by the shape of accuracy versus sparsity, where a flat stretch means redundancy and a cliff means the pruning just crossed something essential.** This is the folder's first circuit-quality criterion that needs no ground-truth circuit - it reads the object by how it breaks. It is weaker than faithfulness or sufficiency (a circuit that is redundant and a circuit that is wrong both look flat) but it costs one sweep and no reference labels, which makes it the right first screen before an expensive causal test.
- **A prediction we should test rather than assume: when a behaviour is distributed, structured pruning of the responsible component type fails for *every* attribution method - here all methods fail on attention heads for IOI, while the same methods succeed on MLP neurons.** That is the honest diagnostic value of a null: it is not evidence that attributions are bad, it is evidence that the unit is wrong. If our white-box model returns a mirage on one unit type and a clean circuit on another, the unit is the finding.
- **A reusable technique with a clean interpretation: differential attribution.** Score every component twice, once on references that elicit the behaviour and once on a general corpus, subtract, and prune the most negative difference - i.e. components the bad behaviour needs and everything else does not. This is the same double-difference shape as the folder's contrastive and counterfactual criteria, done at the parameter level and requiring no patching at all, and it is the only correction method in this folder that produces an edited model rather than an analysis of one.
- **A warning about what "0.3% of neurons" is not:** the number counts neurons inside the fc1 layers only, on a 125M-parameter model, with no comparison against a size-matched random or activation-ranked set reported in the body (Wanda and gradient serve as alternative *rankings*, not as a matched null). So the claim "toxicity is localized to a tiny set" is supported against other rankings but not against chance. Given run 764's and 760's matched-control rule, treat the correction result as a ranking comparison, not yet a localisation claim.
- **A shortcut and its price for our own tooling:** relevance from a single forward-backward pass is the cheapest circuit signal this folder has seen, and Wanda shows a forward pass alone suffices for compression. But the same appendix that reports the cost also shows LRP's attribution distributions are unstable with few reference samples while Wanda's are stable with three - meaning the cheap attribution is the stable one and the sharp attribution is the sample-hungry one, and a pipeline that reports only the sharp one is reporting its variance to the reader as signal.
