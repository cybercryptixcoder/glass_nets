## What they wanted, and why

Abstract interpretation with the interval domain is the workhorse of neural-network certification: you box a set of inputs, push the box through the layers, and if the output box misses the decision boundary you have a proof. Two runs earlier the folder spent an entry on an instrument built by stacking several abstract domains; this paper is the other side of that question — what the *simplest* domain can and cannot do, in principle, independent of any implementation. They ask two things. First, is the interval domain powerful enough that, for any continuous function we might want, some network exists whose interval semantics closely tracks that function's true set semantics? Second, and more important for practice, can such a network be built efficiently? The answer to the first is yes and to the second is no, and the paper is the formal statement of the gap between them: existence is cheap to prove, construction is provably intractable.

## What they studied

Networks defined by a small grammar — constants, input variables, sums, scalar multiplication, and one activation function applied pointwise — which covers feed-forward and convolutional architectures but not recurrent ones. The unit of analysis is the *whole network as a function* together with the interval abstract interpretation of that function; no neurons, features or directions are examined. The activation class is the paper's own abstraction: a function is "squashable" if it has finite left and right limits and is monotone, or if such a function can be built from copies of it using the grammar. This class contains sigmoid, tanh, softsign, ReLU, ELU, softplus and smooth ReLU — for the four unbounded ones they exhibit a uniform construction (roughly, feed the negated input back through the same unit, subtract from one, feed that through the unit again) that produces a bounded monotone function. The target of approximation is any continuous function on a compact box, and the quantity of interest is its collecting semantics: the set of outputs obtained as the input ranges over a set.

## How they did it

Three parts. (1) **Definition of the goal.** A network δ-interval approximates a function when, for *every* input box, the abstractly computed output interval sits within δ of the tightest interval around the true output set. Pointwise approximation is the special case where the box is a single point, so the new notion is strictly stronger, and the paper says so explicitly. (2) **A constructive existence proof.** The input domain is gridded at spacing ε; for each grid cell they build an indicator unit by subtracting two dilated copies of the activation shifted by half a cell, so the unit reads roughly one inside the cell and roughly zero outside a one-cell neighbourhood of it. Because the activation only approaches its limits asymptotically, the dilation factor is chosen from the limit definition so that the transition from ≈0 to ≈1 happens inside half a cell, and the residual imprecision θ is tracked symbolically through every step. An m-dimensional cell indicator is the sum of the per-dimension indicators, shifted so that any single dimension outside the neighbourhood forces the sum negative, then passed through the activation once more as a step. The target function is sliced into horizontal bands of height δ/3 (finer than the δ/2 their predecessor used, to absorb the extra imprecision from arbitrary activations), each band is approximated by a sum of cell indicators, and the bands are summed and rescaled. The whole proof ends in explicit bounds: the interval computed for a box lies inside the δ-inflated true interval and contains its δ-deflated core. (3) **A complexity analysis.** They reduce range approximation to SAT-style hardness: a 3CNF instance is compiled into a network whose maximum output exceeds ½+δ exactly when the instance is satisfiable and stays below ½−δ when it is not, using three hand-built activation-shaped gadgets that simulate literal, clause and conjunction; a mirror construction over 3DNF handles the coNP side. Together with a two-alternating-quantifier characterisation of deciding a range, this pins the problem between NP∪coNP and Δ₂.

## What counts as an explanation here

Not an account of a particular network's behaviour — this is a theory paper, and what it delivers is a *theorem of the possible*: for every continuous function and every tolerance, some network exists whose abstract interpretation is that close to the function's set semantics, with any squashable activation. It is an existence claim, not a construction anyone can run, and the paper is careful that the proof is exponential in the size of the domain. The second half delivers the complementary object: a classification of the construction problem itself, plus a dichotomy — with tolerance above one half the problem is trivial (return the middle of the output range), below one half it is hard.

## How they judged success

For the approximation half, success is a proven inequality for all boxes, not a measured error: the deliverable is a bound of the form (true inner interval) contained in (network's abstract interval) contained in (true outer interval), with every constant in the bound (grid spacing, dilation, the θ slack, the slice height) derived from the limit definitions rather than fitted. For the hardness half, success is a reduction with a stated gap — satisfiable instances above the threshold, unsatisfiable below — and a containment of the decision problem inside a complexity class. There is **no experiment, no benchmark, no dataset, no baseline and no code** anywhere in the paper; every number is a constant in a proof.

## What they found

The interval universal approximation theorem holds: any continuous function on a compact domain, to any tolerance, has a network using any squashable activation whose interval abstraction is that close to the function's set semantics — the earlier ReLU-only result generalised to essentially every activation in use. On cost, a single cell indicator uses 2m+1 activation units at depth 2 and width 2m; restricted to ReLU it takes 4m+2 neurons at depth 4, against 10m−3 at depth 3+log₂m in the predecessor's construction — a linear-factor saving in units, stated as a side benefit. The complexity result is the sharper one: range approximation of a network mapping the unit cube to [0,1] is trivial for tolerance above ½ (pick the midpoint) and, for tolerance below ½, is both NP-hard and coNP-hard, and Δ₂-intermediate when the network is polynomial-time executable with finite input precision. Because interval approximation of a function implies range approximation of a network, and because exact-range decision and range approximation land in the same complexity class, **no efficient construction of an interval universal approximator exists** — and, with a short corollary, falsifying the correctness of a network with any squashable activation is NP-hard, recovering the known ReLU result as the special case where the activation happens to be ReLU. They also show that having a good pointwise approximator of a function does not hand you an interval approximator of it, so the theorem is not a corollary of classical universal approximation.

## Limits and open problems they name

The construction is exponential in the size of the domain, and the hardness result says this is not a defect of their particular proof: no efficient construction exists. The hardness statements are conditional in the usual way — "strictly harder than NP-complete" is asserted under the assumption that coNP is not contained in NP. The Δ₂ upper bound needs two extra hypotheses (polynomial-time executability in the number of inputs, and finite input precision) which the hardness claims do not need. The multi-output and multi-class extensions are relegated to an appendix and amount to running the scalar theorem once per output, then post-processing the classifier so that non-predicted classes are zeroed, *because the interval domain is non-relational and cannot represent relations between classes* — their own words for the domain's central weakness. Nothing in the paper is empirical, so nothing is said about whether the constructed networks would train, generalise, or arise from ordinary training at all.

## What we take from it

Three things, in decreasing order of immediate use. First, **a hard ceiling on any account built out of non-relational intervals**: the paper proves the interval instrument can in principle track any continuous function arbitrarily well, and proves that you cannot find the network that does so efficiently — so when our own account reports interval-style bounds on a black box, the ceiling on the report is not precision in principle but the cost of the search, and a bound that is tight enough to be interesting is exactly the regime the dichotomy marks as hard. Second, **the honest shape of an impossibility claim**: they do not say "intervals are bad", they say the approximation exists and its construction is Δ₂-intermediate; the two halves are proved separately so the negative result cannot be read as a failed positive one, and we should split our own negative results the same way. Third, a **method to borrow**: the "squashable" move — reduce a heterogeneous class of objects (here every activation function) to a canonical form (a bounded monotone function) via a uniform construction — is how the paper gets one theorem for the whole zoo; the equivalent for us would be a single canonical form for our account's components, so that one guarantee covers probes, directions and circuits instead of one guarantee each. Also worth keeping: the proof's own structure, where almost all of the error is localised to the two slice bands straddling the function's minimum and maximum and every other band is shown near-exact, is a template for saying *precisely where in a computation an approximation loses its precision* rather than reporting an aggregate error.
