# Decomposing how prompting steers behavior

- **Key:** arxiv-2606.03093
- **Authors / organisation:** Fan L. Cheng, Nikolaus Kriegeskorte; Columbia University
- **Date:** 2026-06-02
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.03093
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Changing an instruction changes what a model does, with no weight update, and mechanistic work usually describes that change with a scalar (how much the activations moved, how manifold capacity shifted, how a probe did) or with an engineered operator whose form was assumed rather than estimated. The authors want the missing middle: an estimate of *which mathematical class of map* a prompt change actually applies to the representation of the content that follows it. Their framing is that prompts act on the geometry of the stimulus set, not on the stimulus individually, so the object to fit is a stimulus-invariant transformation between two clouds of hidden states indexed by the same stimuli. The larger goal is to explain why add-a-vector and affine steering methods work at all — the hypothesis being that they succeed because prompting itself is close to such a map. Rung goes further: the same hierarchy is used as an instrument, not just as a description, when the fitted map is applied as a single-layer causal intervention and scored on whether behaviour switches.

## What they studied
Six open-weight models: the LLMs OPT-2.7B, Llama-3-8B-Instruct and Qwen3-8B (32, 32 and 36 blocks), and the VLMs BLIP-2, LLaVA-OneVision-7B and Qwen3-VL-8B (32, 28 and 36). Six stimulus sets with a two-attribute factorial design: text — EmotionalStory (1,920), WritingStyle (1,440), Number (1,247); images — EmoSet (1,600), StyleTransfer (1,920), COCO (1,000 images chosen for multi-supercategory coverage). Prompts are crossed into six pair groups: cross-attribute open-to-open (G1) and specific-to-specific (G2), within-attribute open-to-specific (G3) and specific-to-specific (G4), and two task-irrelevant source groups (G5, G6). The unit of analysis is a **population of hidden states** — the residual stream at the last input-prompt token, taken at every block — and the object fitted is a map between two such populations.

## How they did it
For each model, layer, dataset and prompt pair they build two matrices of hidden states over the same N stimuli, one under prompt A and one under prompt B, and fit a **nested chain of five increasingly expressive stimulus-invariant maps**: a translation (mean shift), a rigid transformation with uniform scale (orthogonal Procrustes plus one scalar), the same rotation followed by per-axis rescaling, a full affine map (ridge, lambda = 1), and a one-hidden-layer MLP with 512 GELU units trained for 200 epochs on the same Frobenius criterion. Each tier is fitted by minimising squared reconstruction of the B matrix from the A matrix and evaluated with stratified five-fold cross-validation over stimuli, so every reported number is held-out. Because the chain is nested, the explained variance can be split into per-tier increments that sum to one, giving a "tier profile" per layer per cell. They then causally test each fitted map: run the model on the same stimulus under prompt A, replace the layer-l state at the final prompt token with the mapped vector, and decode greedily up to 50 tokens. Four reference conditions anchor this — prompt A untouched, prompt B end to end, and an **oracle patch** that substitutes B's own held-out representation into A's context at the same layer and token. They also test generalisation two ways: fitting the map to three paraphrases of the target prompt and scoring it against the canonical target (LLaVA on StyleTransfer, Llama on EmotionalStory), and fitting on one dataset and applying to another of the same modality (StyleTransfer to EmoSet; WritingStyle to EmotionalStory). Alignment dimensionality is reported as the number of singular values of the centred cross-covariance above one percent of the maximum, with a ten percent threshold as a check.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **replacement model in the weak sense — a fitted transformation class plus its measured share of the variance**. The explanation takes the form "changing this instruction is, to first order, an affine map of the content representation, and the residual rotation/shear is where the behavioural consequences concentrate". It is explicitly population-level (a map between manifolds of many stimuli, not a per-prompt direction) and explicitly partial: the authors say the hierarchy is a measurement framework, that the nonlinear tier is a lower bound on what any nonlinearity could explain, and that residuals beyond the MLP's capacity are left unaccounted. It predicts, and the prediction is tested causally — a fitted affine map should behave like prompt B when injected into prompt A's forward pass.

## How they judged success
Four things, all cross-validated: incremental held-out R-squared per tier (the increment over the predecessor, which the authors note can go negative when a more expressive class generalises worse); two representational-geometry metrics — Spearman correlation between the transformed data's dissimilarity matrix and a target task RDM derived from prompt B's labels, and the silhouette score of the transformed points under the target labels; and behavioural recovery scored by per-dataset keyword evaluators on two flags, **relevance** (does the output address the target attribute at all) and **accuracy** (does it give the right label or count). The oracle patch bounds the achievable ceiling, and the irrelevant-source groups G5/G6 act as controls in which the map must carry task structure across a source prompt that contains none. Generalisation is scored by comparing paraphrase-fitted maps against the canonical-pair ceiling, and transfer by comparing off-diagonal to diagonal tier profiles.

## What they found
Prompt-induced change grows nearly monotonically with depth: normalised squared Frobenius distance sits around 1e-4 to 1e-2 in early layers and reaches 1e-1 to 1 to the top, with the same shape across architectures and modalities. The representation does reorganise toward the instructed task — in the COCO example prompt B's RDM shows the graded count structure that prompt A's does not. In the decomposition, **translation takes the largest share of explained variance at every layer** and the tier ordering translation > affine > rigid-uniform > axis-scaled with nonlinear last holds across all six pair groups, which the authors read as evidence it is not an artefact of one pairing regime. Model families differ in early-layer strategy: OPT-2.7B leans less on translation early and more on rotation, scaling and nonlinear components, while Llama and Qwen look like a lower-dimensional instruction code; BLIP-2 keeps more translation in late layers than the other two VLMs. Causally, translation and uniform-scaled rigid maps are excluded from the geometry panel because they leave distance-based RDM rankings and silhouettes unchanged; axis-scaled rigid improves geometry but stays clearly below affine, affine gains more, and nonlinear gives the strongest geometry recovery with a smaller gap over affine than affine had over axis-scaled. Behaviour shows a sharper divide: translation alone already steers behaviour substantially and each further tier improves it toward prompt B. Qualitative outputs show LLaVA at layer 22 and Qwen3-VL at layer 27 shifting from "yes, there are people" to actual counts once the affine or nonlinear map is applied, and the irrelevant "capital of France" and "2+2" sources being overridden into image-dependent counts. Paraphrase-fitted maps track the canonical ceiling closely for Llama except in the earliest layers, and for LLaVA mainly in deeper layers. Cross-dataset transfer keeps much explanatory power but the tier allocation shifts, with in-domain translation dominance redistributed toward affine, nonlinear and residual off-diagonal, and asymmetrically (EmotionalStory to WritingStyle degrades more than the reverse).

## Limits and open problems they name
Zero-shot instruction prompts only — no few-shot demonstrations, soft prompts or long-context conditioning. Interventions replace the residual stream at one layer and the final prompt token only, so multi-layer and multi-token edits are future work, as are systematic tests under input distribution shift. The nonlinear tier is a single shared MLP and its residual is a lower bound, not a decomposition of what is unexplained. Generalisation is demonstrated in one text and one vision setting each, and the authors concede that the tier-wise allocation of variance is sensitive to the stimulus distribution, so part of what they call the prompt's map is stimulus-specific. Behaviour is scored by keyword dictionaries rather than a judge, and translation and uniform-scaling are omitted from the geometry panel because the metrics are blind to them — a structural limit of distance-based scoring rather than a null result.

## What we take from it
**A method, and a corrective for our own object of explanation.** Two things are worth importing. First, the **nested-chain increment as the measurement**: fit the cheapest map first, then report each richer class only by what it adds on held-out stimuli, with the residual printed as its own term. That converts "we found a direction" into "a direction explains this much, a rotation explains this much, and the rest is not explained by anything in this hierarchy", which is exactly the accounting our white-box model needs before it publishes a steering vector. Second, the **oracle patch as the ceiling** — substituting the target state itself into the source context gives an upper bound that separates "our map is imperfect" from "a single-layer edit at this site cannot deliver this behaviour", and it costs one extra forward pass per prompt. The domain caution is equally useful: the authors' own instrument cannot see a pure translation at all (RDM and silhouette are translation-invariant), so a decomposition that scores geometry will always rank mean shifts last even when they carry most of the variance and most of the behaviour. Warning for us: a tier ordering is a property of the metric family as much as of the model.
