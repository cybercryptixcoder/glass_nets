# Nonparametric independence testing via mutual information

- **Key:** arxiv-1711.06642
- **Authors / organisation:** Thomas B. Berrett; Richard J. Samworth (Statistical Laboratory, University of Cambridge)
- **Date:** 2017-11-17 (v1, single version)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.06642
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (PDF v1, 46 pages, read end to end; figures not inspected as images, reference list skimmed rather than read item by item)
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

The paper asks how to test whether two possibly multivariate random vectors are independent, with no assumption about the shape of the dependence and a guarantee on the false-positive rate. The authors' complaint about the standard toolkit is structural rather than practical: product-moment correlation sees only linear structure, rank correlations only monotone structure, and for a symmetric predictor whose response is its square all three population quantities are exactly zero — so the tests are blind by construction, not merely weak. Their answer is to test a quantity that is zero exactly when independence holds and positive otherwise, and to lean on a family of entropy estimators whose estimation error has already been characterised in any dimension. The larger aim is an independence test that behaves like a classical test — nominal size under the null, with a local-power analysis — but for multivariate data and arbitrary dependence.

## What they studied

No network, no trained model. The unit is an i.i.d. sample of `n` paired observations taking values in a product of Euclidean spaces, and the object certified is a hypothesis test of independence whose statistic is an estimate of the mutual information between the two blocks. Three settings are developed in turn: one marginal density known, both marginals unknown (the realistic case), and a regression setting in which the test is repurposed as a goodness-of-fit check on a linear model. Comparisons are against empirical-copula, kernel (HSIC), distance-covariance and sign-covariance tests on simulated data, and against a small real data set of US city temperatures.

## How they did it

The statistic is built from the entropy decomposition of mutual information — mutual information equals the sum of the two marginal entropies minus the joint — so an estimate of mutual information is a signed sum of three entropy estimates. Each entropy is estimated by a weighted nearest-neighbour estimator of the Kozachenko–Leonenko type: for every point, take the distance to its k-th neighbour in the relevant sample and average a log of a volume-normalised power of that distance, with weights chosen so that the leading bias terms cancel in any dimension and so that the neighbour distances used are not too correlated. The interesting engineering is in the critical value, because the statistic's null distribution is unknown. When one marginal is known, they draw fresh samples from it, re-pair them with the observed other block, and recompute the statistic many times to read off the null quantile; a small algebraic observation saves work and assumptions here, because the marginal entropy of the untouched block appears identically on both sides of the comparison and cancels, so only the joint entropy of the re-paired sample has to be estimated. When neither marginal is known they permute the pairing instead, again recomputing and comparing, and again the marginal entropies cancel so that only the permuted joint entropy is needed. For the regression version they test covariate–error independence using residuals, which is delicate because residuals are not independent draws; they sidestep it by standardising residuals, simulating fresh standardised residual vectors from an assumed error law, and pushing each simulated vector through the same hat-matrix projection so the null statistics are exchangeable with the observed one under the null — and they add a sample-splitting variant for the power proof. Two practical tuning additions: a data-driven choice of k that minimises the disagreement between paired entropy estimates over permuted samples, and a multiscale variant that averages the statistic over a range of k and compares averages.

## What counts as an explanation here

An instrument, with a size guarantee and a power theorem. There is no model of a mechanism and nothing to interpret; what substitutes for explanation is a certificate of two kinds — exact finite-sample size control (proved by an exchangeability/rank argument, for every fixed k and every number of resamples) and uniform power against weak alternatives (proved under an explicit density class). The predictive content is narrow but real: it says how small a dependence can be and still be detected at a given sample size, uniformly over a class, and it is deliberately partial — it certifies detection, not estimation of the strength or shape of dependence.

## How they judged success

For size: the probability of rejection under the null at nominal level q, proved to be at most q for every q and every k. For power: uniform consistency, i.e. power tending to one over classes of densities whose mutual information exceeds a sequence `b`, with the sequence as small as they can make it — `o(n^{-1/2})` in the known-marginal case, and any fixed positive floor in the unknown-marginal case. For the regression test the floor is required to blow up, at a slow rate. Empirically: rejection frequency at nominal 0.05, `n` = 200, 5,000 repetitions per parameter setting, against copula, HSIC, distance-covariance and rank-based competitors run at their authors' default tuning, plus an oracle variant that picks the best k per setting as an upper bound. Three simulated mechanisms (a sinusoidal density whose mutual information is held fixed as the dependence is localised, a ring-vs-disc construction, and a multiplicative-noise construction) each in univariate and bivariate versions, and the tuning variants compared against each other as well as against the competitors.

## What they found

The size result is clean and requires nothing beyond exchangeability of the observed statistic with its simulated or permuted copies, which is why the authors stress that it holds for any k rather than for a well-chosen one. The local power analysis is claimed as the first of its kind for multivariate independence testing, and the achievable rate under the known-marginal case is stated as arbitrarily close to the parametric rate in the exponent of the threshold dependence. Empirically there is no uniformly most powerful test among those compared, as expected, but the picture is informative: the mutual-information tests are strong in the two settings where the dependence is localised and grows harder to find as the localisation parameter rises, which the authors attribute to the freedom to use a small k when dependence is local, and they are competitive but not best where the dependence is global and multiplicative; the multiscale average is markedly better than picking a single k by the data-driven rule and in one setting outperforms even the oracle single-k choice. On the real data (average minimum January temperature, latitude and longitude, 56 cities, 1931–1960), the diagnostic plots are hard to read and the test rejects the normal linear model outright, with the partial-regression follow-up pointing at a cubic relation with longitude after latitude is removed; adding quadratic and cubic longitude terms is overwhelmingly supported, and the extended test on the enlarged model does not reject at the conventional threshold. That sequence — a rejection, a diagnosed shape, a repaired model, and a non-rejection of the repair — is the single most useful worked example in the paper.

## Limits and open problems they name

The regression test needs the error law known up to scale, which is a real restriction (they note the normal case is the practical one). The power guarantees are asymptotic and uniform over smoothness-and-moment classes that are technical and exclude some interesting densities. The tuning remains a genuine awkwardness: they offer two heuristics and no theory saying which k to use, and their own numbers show the data-driven rule being beaten by a fixed range average, which is evidence the selection problem is unsolved rather than merely inconvenient. Unweighted Kozachenko–Leonenko estimators are what the practical tests use, even though the theory is developed for weighted ones, so the implementation is not literally the object analysed. The permutation null also requires the observations to be exchangeable, which is exactly what residuals are not — the reason the regression section exists at all.

## What we take from it

Four things. (1) **A detection instrument with a size proof that costs nothing:** exchangeability of the observed statistic with resampled copies gives size control for *any* tuning parameter, so if we ever report "these two activation summaries are independent", the honest construction is a permutation of the pairing with the entropies cancellation trick, and the report can state size exactly rather than approximately. (2) **The cancellation trick is the reusable refraction:** subtract statistics computed on the same data so the shared, expensive, unknown term disappears — the practical test then needs only the joint term, and the number of tuning parameters to defend drops. (3) **The competence profile is the lesson, not the numbers:** a test that wins on localised dependence and loses on global multiplicative dependence is a test whose skill is conditional, so any independence verdict we report should name the alternative family it was powered against; a single power curve against one simulated alternative is a claim about that alternative only. (4) **The repair sequence on real data is a template for our own audits:** reject, isolate the residual structure by partial regression, add the structure to the surrogate, retest — and report the retest, because a rejection with no follow-up model leaves the reviewer nowhere to go. The warning worth carrying: mutual information is invariant to smooth invertible reparametrisation, so it is invariant under exactly the transformations that make a linear read-out wrong, which is why it is the right null instrument for a nonlinear probe and the wrong one for certifying linearity. Coreference `arxiv-1804.07203` and `arxiv-1807.05405` (the same authors' later arc: where conditioning makes this problem impossible, and the permutation reweighting that restores it in restricted form) and `arxiv-1909.10140`/`arxiv-1910.12327` (rank-based dependence coefficients that target the same any-dependence question with a scalar instead of a test).
