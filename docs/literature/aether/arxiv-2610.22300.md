# Knowledge That Is Linear Before Fine-Tuning Generalizes After It

- **Key:** arxiv-2610.22300
- **Authors / organisation:** Faridoun Mehri; Hinrich Schütze (Center for Information and Language Processing, LMU Munich; Munich Center for Machine Learning)
- **Date:** 2026-10-15 (v2 2026-10-19)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22300
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Language models are expected to infer facts nobody stated — someone described entirely through French schooling and French institutions should still be assumed a French speaker. The authors call this statistical generalization, and distinguish it from entailment (where the new fact follows necessarily) and from paraphrase (where the same fact is merely worded differently). Their observation is that any given fact may or may not support such an inference, and that a model can state a fact reliably while being unable to build anything new on it. They therefore split facts into **active** ones, which carry generalization beyond their own recall, and **inert** ones, which are recalled but do not. The question they pose is what property of a pretrained representation makes the difference, and the answer they test is linear readability. The larger goal is a read-out theory of when existing knowledge can be reused, which is the same question a white-box model has to answer about which of its components are load-bearing.

## What they studied
Eleven open-weight language models from 2B to 27B parameters — G9v3-3B, Granite-4.2-3B, LFM2.5-2.6B-Base, MiniCPM5-2B-Base, Nanbeige4-3B-Base, Phi-4-mini-instruct, Gemma-4-E2B, OLMo-3-1025-7B, Qwen3.5-9B and Qwen3.5-9B-Base (the pair chosen for a direct base-versus-post-trained comparison), and Qwen3.8-27B for scale; base and post-trained checkpoints were mixed by a coin flip per model. Seventy pretrained relations, screened from three sources against subject/answer count requirements: eleven from Hernandez et al.'s relation set (two spelling relations dropped because the answer is readable off the subject's own letters), six of the authors' own Town relations over a shared pool of 300 towns, and fifty-three usable BEAR-big relations (place of detention excluded on privacy grounds). The unit of analysis has two levels: the **relation** (its three scores, correlated across the 70 within each model), and the **held-out subject** (individual errors attributed to explanations). The probe reads the last-token state of the subject span at one selected layer of the query template.

## How they did it
A new relation is manufactured from each pretrained one by drawing a random bijection from its answers to a subset of an animal label set, so continent becomes animal (Europe to crab, Asia to zebra) and subjects sharing a continent share an animal; the new relation is therefore unknown to the model but inherits its structure, and the model can only generalize by building on what it already knows. Subjects are split into fine-tuning and held-out sets, with at least four training subjects per answer and at least one held-out subject; the held-out subjects are never used to fit, select or tune anything. The model is fully fine-tuned for 20 epochs (80 exposures per fact across four wording templates, a fifth template reserved for held-out evaluation) until training accuracy is 1.00 on every model — Table 3 is all 1.00 cells — so the test is not about whether the new relation was learned. Three quantities are then measured: **Recall** (the pretrained model's class-balanced candidate accuracy on the original relation), **Generalization** (the fine-tuned model's class-balanced accuracy on held-out subjects of the new relation), and **Pre-linearity** (a linear probe's class-balanced accuracy on the subject's state). The probe is deliberately fixed in advance: the state is reduced to 16 dimensions by PCA plus whitening, the classifier is softmax over those 16, ridge coefficient 10^-2, the same for every model and relation, never tuned; layer choice and everything else are handled inside a nested cross-validation of ten outer folds over the fine-tuning subjects and five inner folds for layer selection only, with every fit recomputing its own mean, principal directions and scales, and a majority-class baseline subtracted at the end. Four controls are computed per relation — Recall, log answer count, log fine-tuning subject count, and subject frequency in the Dolma v1.7 corpus counted through the infini-gram API — and partial correlations given each alone and given all four jointly. Robustness comes from three re-runs that change only the bijection and the fine-tuning seed, and from a label-set comparison that swaps animals for thirteen other WordNet noun categories on the smallest model. Finally, every wrong held-out answer is mapped back through the bijection to a wrong original answer and credited to whichever explanations name it, split equally when several do — a Shapley game over six explanations (probe top-three, pretrained top-three, most frequent answer, five nearest subjects by cosine, pre-fine-tuning answer) with two baselines: a shuffle that keeps each model's wrong-label preferences but breaks their link to the subject, and uniform chance.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **probe used as a predictor**, not a mechanism. The claim is not that the probe's 16-dimensional space is how the model stores the relation — it is that a specific linear read-out of the pretrained state forecasts, before any fine-tuning happens, how well knowledge built on that relation will carry to subjects never trained on. The form is intentionally partial and correlational: one number per relation per model, a correlation across relations, and a separate within-relation error attribution that is meant to block the obvious alternative reading ("the fine-tuned model is just repeating its pretrained answer"). The predictive content is a per-relation ordering: rank the relations by Pre-linearity and you mostly rank them by Generalization.

## How they judged success
Pearson r and Spearman rho across each model's 70 relations, with 95% percentile bootstrap intervals of 10,000 resamples over relations reported as a symmetric half-width, partial correlations with the four regressions refit (and ranks retaken for rho) inside every resample. Judge success was set in advance as: the correlation holds in every model, survives each control alone and all four jointly, exceeds Recall's, and does not move under re-randomization or relabelling. Error analysis is judged against the shuffled and chance baselines as exact expectations rather than sampled draws, with intervals from bootstrap resamples over the 757 fine-tuned model–relation pairs that made at least one error.

## What they found
Pre-linearity beats Recall as a predictor. Across relations within a model the raw correlation with Generalization is r = 0.87 to 0.92 (mean 0.89, rho 0.85 to 0.90), and with all four controls partialled out jointly it is still 0.70 to 0.82 (rho 0.75 to 0.85), no interval falling below 0.56. Recall alone correlates at r = 0.63 to 0.74, but once Pre-linearity is controlled for Recall drops to 0.40 to 0.58 (rho 0.25 to 0.42), and its rho interval excludes zero in only eight of the eleven models — so Recall's apparent predictiveness is largely borrowed. The association is insensitive to the arbitrary choices: three re-runs differing only in bijection and seed give 0.92, 0.93 and 0.92 on the smallest model (mean of three 0.93), on the 17 Hernandez and Town relations re-run three times the per-run r lies between 0.78 and 0.95 across ten models, and swapping animals for thirteen other categories keeps r at 0.91 to 0.93 with the relation ordering at rho 0.95 to 0.97. The error attribution is the paper's most surprising number: pooled over 37,571 wrong held-out answers from 757 fine-tuned models, the probe's top three answers take 21.5% of errors against 8.8% under the subject-broken shuffle, while the pretrained model's own top three take 11.5% against 7.3% — the fine-tuned model's mistakes look more like the probe's ranking than like its own pretrained ranking. The one explanation the shuffle cannot test, the most-frequent answer, rises to 6.1% shuffled from 5.0% observed yet still clears chance at 1.9%, which the authors report honestly rather than hiding.

## Limits and open problems they name
The setting is one narrow manufactured task, not free-form learning: the new relation is a relabelling inside a fictional frame, so the evidence is that structure carries for arbitrary bijections of an existing relation, not that any downstream learning benefits. Only first tokens of answers are scored, so answers sharing a first token collapse into one effective answer, and the label sets are pruned hard to make that safe (179 animal labels survive seven filtering steps from 3,048). The relation inventory is skewed toward entity–attribute relations with many subjects; the authors' own screen excludes anything with fewer than 25 fine-tuning subjects, which removes exactly the sparse long-tail relations where generalization would be hardest. The probe is a 16-dimensional read-out with hyperparameters fixed by hand and never tuned, and the error attribution deliberately uses one probe refit on all fine-tuning subjects at its most frequently selected layer, so "Pre-linearity" is one instrument's reading rather than a property of the representation; the correlation is across relations, and nothing here predicts a single new relation's absolute generalization accuracy. The error analysis pools over models and relations, so it cannot say which model follows its probe most closely.

## What we take from it
Three things, in order of usefulness for a predictive white-box model. First, a **transfer design** we can copy almost verbatim: train on a subset of a structure, hold out entities that share a latent group with the training ones, and require the model to move the new label to the unseen entity — that isolates "did the representation supply the structure" from "did the model memorize", which is precisely the question our model must answer when it claims a component predicts a behaviour it was not fitted on. Second, a **two-instrument honesty check** buried in the design and easy to miss: Pre-linearity and Recall are both accuracy numbers of a linear classifier over the same subjects, and the paper's whole result is that they diverge, so the folder's recurring warning gets a clean instance — a readable state and a recalled fact are different measurements, and only one of them broke down by relation. Third — the warning this entry exists for — **a forecast that beats its control can still be a pooled average that hides the per-model question**: the shuffled baseline is the strictest null the paper could construct (each model's wrong-label preferences held fixed, only their link to the subject broken), and 21.5 against 8.8 against it is the strongest single piece of evidence here, yet both numbers are shares of errors pooled over 757 fine-tuned models with no per-model breakdown, which is exactly the resolution our own reports need. The reusable instrument is the **subject-broken shuffle** itself: permute the input–output pairing while preserving the output distribution, and compare any attribution we report against it rather than against chance alone.
