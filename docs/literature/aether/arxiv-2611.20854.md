# When Is the Expensive Method Worth It? A Content-Driven Analysis of Classical Bayer Demosaicing

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.20854
- **Authors / organisation:** Taranum Wasu, Harsh Kashyap, HS Pannu; Thapar Institute of Engineering and Technology, Patiala, India
- **Date:** 2026-11-16
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20854
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (HTML build, all six sections plus references; the four data figures were not inspected, the numbers below come from the tables and from the prose that restates them)

## What they wanted, and why
Every classical demosaicing algorithm is a prior on how images behave, and those priors are ranked by their average score over a benchmark. The authors' complaint is that a dataset-average ranking answers the wrong question for anyone paying for compute: it says which method wins on average, not whether the directional, roughly ten-times-slower method is worth its cost on *this* image, or which cheap method to fall back to when it is not. The goal is therefore a method-selection policy driven by measurable image content, in which every step — the descriptors, the threshold, the tree — is inspectable rather than trained end to end into a black box. It is written against the learned-demosaicing literature on the grounds that learned reconstruction needs large training sets and offers little interpretability, so the classical family is the one where the cost–quality trade-off can be stated exactly.

## What they studied
No neural network of any kind. Six hand-written classical demosaicers over a Bayer RGGB mosaic: nearest neighbour, bilinear, colour-difference (smooth-hue), Malvar–He–Cutler, Alleysson's frequency-domain luminance/chrominance separation, and Menon's edge-aware directional method as the expensive one. The unit of analysis is the **image**: 80 benchmark photographs — 24 Kodak (768×512), 18 McMaster (500×500), 20 BSD100, 18 Urban100 — each assigned by visual inspection to one of five content categories (smooth, edge-heavy, fine texture, repetitive/high-frequency, high colour). Nine content descriptors per image: Canny edge density, Sobel gradient mean and standard deviation, local variance, FFT high-frequency energy, Lab colour variance, HSV saturation mean and standard deviation, and a Laplacian noise estimate.

## How they did it
A controlled simulate–reconstruct loop. Each reference image is converted to linear RGB, sampled through the Bayer pattern to a single-channel mosaic, reconstructed by each of the six methods, converted back to sRGB, and compared against the reference. Sampling and reconstruction happen in linear light; metrics are computed in 8-bit sRGB; every preprocessing setting is shared across methods so the method is the only variable. A spatially constant image reconstructs with zero error under all methods as a correctness check on the mosaicing and channel indexing. Quality is measured five ways so the conclusion does not rest on one: PSNR, luminance SSIM, ΔE2000 in CIE Lab, an edge-preservation score (correlation of gradient-magnitude maps), and an edge-localized artifact score built from chroma error plus the local 5×5 variance of luminance error, both averaged over the 20% highest-gradient pixels. The central quantity is defined per image as the expensive method's PSNR minus the best PSNR in the fast tier (bilinear, colour-difference, Malvar, Alleysson), i.e. the extra quality actually bought by the extra compute. Nine content descriptors were **written to disk before the experiment matrix was run**, and their correlation with that gain was then measured. Finally a depth-2 decision tree over the descriptors, fitted to recommend the best fast-tier method, is evaluated by leave-one-image-out cross-validation against a constant always-pick-the-most-common baseline.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not a circuit or a dictionary but a **policy with a stated mechanism**: image saturation predicts how much the directional method buys, and the reason given is structural — the expensive method's advantage is interpolation along edges, so on saturated images with little directional structure there is nothing for it to exploit and its chroma handling is no better than a good linear method's. The explanation is intended to be complete in the narrow sense that matters for deployment (a predictive statement about a per-image quantity from pre-computable descriptors) and explicitly partial in scope (clean images, one CFA, PSNR as the primary axis). It is a description of *when* a prior is the right prior, not of what any single method computes.

## How they judged success
Per-image gain distribution (range and mean, with a paired t-test over images); Pearson correlations of that gain with each of the nine pre-registered descriptors, with p-values; per-category means with 95% confidence intervals and a two-sample t-test between the best and worst categories; fast-tier win counts per metric; and recommender accuracy under leave-one-image-out validation against the best constant baseline.

## What they found
Average ranking first: edge-aware leads every metric but by a small margin — 31.78 dB PSNR against 30.40 (Alleysson), 30.07 (Malvar), 29.03 (colour-difference), 28.80 (bilinear), 25.36 (nearest) — with artifact score 10.48 against 13.07 for the next best. The per-image gain is anything but constant: it spans −6.6 to +6.8 dB, mean +0.85 dB with a paired t of 2.77 and p = 0.007, and on 25 of 80 images (31%) it is zero or negative, so a cheap method matches or beats the expensive one. Saturation is the dominant predictor (r = −0.86, p < 10⁻⁶), colour variance follows it (r = −0.71), and gradient standard deviation predicts the opposite direction of gain (r = +0.36, p = 0.001). By category the expensive method pays on smooth (+2.01 dB), edge-heavy (+1.89) and repetitive/high-frequency (+1.57) images, and is *harmful on average* on high-colour images (−1.56 dB; the best-versus-worst category difference is significant at t = 3.13, p = 0.004). Within the fast tier the winner is content-dependent — Malvar takes 35 of 80 on PSNR, Alleysson 30, bilinear 10, colour-difference 5 — and metric-dependent (Malvar's edge-preservation wins 61 of 80 while Alleysson takes 26 of 80 on the artifact score). The depth-2 tree over the fixed descriptors reaches 63.8% leave-one-out accuracy against 43.8% for always-pick-Malvar, a 20.0-point lift, and its rules are readable: saturation splits first, colour variance then separates Malvar from the frequency-domain and bilinear winners.

## Limits and open problems they name
Eighty images is a modest sample relative to the content space, and the authors present the correlations as suggestive rather than definitive. Everything is measured on clean images with PSNR as the primary axis, so a per-metric or noisy-capture extension may move some winners; the noisy regime is named as future work because it stresses the directional method's assumptions. The fast-tier recommender only chooses among the cheap methods, and bilinear is kept as a baseline floor rather than as a serious candidate. Two disclosures worth noting for their honesty: an initial hand-tuned rule set underperformed the constant baseline and was replaced by the data-derived tree, and the error analysis shows the misclassified images cluster at the tree's decision boundaries — where the two candidate methods are nearly tied, so the error costs little.

## What we take from it
Three things. (1) **An aggregate ranking and a per-instance decision are different instruments, and this paper's 31.78-over-30.40 gap shows how much an average can hide**: on nearly a third of instances the ranking's winner is a loss, and the honest headline for our read-out comparisons is not "method A beats B on average" but the distribution of the per-item difference with the share of items where it is zero or negative. Our probe-versus-probe tables are built exactly like the dataset-average ranking this paper was written to replace. (2) **A pre-registered descriptor set is the cheap substitute for a held-out split**: writing the nine descriptors and their definitions to disk before running the matrix is what licenses the r = −0.86 claim, and it costs nothing — the equivalent discipline for us is to fix which measurements count as candidate explanations before the ablation battery runs, so the reported correlate is not chosen from the results. (3) **A decision rule whose splits are readable is worth more than a marginally better opaque one**, and the depth-2 tree is the minimal form: a single dominant split (saturation) with one refinement, 20 points over the constant baseline, with the error mode identified as boundary cases where the choice does not matter. If a routing rule over our instruments needs a deep model to express it, that is a signal the instruments are not yet separable by anything we can state. **Warning:** the correlation is on 80 images and the authors say so; the mechanism story (saturation leaves no directional structure to exploit) is plausible and consistent with the category means, but it is an interpretation of the correlation rather than an experiment that varies saturation independently.
