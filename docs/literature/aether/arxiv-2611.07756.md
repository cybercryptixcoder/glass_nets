# Scaling Laws for SAE Training Data

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.07756
- **Authors / organisation:** Nikita Koriagin (work done while at T-Tech), Nikita Balagansky, Daniil Gavrilov — Yandex / T-Tech

- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.07756
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (arXiv HTML v1, 53,437 clean chars; abstract, §§1–6, Tables 1–9, Appendices A–E including the fitted-parameter table and the NeurIPS checklist's own limitations answers). Figures 1–5 used only through their captions and the prose sentences that state their values; no curve was digitised.
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

Training a sparse autoencoder at scale means storing enormous numbers of activation vectors, and the standard practice is to keep one fresh vector per optimisation token. The authors separate two quantities that the usual "training budget" conflates: the **total number of activation presentations** the optimiser sees, and the **number of unique activations** in the buffer it draws them from. Their question is how large the buffer must be before replaying it is as good as streaming fresh data for the rest of the budget — because only the second quantity sets the storage cost. The larger goal is a practical sizing rule with a fitted curve behind it, in the tradition of compute-optimal and data-constrained scaling laws, so that a lab can pick a dictionary width and a budget and be told how much disk it needs. Interpretability is framed as the beneficiary: less storage per SAE means more SAEs and more sizes explored for the same cost.

## What they studied

BatchTopK SAEs on residual-stream activations of Llama-3.1 8B layer 15 as the main grid, at target average sparsity k = 32, dictionary sizes 16k / 32k / 64k / 128k, training on FineWeb. Validation on Llama layers 5, 15 and 25, and on Qwen3-1.7B / 4B / 8B at comparable middle layers with standard TopK; gate comparison between BatchTopK, TopK and JumpReLU at D = 32k; domain sweeps on FineMath-3plus and Stack-Edu Python. The unit of analysis is the **dictionary as a whole** (its explained variance and its downstream scores), not individual features — the paper says nothing about what any latent means, and explicitly treats the effective activation count as a discount on repeated data rather than a count of features.

## How they did it

They hold the total optimisation budget fixed within each sweep and vary how much of the data is unique. For each total budget N_total ∈ {128M, 640M, 1.28B} tokens and each width, they draw a buffer of U unique activations from a fixed fraction grid U/N_total ∈ {1/128 … 1}, then train for E = N_total / U epochs over it — so the left end of a curve is 128 passes over a tiny buffer and the right end is one pass over the whole set, always at the same number of optimiser steps. That is 96 runs (3 budgets × 4 widths × 8 fractions). Quality is held-out explained variance on a 10^6-token FineWeb set excluded from every buffer (activations normalised to unit norm; AdamW, lr 3e-4 cosine to 1e-6, batch 4096, dead-latent auxiliary penalty 1/32). Saturation is defined as the smallest tested buffer whose EV is within ϵ = 0.005 of the best value on that curve, and since the grid is logarithmic the answer is reported as an interval between adjacent points. They then evaluate 24 checkpoints from the layer-15 / 640M BatchTopK sweep on SAEBench (reconstruction MSE, feature absorption, automated interpretability, sparse probing), run the buffer sweep at other layers, other model sizes, other gates and other domains, and fit a two-term law with a repetition-discount term borrowed from data-constrained language-model scaling.

## What counts as an explanation here

Not an explanation of any model behaviour — this is a **measured tradeoff and a fitted predictive law**: EV is modelled as a ceiling minus a capacity penalty in dictionary width minus a data penalty in effective activations, and the repackaging of U unique activations replayed E times into an "effective count" is a phenomenological discount with a fitted repetition scale. Its predictive claim is narrow and testable: given (D, N_total, U), predict held-out EV, and from the predicted curve read off the buffer at which the quality plateau is reached. The authors are careful that the effective count does not measure features and that the ceiling parameter is a property of the model, layer, normalisation, objective and sparsity, not a universal constant.

## How they judged success

Primary metric held-out explained variance at a stated absolute tolerance (with reconstruction MSE as a consistency check, since EV rescales error on a fixed set); downstream SAEBench metrics quoted with their own saturation thresholds computed the same 5%-of-range way; fit quality by RMSE in EV units and model comparison by AIC between a constant repetition scale and a width-dependent one; validation by predicting a held-out budget (fit on 128M and 1.28B, predict 640M), a held-out width (fit on the three smaller dictionaries, predict 128k), and curve shape transferred to other layers and models with level parameters refitted. No seed replicates, no error bars, no confidence intervals — the checklist says so plainly.

## What they found

Saturation is real and it happens early. At a 128M budget, going from 1M to 128M unique activations lifts EV from 0.5801 to 0.6393 (16k dictionary) and from 0.5870 to 0.6610 (32k); past the plateau, replaying the buffer is close to using fresh activations throughout. The saturation interval at the largest budget scales roughly linearly with width — 20–40M for 16k, 40–80M for 32k, 80–160M for 64k, 160–320M for 128k — and barely moves with the budget once the budget comfortably exceeds it (16k, 32k and 64k all share intervals across 640M and 1.28B), so the storage saving grows with budget: 64× for the 16k dictionary at 1.28B tokens, and at least 8× in every grid cell. Concretely, the full 1.28B corpus in bf16 at 8 KiB per activation is 9.54 TiB; the recommended 20M buffer is 152.6 GiB. Wider dictionaries reach higher plateaus but need more diversity: doubling D raises best EV by about 2–3 points and moves the saturation interval by about 2×, and the 128k dictionary's 0.711 at 640M is diagnosed as undertraining rather than a ceiling. The fitted law collapses all four widths and three budgets onto one power-law curve after the capacity term is removed (RMSE 4.8e-3 EV), AIC prefers a repetition scale that shrinks with width (γ = 0.526, c = 103.3, ΔAIC −77.3 against the constant-scale model: doubling width multiplies the repetition scale by ≈0.69, so wide dictionaries tolerate fewer replay passes), and prediction of the held-out 640M budget from the two extreme budgets gives 3.1e-3 EV RMSE. Downstream checks are the interesting part: at the buffer chosen by EV, reconstruction MSE is near its best, AutoInterp stays high, sparse probing is nearly flat — but **absorption does not improve monotonically and its 32k and 64k curves rise with diversity**, and AutoInterp's own saturation point sits at 160M for the 32k and 64k widths against an EV recommendation of 40M and 80M. Domain sweeps keep the pattern with different constants (FineMath 80M, Python 40M at 640M budget), and the tolerance is a first-class variable: tightening ϵ to 0.0025 doubles the Python buffer and halves the saving, loosening to 0.010 halves the buffer.

## Limits and open problems they name

Single training run per cell, so no seed variance and no error bars; the full grid is one model, one layer, one sparsity, one dataset; the layer/model/gate sweeps test curve shape with level parameters refitted, so they cannot establish that the exponents or coefficients transfer; extrapolation to a wider dictionary degrades exactly in the undertrained regime, which is the regime a practitioner sizing a big dictionary is most likely to be in; the saturation interval is an interval between grid points, not a located threshold; gate comparison is at one width and confounded by sparsity (JumpReLU settles near L0 ≈ 100, so its higher EV cannot be attributed to the gate); SAEBench covers only the BatchTopK layer-15 sweep; and no end-to-end runtime was measured, only the storage ratio. The authors also flag that "feature absorption" and "interpretability score" moving in different directions from reconstruction is not resolved by this work.

## What we take from it

**A method and a measure, plus a warning.** The method: when our own battery pays for activations — a probe sweep, an intervention grid, a per-layer dictionary — the diversity–repetition split is a free experimental axis and a cheap pilot sweep, and reporting the plateau interval rather than a single chosen size is the honest form. The measure: **the saturation point per metric, not per run** — this paper's Appendix C is a one-table template we can copy directly, and its content is the finding most relevant to us: reconstruction saturates at one buffer size, sparse probing at another, AutoInterp at a third, and absorption does not saturate monotonically at all. Any statement of the form "we trained an SAE at size X and it is good" is therefore a statement about whichever metric was used to choose X, and the metrics disagree. The warning: a scaling law fitted on EV is a law about *reconstruction*, and using it to size a dictionary for a feature-level claim imports an assumption the paper explicitly declines to make — that the effective activation count measures features. For us the practical rule is that a white-box artefact's training-data budget should be set against the metric the artefact's *claim* needs, and that the gap between that metric's saturation and the EV saturation is the amount by which our dictionaries are being under- or over-trained.
