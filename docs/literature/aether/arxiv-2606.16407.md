# A Mechanistic Understanding of Pronoun Fidelity in LLMs

- **Key:** arxiv-2606.16407
- **Authors / organisation:** Katharina Trinley, Jesujoba O. Alabi, Dietrich Klakow, Vagrant Gautam; Saarland University (Saarbrücken) and Heidelberg Institute for Theoretical Studies
- **Date:** 2026-06-15
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.16407
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

Pronoun fidelity is the task of reusing a pronoun for an entity after other entities with other pronouns have been mentioned (the technician … the customer … the technician said that ___ ). It is trivial for people and hard for language models: adding one distractor sentence about a second person drops accuracy sharply in prior work, and accuracy also falls as one moves from he to she to singular they. Earlier studies of *why* models fail were purely behavioural — they varied the input and watched the output — so they could not say whether the model was binding an entity to its pronoun, repeating the most recent surface form, or falling back on an occupation stereotype. The authors want the same question answered with interventions on internal representations: which of those three causal hypotheses are actually implemented in the network, how the implemented ones combine, and which attention heads carry them. This is squarely our target — a small set of named causal variables that predict behaviour rather than describe it.

## What they studied

Ten instruction-tuned decoder-only models from four families: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct; gemma-2-9b-it and gemma-2-27b-it; Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct; and four sizes of OLMo-2 (1B, 7B, 13B, 32B), with two 7B OLMo checkpoints distinguished by their release date. Data comes from RUFF, a template-built dataset of several million pronoun-fidelity instances, restricted here to zero or one distractor sentence and to the three singular pronouns that tokenize as one token (he, she, they); neopronouns are excluded because they split across tokens. The unit of analysis moves across three levels: causal subspaces of the residual stream (learned by distributed alignment search), the model's output distribution over three pronouns, and individual attention heads.

## How they did it

Each failure mode is written down as a high-level causal model: group binding G reads the occupied entity's stated pronoun, recency R reads the last pronoun mentioned, stereotype S reads the occupation's prior pronoun. Three diagnostic datasets of 14,400 instances each are then built so that two of the three mechanisms agree on the source input and only the third differs, which makes a single interchange intervention informative about that third mechanism. At every second layer of each model they train a boundless DAS featurizer — an orthogonal rotation of the residual stream plus differentiable soft boundaries on how many dimensions to swap — for each mechanism and each dataset, and keep the layer with the best interchange intervention accuracy (IIA), the fraction of intervened runs whose output matches the high-level prediction. Stereotype has its own IIA variant that scores against the occupation's stereotypical pronoun rather than the sentence's stated one. Specificity is then tested by freezing the three learned rotations and scoring each on all three datasets, giving a 3×3 transfer matrix per model. The three mechanisms are combined into a mixture over pronoun distributions: for each mechanism and example, an inference-only interchange intervention at its best layer yields a softmax over he/she/they, and a small weighted model over the triple of predictions is fitted with a Jensen-Shannon divergence loss, then ablated mechanism by mechanism. Finally two new copying datasets of 50 identification and 50 held-out examples separate concept-level from token-level copying, heads are ranked by the log-probability gain from patching their pre-projection output from clean into corrupt runs, and the top hundred are ablated singly and cumulatively, against a same-size random-head ablation.

## What counts as an explanation here

A low-level causal model: three named high-level variables, each realised as a learned linear subspace of the residual stream at a specified layer, with interchange intervention accuracy as the certificate that the subspace is causally sufficient, plus a weighted mixture that turns the three into a predictor of the output distribution. It is partial by design — it does not claim the three mechanisms are exhaustive, and the attention-head analysis is a separate, non-causal-model account of *where* the routes live. It is also explicitly a competition story rather than a decomposition: the mechanisms are all active at once and the output is whichever wins.

## How they judged success

Interchange intervention accuracy per mechanism per layer; a best-layer per mechanism from a search over every second layer; a cross-mechanism specificity matrix (rotation × diagnostic dataset) with each rotation frozen and applied out of its own dataset; Jensen-Shannon similarity between the mixture model's predicted distribution and the empirical distribution from interventions, reported for the full mixture and for every single- and double-mechanism ablation; and cumulative head ablation deltas in accuracy against a random-ablation baseline. Baselines are the ablated mixture itself and the random-head control; the layer search is the search baseline for depth. No significance tests are reported; base accuracies on the underlying task are tabulated per model and distractor setting.

## What they found

All three subspaces exist in every model and each is individually sufficient to flip a prediction — worth quoting, since it is why the authors say no hypothesis can be ruled out by absence. IIA is not uniform: in OLMo-2-7B group binding reaches 0.935 against 0.665 for stereotype, while in Qwen2.5-7B and OLMo-2-13B stereotype leads instead, at 0.84 and 0.76 ahead of recency (0.76, 0.72) and group binding (0.74, 0.62), and gemma-2-9B reaches 1.00 on both group binding and recency at a single layer. The specificity matrix is the sharpest result: recency is distinct from the other two in every model — in gemma-2-9B its frozen rotation scores 0.99 on its own dataset and 0.00 on the group-binding dataset — whereas group binding and stereotype are never clearly separable from one another, rising and falling together. The mixture explains almost all behaviour, with Jensen-Shannon similarity from 0.915 to 0.995 across the ten models, but the ablations say the coverage is carried by two named heuristics rather than three: removing recency and stereotype together costs the most everywhere, while removing group binding sometimes leaves the fit unchanged or improves it (0.022 higher in OLMo-2-13B), and in Qwen2.5-7B, where stereotype was the dominant single subspace, dropping it lowers the fit from 0.915 to 0.859. The head analysis splits the two routes structurally — concept-level copying is carried by a few heads (OLMo-2-1B and OLMo-2-7B fall to 0.16 accuracy after ablating 11 heads, and Qwen2.5-7B to 0.30 after 29, where random ablations do almost nothing), while token-level copying has no distinct head set (OLMo-2-1B needs 72 heads to fall from 0.80 to 0.44) and in Qwen2.5-7B and Llama-3.1-8B ablating its heads *raises* accuracy by 0.44 and 0.40. That last pair is the paper's evidence for competition: suppressing one route lets the other produce a correct answer.

## Limits and open problems they name

RUFF is English-only and template-generated, so ecological validity is traded for control; only the 0- and 1-distractor settings are used out of the 0-5 available, and accuracy under more distractors may be shaped by different mechanisms. Because English has exactly three singular pronouns that tokenize singly, the diagnostic datasets cannot represent all three mechanisms independently inside one dataset — one pronoun always has to stand for each. There may be another single mechanism that fits behaviour as well as the three-way mixture. Circuit discovery was not used, on the stated grounds that the same task may be implemented by several distinct circuits and so be less robust as an explanation; the head analysis is activation patching, not a circuit. The sample is instruction-tuned decoder-only models; encoder-only and reasoning models are untested and known to behave differently on RUFF. The group-binding subspace overlaps stereotype so much that the two are not separated by this evidence.

## What we take from it

A reusable instrument, first: the two-mechanisms-agree, one-differs construction is the cheapest way to make a single interchange intervention attributable, and it is stricter than post-hoc layer differences, which the authors explicitly say are only suggestive because two mechanisms that agree on a prediction both score well. A measure, second: the frozen-rotation transfer matrix — train every subspace, then score every subspace on every isolated dataset — is a specificity test we can copy directly, and it is what turns "three subspaces were found" into "two of the three are distinct and one is a duplicate". A warning, third, and the one that stays with us longest: the mixture reaches 0.915-0.995 fit while one of its three components is removable at no cost, so *coverage of behaviour is not evidence that a component is load-bearing* — the ablation, not the fit, decides. The Jensen-Shannon mixture over per-mechanism intervention softmaxes is also the closest thing on disk to a small white-box predictor of an output distribution rather than a single label, and it costs one forward pass per mechanism per example. Finally their competition framing is a useful hedge against decomposition-by-default: a model where two routes carry the same signal may be *simpler* to describe as a contest than as a sum.
