# Sample Complexity and Decision-Theoretic Guarantees for Bayesian Model Averaging over Decision Trees with Catalan-Exponential Priors

- **Date:** 2026-06 (v1 2026-05-31, v2 2026-06-02; submitted to JMLR)

- **Kind:** paper

## What they wanted, and why
Their baseline model is a Bayesian decision tree whose leaves carry Dirichlet-Multinomial likelihoods and whose size prior is built on Catalan numbers, a construction the same two authors introduced earlier; empirically it beat the standard Chipman-prior Bayesian CART on small medical imaging sets, and nobody had said why. The paper's actual question is not statistical but decisional, and it is stated in the abstract: at what point do model-averaging weights carry enough information about *which* account is right that one may stop hedging and start exploiting the average? They call the alternative "epistemic fragility" and take it from the aleatoric/epistemic split, reading fragility as uncertainty that extra data could remove. The wider aim is to give a closed-form answer — a sample-size threshold — so that a practitioner knows when committing to an averaged account is warranted rather than merely convenient.

## What they studied
Binary decision trees with k leaves, C-class Dirichlet-Multinomial leaf models, a Catalan-exponential prior over the number of leaves (mass proportional to a decaying exponential divided by the k-1st Catalan number, so the base measure is uniform over the Catalan many tree topologies of that size), and WAIC as the model-selection criterion feeding the averaging weights. The comparison prior is Chipman's, with default split hyperparameters. Unit of analysis is the **weight vector over tree sizes** and the posterior concentrated on the true size; posterior sampling is by reversible-jump MCMC with four move types. One real dataset is used throughout — a 40-sample knee osteoarthritis set with two regions of interest — and everything else is simulation with simulated leaves whose true size is known.

## How they did it
Four results, each proved and then simulated under a fixed seed, with code published. First, a per-leaf expansion: the WAIC deviance and the leave-one-out deviance for a Dirichlet-Multinomial leaf differ by a term that is exactly proportional to N over N plus the prior strength, so the difference between two models' criteria is of order one over N and their rankings agree in the limit; an asymptotic normal argument then turns the per-observation advantage of the true account into a minimum sample size. Second, they characterise the Catalan prior's tail by bounding the Catalan numbers from below, extracting the successive-mass ratio, and fitting a multiplicative correction to the asymptotic decay rate; they then bound the posterior mass on the wrong size by splitting it into an underfitting part that decays exponentially in N and an overfitting part that is the prior tail times a power of N, and simulate the concentration curve against Chipman's at increasing N. Third, a Jensen argument gives a convexity bound on the averaged account's risk plus an excess-risk bound that decays exponentially once selection is consistent. Fourth, they apply McAllester's PAC-Bayes bound to the weight vector treated as a posterior, obtaining a sample size proportional to the prior's log-loss at the true size. The decision-theoretic section recasts all four thresholds as the point where an entropy penalty on committed actions switches off. Appendices prove reversible-jump detailed balance for the birth/death pair, give a calibration bound whose leading term scales with the prior's expected tree size over N, and give an asymmetric-loss threshold analysis.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not a description of any model but a **set of closed-form thresholds**: for a stated per-observation evidence advantage, a stated confidence, or a stated risk tolerance, the formulas return the number of observations at which the hedging weights become informative. The object is a *timing* statement about the account ensemble, and it is predictive in a narrow sense — it predicts, before seeing data, which sample sizes will produce a degenerate weight vector and which a spread one. The decision-theoretic layer adds a second object, a per-account commitment cost equal to minus the log prior mass at that account, which they read as the KL price of trusting it.

## How they judged success
Agreement between formula and simulation: the WAIC–LOO gap in simulation against the closed-form expression (reported maximum deviation 0.03, and the formula's exact constant versus the full expression at N equals the prior strength); the simulated concentration curve for the posterior on the true size, with the smallest N reaching 0.95 reported per prior, averaged over 50 datasets; the oracle and Jensen bounds checked against measured risk over 80 datasets with the ratio of bound to measured error printed; the WAIC selection rate, which they report reaching 100% at a sample size matched to the predicted threshold; the universal decay-correction constant checked across a range of decay parameters to six decimals; and PAC-Bayes penalties tabulated per true size. The reversible-jump chain's numerical convergence is reported as a total-variation distance over 50,000 steps. There is no comparison against any non-Bayesian predictor and no external benchmark — the baselines are entirely other priors inside the same model family.

## What they found
The decision threshold and the model threshold disagree, and that disagreement is the paper's most useful number. For the knee dataset the per-observation advantages are 0.182 and 0.134 for the two regions, giving minimum samples of 29.7 and 40.4 against 40 available — so one region is just above and one sits exactly at the boundary, which they offer as the explanation of the earlier empirical failure there. But the PAC-Bayes analysis says the sparse model that regime needs would require 74 samples under the Catalan prior and 599 under Chipman's at a risk tolerance of 0.05, i.e. the Catalan prior buys a factor of 8.1 at true size one and *loses* to Chipman by about two to two-and-a-half times at true sizes two through four. The concentration simulation puts the crossover at roughly 126: Chipman concentrates better below that, Catalan better above, ending at 2.3% residual posterior error against 5.2% at 800. In the oracle-inequality table the Jensen bound exceeds the measured excess risk in every cell with ratios from 1.06 to 1.21, and measured excess risk falls from 0.087 to 0.002 across sample sizes 20 to 800. The Catalan prior's expected tree size is 1.373 against 2.509, and the resulting calibration bound is reported as 26% tighter.

## Limits and open problems they name
Everything is exact only for Dirichlet-Multinomial leaves; a Gaussian leaf model would need a different variance calculation, so the constant in the threshold is model-specific. They concede that the PAC-Bayes bound is vacuous for large models and report their own penalty term as non-trivial but below the random-guessing baseline at their operating point. The framework is static — one commit, no sequential decisions — and they flag a belief-state extension as the natural next step, with the entropy collapse rate as the quantity that would drive it. The decision-theoretic reading is presented as an instantiation of a criterion from other authors rather than as a tested theory. Two weaknesses the paper does not name: the "explanation" of the real-data failure is a coincidence of two numbers computed from the same dataset, not a prediction tested on held-out data; and the whole analysis compares priors within one family, so it cannot tell a reader whether either prior is a good account of the task.

## What we take from it
A conjunction to adopt and a warning to keep. Adopt the **shape**: a commitment threshold should be reported as the sample size at which the account weights stop being informative, derived from the per-observation advantage, and printed beside the actual amount of evidence — because the two are cheap to compute and they are what tells a reader whether the hedge means anything. Adopt also the honest multi-number comparison: their own tables show one prior winning at small true size, another winning at large, so the right form for a prior claim is a formula with a stated crossover rather than a direction. The warning is sharper: **a sample-size threshold certified by one criterion can sit far below the sample size the same paper's own risk bound demands** — 40 against 74 here — and both are internally correct, because one is about *selecting* the true account and the other about *committing* to it at a stated error tolerance, and a reader who quotes the smaller number has silently switched questions. For our white-box model this is direct: the number of probes that reliably identifies which account is right is not the number that justifies acting on it, and every threshold we publish must name which of the two it is.
