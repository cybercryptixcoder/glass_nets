# Why LoRA Fails to Forget: Regularized Low-Rank Adaptation Against Backdoors in Language Models

- **Key:** arxiv-2601.06305
- **Authors / organisation:** Hoang-Chau Luong, Lingwei Chen — Golisano College of Computing and Information Sciences, Rochester Institute of Technology (Rochester, NY); both with @rit.edu addresses.
- **Date:** 2026-01-09
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.06305
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
A model poisoned during pretraining carries a trigger-to-target association in its weights, and the hope is that ordinary downstream fine-tuning on clean data washes it out. Full fine-tuning does this reasonably often; LoRA, the standard cheap alternative, mostly does not, and the field's explanation has been the obvious one — a rank-limited update has less capacity than a full-weight one, so it cannot overwrite as much. The authors think that explanation is wrong in an instructive way: rank is not the binding constraint, and the actual constraint lives in the spectrum of the update relative to the spectrum of the poisoned weights. Their aim is double: a mechanism for when a low-rank update can suppress a trigger at all, and a modified LoRA that reaches the regime the mechanism says is required. It is a defence paper, but its argument is a weight-level one about *which directions* an update must out-shout.

## What they studied
BERT-large, RoBERTa-large and LLaMA, each poisoned during pretraining under a weight-poisoning threat model (1,500 clean-label poisoned samples, target label 0, proxy dataset chosen to match the downstream label distribution: IMDB→SST-2, MR→CR, SST-2→CoLA), with every poisoned model required to show attack success above 95% before any defence is tried. Two attacks: BadNet with the rare token "mn", and InSent with a fixed natural-language sentence. The unit of analysis is the weight matrix and its spectrum — the pretrained matrix W and the LoRA update ΔW = BA, each decomposed by SVD, compared through their maximum singular values (spectral strength) and through the cosine similarity between the update's leading singular vector and the pretrained matrix's top singular vectors (spectral alignment). Success is measured at the behaviour level by clean accuracy and attack success rate.

## How they did it
First a descriptive measurement: for each layer, take the ratio of the largest singular value of W to that of the inferred ΔW, and the maximum cosine between the update's top singular vector and the top-32 singular vectors of W. Both are plotted layer by layer, and the first is backed by a table of what the poisoned models achieve on the downstream task *without* any fine-tuning at all, to establish how much of the task the frozen weights already solve. Second, a linearised argument. Write the triggered logits as the frozen model's contribution plus s·ΔW applied to the triggered input, split the input into backdoor-aligned and clean-aligned pieces, bound each piece with two assumptions (the pretrained model is effectively backdoored at initialisation; the update has some clean margin and some bounded trigger margin), and solve for the scale s at which the clean label overtakes the target on a triggered input: a threshold that shrinks as the alignment gap (clean alignment minus trigger alignment) grows and as the update's spectral norm grows. Third, three interventions designed to move the two quantities separately: dropout on the frozen weights during fine-tuning so the adapter must carry clean information itself; a soft orthogonality penalty pushing BA away from the column and row spaces of W; and a post-training rescale of the update per layer to match the pretrained layer's largest singular value. Fourth, evaluation of the combined method against full fine-tuning, four LoRA variants and three inference-time defences, plus ablations of the three components, the sweep over the rescaling factor, the regularisation strength, the dropout rate, the rank and the training-time scaling.

## What counts as an explanation here
A mechanism, stated as a weight-level condition with a threshold: forgetting happens when the update's spectral strength and its alignment gap are large enough that the scaled update dominates the trigger-induced logit margin. It is partial by construction — the authors say outright that they pursue the "make the clean task win" route and not "detect and cancel the poisoned direction", so the account explains *task dominance* and not trigger removal. The explanation predicts two comparative claims that the experiments then test: scaling the update must show a threshold effect (attack success flat, then a sharp fall), and improving alignment must lower the scale needed. It does not predict where in the weights the trigger lives, and the spectral picture of the poisoned model itself is not decomposed.

## How they judged success
Clean accuracy on the downstream test set and attack success rate on the poisoned test set, both for the six model-dataset pairs; a derived robustness margin defined as clean accuracy minus attack success rate; per-iteration training time as the cost control; component ablations (clean-strengthening alone, trigger-insensitivity alone, all three, each against the LoRA baseline); sweeps over regularisation coefficient, dropout rate, rank and training-time scaling; and a case study with two sentences. Baselines are full fine-tuning, LoRA, PiSSA, DoRA, OLoRA, Back-Translation, SCPD and ONION. The authors are explicit that the FFT, LoRA, Back-Translation, SCPD and ONION rows are copied from a prior paper's table, that PSIM is excluded because it filters inputs before inference, and that their own numbers are best-of-several-runs rather than means.

## What they found
Standard LoRA does not forget: under BadNet it reaches 99.70 attack success on BERT/SST-2, 99.74 on RoBERTa/SST-2 and 100.0 on LLaMA/SST-2 and LLaMA/CR, and its robustness margin is negative in most cells. The three LoRA variants help unevenly and not reliably (PiSSA 42.57 on BERT/BadNet/SST-2 against OLoRA's 35.09 and DoRA's 56.11), while full fine-tuning is better but far from clean (77.63 on the same cell). The measurement supports the premise that rank is not the story: the update's top singular value sits well below the pretrained layer's at every layer, and the leading-direction cosine is small but distinctly nonzero. The combined method moves attack success into single digits in most cells — 7.48 on BERT/BadNet/SST-2 against LoRA's 99.70, 2.70 on BERT/BadNet/CR, 2.31 on LLaMA/BadNet/SST-2, 6.49 on RoBERTa/BadNet/SST-2 — with clean accuracy generally within a point or two of the baseline. The rescaling sweep shows the threshold shape the argument predicts for plain LoRA: attack success stays near maximum until the scale is large, then falls sharply, and clean accuracy degrades at the far end, so strength alone is insufficient. The ablation is the most informative table: the orthogonality term alone barely works under InSent (attack success 93.84 on RoBERTa, 98.35 on LLaMA), the dropout term alone is the strong one (10.34 and 22.99 under BadNet), and only the combination gets everything low. Cost is close to free: +6 ms per iteration on BERT and LLaMA, +15 ms on RoBERTa. One clean cell goes the other way and is not discussed: RoBERTa/BadNet/SST-2 under full fine-tuning reports 77.63 attack success with 15.43 clean accuracy, and InSent on CoLA is the systematic soft spot — RoRA lands at 60.06 attack success on RoBERTa and 30.24 on LLaMA where other cells are single-digit.

## Limits and open problems they name
Adaptive and more recent attacks are not tested; the models are moderate-scale (their own wording leaves GPT-3-and-beyond scale open); and the theory is spectral and linearised, with the authors naming the extension to nonlinear settings as open. To these the paper's own setup adds several the authors do not state: the trigger is always a single target class, the thoroughness of a defence is measured only by attack success and clean accuracy, no random-subspace or rank-matched control is run for the orthogonality penalty, and the spectral measurements that motivate everything are figures whose values are not tabulated.

## What we take from it
Three things, in order of durability. (1) *A weight-level condition with a threshold, and the reporting format that makes it credible*: strength (a spectral-norm ratio against the layer it edits), alignment (an overlap with the subspace you want to leave alone), and the sweep that shows the threshold rather than the one chosen operating point — the same three-column discipline the folder's spectral entry arrived at from a detector, now from an intervention, and now with the layerwise curve rather than a single cell. (2) *The orthogonality penalty is a directional intervention on weights and it fails alone*, which is a warning with teeth for us: pushing an update off a subspace is the cheapest weight-space edit available and the ablation shows it does almost nothing unless something else makes the update carry the task. If our own steering or editing write-ups ever report "we removed the component's projection onto X and behaviour changed," the analogue of this table is the ablation that removes the projection without the compensating term. (3) *The inherited-accuracy observation as a confound for any fine-tuning experiment*: the poisoned frozen models already score 75.84 to 86.19 on the downstream test sets before any adaptation, so a downstream score is not evidence that the adaptation did the work, and a forgetting result is partly a statement about how little there was to change. Method warning: figures as the only source for a motivating measurement (here the per-layer spectral ratio) means a reader cannot check the premise — our tables should carry the numbers the argument rests on.
