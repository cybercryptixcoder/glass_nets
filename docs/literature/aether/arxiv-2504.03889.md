# Using Attention Sinks to Identify and Evaluate Dormant Heads in Pretrained LLMs

- **Key:** arxiv-2504.03889
- **Authors / organisation:** Pedro Sandoval-Segura, Xijun Wang, Ashwinee Panda, Micah Goldblum, Ronen Basri, Tom Goldstein, David Jacobs; University of Maryland, with Columbia University and the Weizmann Institute of Science
- **Date:** 2025-04-04
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.03889
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (v1 HTML, main body plus Appendix A.1-A.11; figures read from captions and reporting prose, not inspected as images)
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central: the folder's cleanest case where a cheap per-input statistic is defined by *what a head emits* rather than by *where it attends*, and the two definitions are then compared by the only test that matters here - intervention - with the simpler one failing on a model whose sink is not at position zero; it is also the strongest evidence gathered so far that "unimportant under ablation" and "removable in this context" are the same measurement made twice, since the identified set differs with every input sequence)

## What they wanted, and why

Attention sinks - positions that absorb most of a head's attention despite carrying almost no information - are now ubiquitous in pretrained language models, and the obvious reading is that heads dominated by a sink are doing nothing. This paper asks whether that reading is true, and in asking it makes a definitional argument: existing operationalisations of a "dormant" head look at the attention matrix (how much weight goes to the first token), which bakes in an assumption about *where* the sink sits and ignores the value vectors that decide what the head actually writes into the residual stream. The larger goal is an evaluation discipline rather than an efficiency trick: a definition of unimportance that is meant to be checked by zeroing the named heads and watching benchmark accuracy, and that behaves consistently across model families and datasets without retuning. The authors are explicit that inference speed-up is a possible downstream use, not the aim - the four questions posed are how to define a dormant head, when it appears in pretraining, how it changes, and which inputs provoke it.

## What they studied

Six pretrained base models from three families: Llama-2-7B, Llama-3.1-8B and Llama-3.2-3B; OLMo-2-1124-7B; and Qwen2.5-7B and Qwen2.5-14B - between 672 and 1,920 attention heads each, 28 to 48 layers deep. Evaluation uses four zero-shot multiple-choice benchmarks (MMLU, ARC-Challenge, HellaSwag, WinoGrande) plus GSM8K at five shots as an open-ended generation check, all through lm-evaluation-harness. The unit of analysis is the attention head, and - the point the paper turns on - the *head per input sequence*, since a head can be dormant on one question and active on the next. Secondary units are per-layer dormancy rates, per-head dormancy counts over the 14,042-question MMLU test split, individual checkpoint checkpoints from OLMo-2's released pretraining run, and 10,000 FineWeb-Edu text samples truncated to 500 tokens for the input-characteristics study.

## How they did it

Two definitions are set against each other. The prior definition ("First Token") declares a head dormant when its average attention weight to position zero exceeds a threshold. The proposed one, HONOR, computes each head's output per token as the usual convex combination of value vectors, takes the average L2 norm of those outputs across the sequence, and normalises it by the mean of the same quantity over all heads *in that layer* - a head is dormant when this layer-relative ratio falls below a threshold. The normalisation is what makes one threshold transfer across layers whose output scales differ. The whole forward pass is then instrumented: for every input sequence the mask is computed online, and the outputs of the masked heads are set to zero in place of the head's write to the residual stream, which is mathematically the same as pruning those heads for that forward pass. Thresholds are swept rather than chosen - 14 values in [0.124, 0.7] for HONOR, 20 in [0.5, 0.95] for First Token - so each method produces a whole trade-off curve of fraction-of-heads-zeroed against accuracy, and the headline comparison is where each curve crosses the intact model's accuracy, or 0.5% and 1% below it, obtained by linear interpolation where a curve never reaches the baseline. A uniform-random control zeroes the same fraction of heads per input. Supports follow: agreement between the two masks at matched sparsity (precision and intersection-over-union, using HONOR as ground truth); a replacement test where selected heads' outputs are swapped for Gaussian noise matched to the head's own mean and standard deviation, so that "zeroing works" is not confused with "zeroing happens to be the right shape of perturbation"; and a first-token value-vector intervention over a sliding window of five consecutive layers, which removes any ability to read information from position zero at each depth band. For pretraining dynamics, 109 of OLMo-2's 928 stage-1 checkpoints (100 evenly spaced from 1B to 4T tokens plus 9 extra early ones) are scored with a fixed HONOR threshold and each head's dormancy frequency is tracked across checkpoints individually. For input effects, six candidate text statistics are regressed against per-sample dormant percentage. Finally, dormancy counts per head are used as a fingerprint to compare model families - including Llama-3.2-3B, which Meta built by structured pruning from Llama-3.1-8B.

## What counts as an explanation here

A one-line rule with a single scalar threshold that is meant to identify, per input, a set of heads whose removal is behaviourally invisible - i.e. a *predictive* statement about an intervention rather than a description of what a head computes. The account is deliberately thin and the authors say so: the rule names no mechanism, and dormancy is characterised extrinsically (the head's write is small relative to its layer) rather than intrinsically (this head computes the sink). It is complete only in the sense that every head gets a label on every input; it predicts nothing about outputs, only that setting the label to zero's heads to zero leaves the output close to unchanged. The paper's other explanations are descriptive statistics - when dormancy arises, which text features correlate with it - and are explicitly correlational.

## How they judged success

Four multiple-choice accuracies averaged, compared against the intact model's own average and against a per-input uniform-random ablation of the same size, across a threshold sweep; separately, GSM8K exact match at five shots. Threshold invariance - whether *one* threshold array works across six model-dataset pairs without retuning - is treated as a first-class criterion rather than a nuisance parameter. Agreement with the rival definition at matched sparsity via average precision and mean intersection-over-union, with HONOR taken as the reference label. An internal consistency check on the intervention itself: per-layer dormancy rates re-measured *inside* the modified forward pass to verify that zeroing heads in early layers does not change the dormancy measured in later ones. For the emergence study, dormancy frequency per checkpoint over training tokens. For input effects, R² from univariate linear regressions on 10,000 samples, with significance reported. Notably absent: any variance or confidence interval on the accuracy numbers, any repetition under different seeds (the models are not trained, so this is cheap to argue and still untested), and any held-out split of the benchmark questions between the threshold sweep and the reported crossing points.

## What they found

HONOR dominates the attention-weight rule on the axis the authors care about. On the four MC tasks, five of six models allow more than 4% of heads to be zeroed at *no* average accuracy loss (Llama-3.1-8B 12.4%, Llama-3.2-3B 12.1%, OLMo-2 8.6%, Llama-2 5.9%, Qwen2.5-14B 7.1%, Qwen2.5-7B 1.0%), and all six allow more than 14% at a 1% budget - with First Token losing on nearly every cell (0.0% at zero loss for four of the six models; the exceptions are Qwen2.5-14B at 7.1/0.0 and Llama-3.2-3B at 0.0/12.2). The single sharpest failure is OLMo-2-1124-7B, whose sink sits at an intermediate position and which shares attention between two sinks: First Token identifies essentially nothing there at any threshold in the swept range, including on GSM8K (0.0% across all three budgets), while HONOR reaches 1.8% / 14.5% / 16.0% on the same task. GSM8K shows the asymmetry most cleanly - Llama-3.1-8B tolerates nearly 27% of heads zeroed within 1% accuracy, Qwen2.5-7B about 20%, and the open-ended task is not systematically harder for the criterion. Threshold stability is the second result: at the same τ, First Token's identified fraction ranges from 4.2% to 11.2% across two models on ARC-Challenge, whereas HONOR at τ = 0.3 gives 1.3% and 3.2%; a single τ = 0.478 is reported as best across all settings. At matched sparsity the two masks are highly *contained* but not similar - First Token's average precision against HONOR is 0.678 on MMLU and 0.645 on HellaSwag, yet mean intersection-over-union stays under 0.50 on every benchmark, so the simpler rule recovers a majority of what HONOR calls dormant while adding a comparable set of heads HONOR does not, which is the paper's own reading of "measuring only the sink underestimates prevalence". Per-head counts over MMLU confirm the sparsity is context-dependent: almost no head is dormant for all 14,042 questions (12 in Qwen2.5-14B, 0-1 elsewhere) but dozens are dormant for 95% of them (46 of Llama-3.1-8B's 1,024 heads, 36 of Qwen2.5-14B's 1,920), and overall 64-82% of heads are dormant for at most 3% of questions. Dormancy is not a deep-layer phenomenon and its geometry differs completely between models of identical shape (Llama-2, Llama-3.1-8B and OLMo-2 all 32×32, all different patterns) - except Llama-3.2-3B, whose dormancy map looks like a degraded copy of Llama-3.1-8B's, consistent with structured pruning from it, which the authors offer as evidence that dormancy counts act as a lineage signature. In pretraining, dormancy appears early: under 0.1% of heads before 5B tokens, a spike past the final model's rate around 55B tokens, then a slow decline to 16.5% at the end - and individual heads flip in both directions, e.g. layer 15 head 13 goes dormant early then returns to active around 2T tokens, which the authors note is hard to reconcile with a mutual-reinforcement mechanism that would make dormancy sticky. Input text explains a third of the variance in dormant percentage: alphabet density 29.5%, English-language score 18.3%, perplexity 4.3%, and a combined density-plus-0.3×language-score metric 35.8%, all positively correlated at p < 0.01; prose and conversation make many heads dormant, code, formulas, citations and links make few. Two annexes bound the claim from underneath: replacing dormant outputs with matched Gaussian noise also preserves accuracy but for a *smaller* fraction (over 11% of Llama-3.2-3B's heads at the 0.5% budget, against more than the zeroing budget), and zeroing the first token's value vector in early layers damages every model, so the sink position itself carries real information at shallow depth and is redundant only later.

## Limits and open problems they name

The authors concede that the rule needs each head's output computed, so it cannot be used as a static pruning criterion, and that heads are re-selected per input, which rules out a fixed pruned model; efficiency is future work, and the paper did no training of its own. They also flag that the linear regressions over text characteristics leave most of the variance unexplained and that whether any single measure could predict dormancy in something as complex as a transformer is unresolved. Limits visible in the numbers but not stated: the accuracy table has no seed variation, no confidence intervals and no question-level train/test split separating the threshold sweep from the reported crossing; the "zeroed at no loss" cells rest on the *average* over four benchmarks, so a small gain in one benchmark can pay for a small loss in another and per-benchmark harm is only in an appendix figure; the Gaussian-replacement arm is run on one model only, so the claim that zeroing is special rests on a single 0.5% budget comparison; "inactive" is defined relative to *one layer's* mean output norm, so a layer in which every head is large or every head is small yields no dormant heads by construction; and the pretraining analysis uses a threshold fixed at the final model's scale applied retrospectively to early checkpoints.

## What we take from it

A **method and a warning** in equal parts. The method is the layer-relative output-norm statistic: it is cheap, requires only a forward pass, needs no training, no labels and no probe, and is defined on the quantity that actually enters the residual stream rather than on the attention pattern that a reader likes to look at - which makes it directly reusable as a "does this head write anything on this input" gate in front of any more expensive probe we run on a white-box model. The comparison design is the reusable part: two plausible definitions of the same concept, matched on the quantity they select (fraction of units), scored by the same intervention, over a sweep rather than a point, with the losing definition's failure traced to a *specific model property* (the sink's position) rather than dismissed as noise. The warning is the one this folder keeps rediscovering and this paper states in its cleanest form: the dormant set is chosen **per input**, so "this head is not part of the mechanism" is not a property of the head at all in this framework - the head has a dormancy *frequency* over inputs (64-82% of heads are dormant on fewer than 3% of questions while 46 are dormant on 95%), and a single-corpus ablation average silently reports the frequency-weighted compromise. The 3.2B-vs-8B fingerprint is a bonus we should keep: if dormancy counts transfer through structured pruning, they are tracking something about the architecture's lineage and not only about the task, which is a cheap check on any per-head structural statistic we publish.
