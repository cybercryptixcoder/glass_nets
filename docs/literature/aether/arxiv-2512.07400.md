- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful) — it is the folder's first entry where a *head-side* statistic and a *feature-side* statistic of the same network are swept against the **same** controlled knob (buffer size), and where the theory predicts which of the two should move first. The prediction is that features saturate almost immediately while the readout misaligns until the buffer is large; that is a testable, signed claim about a two-part decomposition, which is exactly the shape our white-box model needs.

## What they wanted, and why
Continual learning is known to leave networks that can still be linearly decoded for old tasks even when their own predictions on those tasks have collapsed — a gap researchers have documented with linear probes but not explained. The authors want a geometric account of why experience replay fixes the two things at such different costs: a tiny buffer preserves the feature geometry, but classifier accuracy keeps degrading until the buffer approaches the full dataset. Their larger goal is a predictive theory of feature-space forgetting in which the answer for "how much replay do I need" is derived from measurable representation statistics rather than tuned. Neural Collapse (NC) supplies the vocabulary: at convergence features collapse onto class means whose centered versions form a simplex ETF and align with the classifier rows.

## What they studied
ResNet18 trained from scratch, ResNet50 and a ViT-Base pretrained on ImageNet, on Cifar100, Tiny-ImageNet and CUB200, each split into ten tasks; the three continual-learning regimes of van de Ven et al. (task-, class- and domain-incremental). Baselines in the appendix extend to DER, FDR and iCaRL. The unit of analysis is the penultimate feature map together with the linear head: per-class feature means and covariances, the subspace they span (the "active subspace"), the centred-mean norm, the covariance ranks, and the ETF/duality statistics of NC1–NC3. Buffer size is the independent variable, swept over ten settings (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10 and 100 percent of each task's dataset), with weight decay swept as a second axis. Three seeds per configuration.

## How they did it
Two forgetting measures are computed for every past task: shallow forgetting, the drop in output accuracy, and deep forgetting, the drop in accuracy of a logistic-regression probe retrained on frozen features. They are plotted against buffer size, which produces the paper's headline asymmetry. To explain it, the authors first confirm that NC emerges during sequential training (metrics sampled every 100 steps), then define an input as out-of-distribution with respect to a checkpoint if its mean representation is orthogonal to the span of the training class means (their Definition 1, following the ID/OOD orthogonality property of NECO). Past-task classes are then treated as OOD inputs, and the terminal-phase dynamics of such inputs are derived: once class means align with the classifier rows, gradient updates are confined to the active subspace, so the component of an old class's mean outside it is frozen, or decays geometrically under weight decay. Replay is modelled as a mixture between the OOD distribution and the fully-collapsed NC distribution, with a mixing coefficient that grows with buffer size. The classifier half of the story is checked with a synthetic LDA counterfactual in which population means and covariance are replaced, one at a time, by their buffer estimates, isolating rank deficiency from mean-norm inflation.

## What counts as an explanation here
A causal abstraction of forgetting in two layers — a mixture model with an explicit mixing coefficient and an asymptotic theorem about linear separability — plus a mechanism for the head-side failure (a cost function that is invariant along directions the buffer does not span, hence a manifold of equally-low-loss heads). It is a surrogate for behaviour in the terminal phase: it predicts *orders of magnitude and limiting behaviour* of the separability statistic, not accuracy. The theory is deliberately asymptotic, so it explains trends rather than values. It predicts, and the paper treats this as the central prediction, that any non-zero buffer fraction keeps separability from vanishing, while classifier alignment has no such guarantee.

## How they judged success
Linear separability is proxied by a signal-to-noise ratio, squared inter-class mean distance over the trace of summed within-class covariances, which they prove lower-bounds the population Mahalanobis distance. The theorems are Big-Theta statements in the buffer mixing coefficient, the weight-decay decay factor and the centred-mean norm. Empirically, success is the *shape* of the deep-versus-shallow curves against buffer size across three datasets, three regimes, four methods and two architectures, plus ablation figures: pretraining convergence speed, a d≪K bottleneck, and a norm-matching head initialisation. No single accuracy table for the main claim; the numbers live in scatter plots, and the only printed table with variance is the appendix bottleneck table.

## What they found
The deep–shallow asymmetry is robust: small buffers pin the feature geometry while output accuracy recovers only near full replay, and the gap closes gradually along the whole sweep rather than at a threshold. It is much smaller in task-incremental learning (one head per task) than in class- or domain-incremental settings, and pretrained models show almost no deep forgetting at all. Domain-incremental learning, usually treated as the easy benchmark, exhibits deep forgetting comparable to class-incremental. Skill in the appendix: in a bottleneck where the feature width is ten and there are 25 classes per task, NC1 and NC3 survive but the ETF spread degrades badly — the pairwise-angle dispersion roughly triples against the standard setting — yet the gap persists, so the effect does not depend on the simplex being exact. Table 4 in that regime prints shallow 52.27±2.41 against deep 26.15±1.92 for class-incremental, 46.38±1.88 against 35.07±0.51 for domain-incremental and 18.45±2.78 against 14.89±1.77 for task-incremental. The LDA decomposition attributes the head-side loss to two additive sources, rank-deficient buffer covariance and inflated buffer means, and reports that substituting both makes the synthetic classifier worse than the network it is modelling. The norm-matching ablation confirms the mechanism for growing mean norms — initialising new heads at the norm of the old ones suppresses the growth — but the authors state plainly that this changes final forgetting and accuracy by a negligible amount.

## Limits and open problems they name
The theory is asymptotic and ignores the transient phase where forgetting actually begins. Modelling replay as a mixture between two idealised extremes is an admitted simplification. Cross-task structure in multi-head networks is left as poorly understood. The authors also name the tension in their own conclusion: NC organises features but is itself what makes small buffers statistically blind, so whether collapse is helpful or harmful in continual learning remains open. Beyond what they name: the deep/shallow curves are figures, so every number in the main empirical claim is read off a plot; the ETF-quality claim rests on figure dispersion rather than a table; and the central theoretical object (a probe accuracy) is only bounded through a lower bound on a moment-based SNR, which is not the same quantity the probe measures.

## What we take from it
A framing and a warning. The framing: split a model into features and readout and require any white-box claim to say which of the two it is about, because here the same perturbation leaves one almost untouched and destroys the other, and the two respond at different rates to the same knob. The warning: the authors' own ablation is a clean case of a mechanism confirmed and a behaviour unmoved — they show the norm-growth cause exists and then show that removing it does not help the metric they care about. For our predictive model, the reusable move is the counterfactual decomposition (replace one population statistic at a time with its sample estimate, and see which replacement reproduces the failure), and the reusable measure is the projection of an out-of-support class mean onto the active subspace, which is computable from weights and a little data and is the cheapest readout-side reliability statistic in the folder. Also useful: their probe-vs-head comparison is the natural control for any claim we make that a representation "still knows" something the output layer does not.
