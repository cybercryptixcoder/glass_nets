# The Cognitive Revolution in Interpretability: From Explaining Behavior to Interpreting Representations and Algorithms

- **Key:** arxiv-2408.05859
- **Authors / organisation:** Adam Davies (Department of Computer Science, University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign); Ashkan Khakzar (Department of Engineering Science, University of Oxford). Two authors, positions from the printed affiliation and e-mail block.
- **Date:** 2024-08-11 (v1, single version)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.05859
- **Kind:** survey/agenda
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
The authors argue that deep-learning interpretation has been living through a behaviourist phase: the dominant products are post-hoc explanations of *why this output for this input* (saliency, feature attribution, counterfactuals), which deliberately say nothing about internal state. Mechanistic interpretability is the beginning of a different project — reading latent representations and the operations over them — and they read it as the analogue of the cognitive revolution that moved psychology off stimulus-and-response and onto mental representations and processing. Their aim is taxonomic rather than empirical: split mechanistic work into two categories with different objects of study, inventory the assumptions and failure modes each one carries, and ask whether a single framework could cover both. The larger goal is to stop work that is studying different things from being compared as if it were competing work on the same thing, and to borrow the conceptual vocabulary cognitive science already built for the same questions.

## What they studied
No models are trained or measured; the unit of analysis is the *literature itself*, organised by what each family of methods claims to be describing. Twenty-odd method families spread over four bodies of work: neuron-level optimisation-and-search (network dissection of human-annotated concepts, maximal-activation input synthesis); structural probing, linear and nonlinear, with CAV-style concept directions; causal probing in the amnesic-probing/INLP lineage and its adversarial, arbitrarily-expressive descendants; dictionary learning via sparse autoencoders in the Anthropic monosemanticity lineage; and circuit discovery over knockout and patching, up to end-to-end circuits such as induction. They fix the frame with Marr's three levels — computational theory, representation and algorithm, implementation — and observe that pre-existing applications of Marr to ML describe the *training process* (task, optimiser, hardware), which leaves the pre-trained network itself unanalysed at the middle level until interpretation methods exist to populate it.

## How they did it
A conceptual survey with a borrowed structural template, not an experiment. Step one is the historical analogy, laid out as a claim with its consequences: behaviourism excluded internal states and stalled on language acquisition, concept learning and working memory, and the fields that succeeded it were organised around representations and processing; contemporary deep-learning interpretation inherited the same exclusion, and mechanistic interpretability is the break with it. Step two is the taxonomy, mirroring a distinction they take from computational neuroscience: **semantic interpretation** asks what latent properties are learned and used, **algorithmic interpretation** asks what operations are performed over them and what algorithm those operations compose into. Step three is a parallel audit — for each category, a framework statement, then the assumptions it cannot do without, then the challenges those assumptions generate, joined where the two categories turn out to hit the same obstacle. Step four is the unification section, reviewing causal-abstraction formalisms in which representations are nodes of a graph and operations over them are edges, and faithfulness is the instance-level agreement between network and graph under interchange interventions, with concept-level and rotation-based variants. They also explicitly park interpretable-ML definitions of interpretability (inherent transparency versus post-hoc explanation) in an appendix, on the grounds that those categories predate foundation models and do not classify what probing or circuit discovery do.

## What counts as an explanation here
The paper's subject matter *is* the answer to this question, and it gives three answers side by side. Behaviourist explanation: a statement about which input features or input changes are responsible for an output — complete for that output, silent about internals. Semantic interpretation: an account of which latent properties are represented and how strongly they are used, in the form of a feature dictionary, a probe, or an identified subspace. Algorithmic interpretation: a sub-graph of weights understood as performing a named operation, composed into an end-to-end algorithm. Only the last two are what the authors call the cognitive project, and only the unification framework claims to be *predictive* — a causal model that, given an interchangeable variable, generates the network's response to the corresponding intervention. They are careful that the two categories are complementary, not rival, and that "faithful" is a relation to a specified behaviour, not a property a circuit has in the abstract.

## How they judged success
No metrics; the paper's evaluative instrument is the audit itself, and it judges methods by whether the assumptions they need are licensed. Four assumptions recur across the taxonomy and each is checked against a known counter-result rather than a benchmark: (1) the **unit assumption** — that neurons are one-to-one with properties, which polysemanticity and superposition already falsify, pushing the field toward feature dictionaries; (2) the **supervision assumption** — probing requires naming the properties in advance, so the most important property may simply never be probed for, which is the stated reason dictionary learning exists; (3) the **correlation-versus-causation gap** — probes establish predictability, not use, which is the stated reason causal probing exists; (4) the **Rashomon problem** — multiple accounts with equal causal efficacy, so no principled choice among them, which they name as a difficulty for both categories and quote the clock-and-pizza style phase-transition work as its sharpest instance. Their read of the current state is that each fix in this chain inherits the previous one's problem one level deeper: causal probing can show that removing information about a property changes behaviour but not that it was *that* property's representation rather than collateral damage (the completeness/selectivity tradeoff), and dictionary learning's causal checks have the same structure.

## What they found
The load-bearing claims are all conceptual. **On the taxonomy**: semantic and algorithmic work are genuinely different projects — what is represented versus what is computed — and the field's habit of treating circuit discovery as the "real" mechanistic interpretability understates dictionary learning and probing, while conversely the habit of treating probe accuracy as an explanation double-counts predictability as use. **On the dictionary route specifically**: they credit SAEs with removing the supervision requirement but argue the cost is a displaced annotation burden — every feature in a new dictionary must be re-interpreted and re-evaluated by human or LLM annotator, which blocks comparison across models, layers and dictionary methods, and in large studies the interpretations and their evaluation are both produced by LLM automation with compounding-error risk. They also note that interpretability fraction is left unquantified: nobody knows what share of millions of features are as clean as the famous Golden Gate Bridge feature. **On circuit discovery**: they observe that, to their knowledge, every circuit found in a real-world LLM required the algorithm to be specified in advance, so discovery is top-down and cannot find an algorithm nobody hypothesised; and that circuit-level interventions cannot rule out other sub-graphs having the same effect, with randomised circuits sometimes matching the effect of the calibrated ones. **On unification**: causal abstraction is the only formalism present that covers both categories, but the empirical versions are limited to toy models and tasks, or intervene at a single layer, so cross-layer algorithmic interpretation of a real model is not yet demonstrated.

## Limits and open problems they name
The unification framework is the acknowledged gap: single-layer intervention in the concept-level variant, toy scale in the instance-level variant, and no demonstration that either describes how a model transforms representations across depth. Beyond that, the audit leaves several named obstacles unresolved — the Rashomon problem has no proposed selection criterion in either category; there is no accepted procedure for choosing which neurons or heads deserve analysis, so targeting is heuristic and cannot even be probabilistically bounded; pipeline-hypothesis readings of probe accuracy across layers remain contested, and probe expression level trades expressivity against memorisation with no settled resolution; and automated LLM interpretation-and-evaluation may be circular because the evaluator can be the same kind of model as the object of study. The paper's own limits are those of a survey: it proposes no new measurement, and its historical analogy is offered as a framing device whose predictive value it does not test.

## What we take from it
(1) **The taxonomy is directly usable as the specification for our project, and it names exactly what our white-box model must contain to count.** A predictive white-box model is not a bigger dictionary and not a bigger circuit — it is a *causal graph whose nodes are read-out quantities and whose edges are operations*, scored by whether it generates the right response to an intervention. That is the unification section's promise, and this paper is the clearest statement in the folder that semantic interpretation alone (dictionary, probes) and algorithmic interpretation alone (circuits) each answer half the question. Our entry should state which half each battery supplies and where the two are joined. (2) **The completeness/selectivity tradeoff is the vocabulary our intervention batteries have been missing.** Runs 495-506 kept demanding randomised comparators, wrong-target controls and retained-set reporting; the precise statement is that any intervention which removes or modifies a property is simultaneously scored on *completeness* (did it remove all of it) and *selectivity* (did it leave the rest alone), and that more expressive interventions buy completeness at the cost of selectivity. Every steering or ablation result we publish should report both numbers, because a large effect with unknown selectivity is indistinguishable from collateral damage. (3) **The supervision assumption is the strongest argument for why our probes must be paired with an unsupervised route and vice versa.** A probe can only find the property you named; a dictionary finds what it finds but needs interpretation and per-dictionary re-evaluation that blocks cross-model comparison. Our batteries should therefore never rest on one route alone, and should report what the unsupervised route found that the supervised one did not name — that gap is the measurement of the supervision cost rather than a rhetorical caveat. (4) **Rashomon is the failure mode a predictive white-box model is specifically designed to defeat, and it sets our acceptance test.** If two accounts predict the same interventions equally well, the discipline the folder already follows (score under intervention, not under description) does not distinguish them — so the predictive model must be evaluated on interventions it was *not* fitted on, and where two accounts tie we must report the tie rather than picking the one with the nicer story. A warning from the same section: intervention effects that random circuits can reproduce mean that an ablation with no structural comparator proves nothing.
