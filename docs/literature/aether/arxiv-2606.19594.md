# Unsupervised Causal Abstractions Discovery

- **Key:** arxiv-2606.19594
- **Authors / organisation:** Théo Saulus, Simon Lacoste-Julien, Dhanya Sridhar; Mila - Quebec AI Institute / Université de Montréal (two authors also Canada CIFAR AI Chairs)
- **Date:** 2026-06-17
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19594
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Causal abstraction is normally a test: a person proposes a small high-level causal model and checks whether the large low-level system (often a neural network) implements it. The authors point out the obvious cost of that arrangement — enumerating candidate high-level models is expensive, and it is impossible for systems nobody understands well, such as a network trained to predict protein folding. They therefore ask the complementary question: can the high-level model be *learned* from low-level measurements alone, with no expert candidate to test? The motivation is explicitly our own — if the high-level model is right, interventions on its variables can be translated into interventions on units and used to steer behaviour. The paper's answer is a theory-first one: learn it only under a low-rank structural assumption, and then say exactly how much of it is identified.

## What they studied
The low-level system is an SCM over observed variables whose causal graph has low Boolean rank: the adjacency matrix factors as A = Q ⋄ R^T over m latent factors, i.e. a *factor DAG* (f-DAG) with observed variables on one side, latent factors on the other, and edges only variable→factor or factor→variable. The high-level model is the SCM over the factors you get by collapsing these two hops. Unit of analysis is the latent factor and its mechanism, not a neuron. No language model appears; validation is on synthetic f-SCMs with continuous variables (16/64/128 variables, 3/6/8 factors) and on one small MLP with 96 hidden units trained to decide divisibility by 6.

## How they did it
The paper is organised as three theoretical moves plus an implementation. (1) Given the true f-SCM, they construct a state map that recomputes each factor from its observed parents, and an intervention map that sends a hard intervention on low-level variables to a *parametric* intervention on the factors downstream of them — one that swaps the intervened variables' mechanisms inside the factor's own mechanism rather than overwriting it. Theorem 1 shows the collapsed factor model is a uniform transformation of the collapsed variable model under these maps, proved by induction root-to-leaf, and the proof leans on the two models sharing their context. (2) For identifiability of the graph they add an *anchor* assumption: every factor has at least one observed variable whose only child is that factor (in-anchor) and one whose only parent is that factor (out-anchor). Under anchors, anchors are exactly the minimal rows and minimal columns of the adjacency matrix, so Q and R can be read off A (Theorem 2: the Boolean factorization is unique up to a permutation of factors). They note the assumption can be weakened to r-disjunctness, giving a reading lemma but not a full identifiability theorem. (3) For the mechanisms: without assumptions the factor values are identified only up to an arbitrary bijection (any reparameterization is invisible at the low level). If the parent mechanisms are affine, the bijection is forced to be affine (Theorem 3); a similarly stated result holds for affine child mechanisms. The practical objective (UCAD) maximises a differentiable interventional log-likelihood over a Gumbel-softmax-sampled factor graph, subject to three constraints: acyclicity (layered ordering or spectral radius on the much smaller factor graph), the anchor condition (threshold τ = 0.99), and L1 sparsity, all handled with an augmented-Lagrangian solver. They also define an approximate-transformation error and prove it is linearly bounded by the training objective when the learned mechanisms are Lipschitz — so a lower loss is a certificate of closer abstraction, not just a better fit.

## What counts as an explanation here
A high-level SCM over a small set of latent factors, equipped with an explicit state map and intervention map, that is provably a (uniform, or approximately uniform) transformation of the low-level model. It is a causal abstraction rather than a description: the claim is about interventional behaviour, not correlation. It is meant to be as complete as the low-rank assumption allows — the factors are the *only* high-level variables — and it makes predictions (the collapsed high-level mechanisms) that can in principle be compared against interventions. The paper is honest that "identified" means recovered up to the symmetry class the theorems leave open, and the class widens sharply when the mechanisms are nonlinear.

## How they judged success
On synthetics: mean correlation coefficient between learned and ground-truth factors, with three variants matched to what each theorem permits — Pearson (affine-invariant), Spearman (monotone), and RDC (nonlinear). They also report R²-linear and R²-MLP decodability and the DCI disentanglement/completeness/informativeness triple, plus constraint violation and validation NLL; model selection is by lowest validation NLL *among runs that reached constraint feasibility*, which matters because the anchor constraint can simply be violated. Controls: each setting is averaged over 5 randomly generated DAGs, with hyperparameter and seed sweeps (60–80 configs); the factor scores are contrasted with MCC computed after random orthogonal rotations of both ground-truth and learned factors, to check the alignment is not an artefact of correlation alone; one ablation trains on observational data only, and another tests on unseen interventional regimes. On the MLP: the learned factors are matched against hand-named positive concepts (divisibility by 2, by 3, by 6, digit sum) *and* against negative controls (divisibility by 5, 7, 11) that should be irrelevant to the task.

## What they found
Synthetic recovery is solid but not clean: over the linear or partly linear settings the mean MCC-Pearson is 0.83 averaged across 13 experiment groups, and per-setting cells run from 0.73 to 0.93 — for example 0.88 with 16 variables/3 factors under noise, and 0.93 with 64 variables/6 factors. Nonlinearity in both directions is where it degrades: with both mechanisms nonlinear, MCC-Pearson falls to 0.64 and 0.62 (16 and 64 variables) while the transformation-invariant MCC-RDC stays at 0.70 and 0.79. The authors flag an unresolved inconsistency — R²-MLP and the DCI scores, which should also be insensitive to nonlinear reparameterization, do not follow the RDC trend, and they say more graphs are needed to understand why. Training on observational data only, or testing on held-out interventional regimes, changes little (MCC-Pearson 0.833 in the observational-only ablation and 0.850 on unseen regimes, both at 64 variables, against 0.810 in the matched interventional setting). On the divisibility MLP the headline contrast is strong: MCC-RDC against positive concepts averages 0.72 while the negative controls average 0.07; one factor correlates 0.80 with divisibility by 2. But the detail is less flattering — divisibility by 3 is recovered at best 0.32 RDC (0.06 Pearson) and the digit sum at 0.16, and the negative control "divisible by 5" is recovered 0.14, about as well as the digit sum. Disentanglement is the weakest reported score throughout (0.76 down to 0.55 across the synthetic settings, 0.51 in the fully nonlinear case). DAG violation is reported as negative, since they target the acyclicity constraint below 1e-6.

## Limits and open problems they name
The theory requires the true number of factors to be known; they say they do not study misspecification. Anchors are an assumption, not a test — the learning objective is *constrained* to produce anchored solutions, so the constraint may be manufacturing the situation it relies on. Identifiability is only up to affine (or, for nonlinear mechanisms, arbitrary bijective) reparameterization, which means the factors are not interpretable in themselves, only through their relation to hypotheses. The one real-system experiment is tiny (96 neurons, one arithmetic task) and the authors concede the factors resist interpretation: they cannot tell a method failure from the network simply using a different strategy. They name scaling to real models as future work, and — importantly for us — note that the actual test of the abstraction should be *steering* experiments, which this paper does not run.

## What we take from it
A framing and a warning. The framing: our white-box model of a black-box network can be cast as an f-SCM whose latents are the abstractions, and if we assume low Boolean rank we inherit a genuine identifiability statement — the graph is recoverable up to factor permutation, the mechanisms up to affine reparameterization. That is the strongest set of guarantees the folder has read for an *unsupervised* decomposition of a network's computation, and it is stricter about what "recovered" means than any SAE entry. The instruments worth copying: (a) the anchor diagnostic — a variable whose only child (or only parent) is the factor — as a cheap structural test of whether a proposed component is distinct from the others, which is the same distinctness question runs 906 and 908 kept raising from the behavioural side; (b) the negative-concept arm, since the paper's own numbers show why it is essential (a control concept reaching 0.14 against a real concept's 0.16 in the same table); (c) the layered acyclicity constraint, which is nearly free for any block-structured network we probe and removes a whole class of impossible high-level solutions. The warnings: "identified" here does not mean "interpretable" — a factor identified up to a bijection is a coordinate system, not a name; and the paper never intervenes on its learned factors, so its abstraction is certified as a *description of the graph and mechanisms*, not as a handle we can steer with. That gap is precisely the one our model has to close.
