# Effect-Level Validation for Causal Discovery in Interactive Telemetry

- **Key:** arxiv-2602.08340
- **Authors / organisation:** Hoang Dang, Luan Pham, Minh Nguyen; Independent Researcher (Vietnam); RMIT University (Australia); Florida Atlantic University (USA)
- **Date:** 2026-02 (v1 9 Feb 2026; v2, camera-ready, 12 Nov 2026)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.08340
- **Kind:** paper (evaluation framework + deployed application), NeurIPS 2026 Evaluations and Datasets Track
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

A product team does not want a causal graph; it wants a defensible answer to one decision question — here, whether playing a game's optional competitive mode early lifts next-day retention. Causal discovery is the usual route, but its benchmarks score a learned structure against a reference structure, and the authors argue that this measures the wrong thing: a graph can rank well on structural distance and still fail to identify the target effect (an orientation through the treatment stays unresolved, or a hidden common cause sits between treatment and outcome), while a structurally distant graph can support exactly the same estimate because the causal pathway is intact. Their goal is an evaluation posture in which a discovered graph is treated as a **hypothesis about structure** and the object being judged is the *effect estimate it licenses*, together with the checks that say how far that estimate can be trusted when no ground-truth graph exists. They frame this as a shift from graph recovery to query reliability under structural uncertainty, motivated by the fact that their own data has no ground truth at all.

## What they studied

One real dataset, not a suite: behavioural telemetry from a shipped Web3 role-playing game over five months, 77,698 users and 26 telemetry-derived features, cut down to 1,376 paying players and a complete-case sample of 1,280 (1,170 after overlap trimming). Six analysis variables: treatment = first PvP exposure inside hours 0–24, outcome = Day-1 retention in hours 24–48, and four pre-treatment covariates (wallet connection, time on the tutorial level, PvE battle count, session count). The unit of analysis is the individual user; the object of inference is a single risk-difference average treatment effect. A second real dataset (a World of Warcraft avatar-history crawl, 36.5M records, 91,064 avatars, three cohort definitions) is used as a replication, and a synthetic known-truth generator matched to the cohort size is used to ask what the checks cannot detect. Nine discovery algorithms across three families are run: constraint-based (PC, FCI, CFCI, FCI-MAX), score-based (BOSS, GRaSP) and hybrids (GFCI, BOSS-FCI, GRaSP-FCI).

## How they did it

The pipeline has five stages and the ordering is the contribution. First, run the discovery algorithms on temporally sliced variables, under a declared set of forbid/require edge directions derived from three evidence sources ordered by how contestable they are (temporal closure, part–whole construction of composite aggregates, and technical preconditions). Second, apply constraints to every candidate graph, and ladder the constraint set across four levels from minimal to full, with a leave-one-constraint-group-out sweep. Third, an **admissibility gate**: keep a graph only if it (i) satisfies the constraints, (ii) admits a valid identification strategy for the target estimand, and (iii) leaves adequate treatment-control overlap; graphs that fail are recorded as identification failures rather than discarded silently, and graphs that return partial ancestral graphs (PAGs) are declared *not evaluated* by the DAG backdoor gate and reported on a separate track. Fourth, estimate the effect on each admissible graph by backdoor adjustment with a doubly robust learner (regression adjustment and inverse-probability weighting as secondary references), with cross-fitting seeds fixed after discovering that an unseeded re-run moved the estimate by up to 1.9 points. Fifth, reliability: compare estimates across algorithms and significance thresholds, then run a placebo treatment permutation, random subsampling at four fractions, and an E-value sensitivity summary. Finally, a known-truth simulation with four regimes (identifiable, latent confounder, orientation ambiguous, null) at the empirical n, 100 replications each, four algorithms and three thresholds, scoring a run as "safe" if within five percentage points of the known effect.

## What counts as an explanation here

Not a description of the model and not a graph: an **effect estimate plus an explicit licence** — the adjustment set that produced it, the constraint set it was produced under, and a verdict from the reliability suite printed as one of trust / caution / reject. Understanding takes the form of a *conditional claim*: this effect holds under this graph and this constraint specification, it is stable in sign under these perturbations, it is sensitive in magnitude to these choices, and these checks can or cannot detect this class of failure. The paper's most unusual explanatory object is a negative one: a named list of what a passing suite does not certify (the absence of unmeasured confounding), demonstrated by a regime in which nearly everything passes and everything is wrong. It is deliberately partial and predictive only in the weak sense — it predicts that the estimate is stable under a named family of perturbations, not what the true effect is.

## How they judged success

Admissibility rate (fraction of the 21 DAG-returning runs that yield an identified estimand) and the spread of the resulting estimates, per constraint level; agreement of estimates across algorithms and across graphs; placebo nullity (a p-value on the permuted-treatment effect); subsample stability of the estimate across 10/50/80/90 per cent draws; E-value as a bound on how strong an unmeasured confounder would have to be to explain the effect away; and, in the known-truth simulation, the share of runs within five points of the true effect, the mean absolute error, and — the key measure — the share of runs that **cleared every check and were still wrong**. Graph-level structural Hamming distance is computed too, but only to test whether it can screen answers, not as a success criterion. Two controls deserve note: the constraint ladder, which makes the licensed estimate visibly a function of the specification, and a claim-to-assumption matrix that states, per claim, the assumption it needs and what its failure would look like.

## What they found

Under the minimal constraint set, the runs that pass the gate pass for the wrong reason: the admissible DAGs simply leave the treatment with no parents, so they report the unadjusted retention difference (0.239) that the descriptive table shows to be heavily confounded. Under the full set all 21 runs are admissible and the estimate is 0.174 on the full sample, regardless of algorithm; on the overlap-trimmed sample the doubly robust estimate is 0.139 with a 95 per cent interval from 0.081 to 0.198 (regression 0.155, weighting 0.146). Cross-algorithm agreement is real but thin: three admissible graphs, pairwise structural Hamming distance 1 to 4, all imply the same single adjustment set (wallet connection), so they agree on one estimand rather than independently recovering an effect. The magnitude is not robust: adding one pre-treatment covariate drives the estimate to 0.075, adding the three parents that the stricter constraint set implies drives it to 0.043, and under all covariates every interval includes zero. Identifiability is *not* monotone in constraint strength (none of 21 runs at the second level, all at the two stronger ones) and does not transfer to the second dataset, where a 24-hour cohort yields no estimand at any level and a 336-hour cohort peaks at an intermediate level. The placebo effect is near zero and the E-value is 1.66, comparable to the association strengths of the observed covariates themselves, so moderate hidden confounding cannot be excluded. Most damaging is the known-truth table: under latent confounding the gate admits 99.6 per cent of runs and 96.7 per cent also clear placebo, subsampling and the E-value together — and every one of those is wrong, by 13.9 points on average; raising the sample size from 500 to 5,000 lifts the share clearing everything from 76 to 98 per cent while the average error stays near 14 points. Pooled across regimes graph distance correlates only moderately with effect error (+0.466) and does not separate safe from unsafe answers, since 51.1 per cent of low-distance runs are still wrong by more than five points.

## Limits and open problems they name

Four, stated plainly: one game and one treatment–outcome pair, with replication only inside game telemetry; the effect is conditional on paying users who were still active at the eligibility landmark, which is a heavily filtered population (roughly a fifth of newly created avatars reach it in the replication); PAG-returning methods are not evaluated under the DAG gate at all, which they call a limit of the rule rather than of those methods; and both the constraint set and the time windows must be re-derived for any other setting. Two further limits are structural rather than incidental and the authors treat them as such: the pipeline abandoned the strictly stricter constraint set entirely because the gate rejects graphs carrying any unresolved edge that lies off the treatment's parent set, so no estimate was produced under it; and no check in the suite can certify the absence of the one failure mode that matters most, unmeasured confounding.

## What we take from it

Three things, one of them a warning that lands directly on our setup. **A measure**: score an account by the *queried consequence* it licenses, not by resemblance to a reference structure — and print, beside it, whether the account even admits that consequence (their gate) and whether the query is answerable at all from what was measured. Our folder's run 557 already argued for structural and intervention scores printed side by side; this paper adds the third cell, *not answerable*, which is the one an averaged table cannot carry. **A control that is cheap and decisive**: a known-truth arm whose regimes are chosen so that a passing suite can fail, plus the reported statistic "share that cleared every check and were still wrong" — a different object from a false-positive rate under a null, because the run is wrong in a specific direction and the checks are blind to it by construction. **A warning about agreement**: agreement across accounts is evidence about the *estimand*, not about the world. Three structurally different graphs converging on one number converged because they agreed on one adjustment set, and the same paper shows the number moving by a factor of three when a covariate enters the set — so agreement among accounts sharing a bias is not corroboration, and our own loop must state which quantity the concurring accounts actually share.
