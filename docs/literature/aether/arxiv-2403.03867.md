## What they wanted, and why
Linear representations are the empirical premise of nearly every direction- and dictionary-based reading of a network, and the authors ask why they should exist at all — could the observed linearity be an artefact of how people look? Their answer is a model small enough to analyse: they replace the language model with a discrete latent-concept model, keep only the softmax cross-entropy next-token objective and the training algorithm, and try to derive linear structure from those two ingredients alone. The larger goal is theoretical grounding for interpretability: if linearity follows from the objective rather than from the architecture, then a linear account of a residual stream is not a lucky coincidence but a consequence of what the training problem rewards. A second, less advertised goal is to build a toy system in which interpretability claims can be *tested* against a known ground truth.

## What they studied
No real network is trained. The object is a latent conditional model: m binary concepts C_1..C_m whose dependencies form a Markov random field (a DAG is noted as equally feasible), an injective map from concept vectors to tokens (so the concept is recoverable from the token), and a map from contexts to concept vectors with some entries marked unknown. A context fixes a "core" set of concepts and leaves the rest to be inferred. Two embedding lookups, one for contexts and one for tokens, play the roles of embedding and unembedding, and their inner product is passed through a softmax to approximate conditional probabilities. The unit of analysis is therefore a *concept direction*: differences between the vectors of two concepts that differ in exactly one binary variable. Real model contact is limited to LLaMA-2-7B.

## How they did it
Three layers, in order. First a data assumption: if the learned log-odds of a concept match the marginal log-odds for every context that does not condition on that concept, then all steering differences for that concept are parallel once an "ambiguous" subspace is projected out — the subspace spanned by contexts that do pin the concept. Independent concepts satisfy the premise automatically, and an appendix extends the result to general Markov random fields, where the paper says the concept vectors land in a low-dimensional subspace rather than a single line. Second, because matching log-odds everywhere is implausibly strong, they decompose next-token prediction into per-concept subproblems and observe that each is a binary classification under exponential loss: learning that a conditional probability is zero forces a large inner product, so each concept acts like a separating hyperplane problem, and gradient descent on exponential loss has a known max-margin direction. Theorems state that with one side of the inner product fixed the unembedding differences converge to cos = 1, and that with both sides trained (from orthogonal, equal-norm init) the embedding and unembedding differences align with each other as well. Third, simulation: random DAGs over m concepts with Bernoulli conditionals drawn in [0.3, 0.7], vectors sampled from the model, some entries masked to a "unknown" symbol, one-hot encodings so the lookups cannot exploit vector structure, models trained to reconstruct the unmasked vector by cross-entropy (SGD, learning rate 0.1, batch 100). Linearity is measured as the mean cosine among steering differences for the same concept, separately in the unembedding space, the embedding space, and across the two. 100 runs for m = 3 and 4, 50/20/10 for m = 5/6/7. Ablations: Adam instead of SGD, incomplete context sets, incomplete concept support (some concept vectors get probability zero), and representation width smaller than m. The LLaMA-2 check uses multilingual OPUS Books sentence pairs for embedding steering vectors and the 27 token-pair concepts of Park et al. for unembedding vectors, plus Winograd Schema sentence pairs that differ in one or two words.

## What counts as an explanation here
A derivation, not a description: an account of the form "this training objective plus this optimiser cannot help but produce a basis in which each concept is a line." The claim is conditional on a data assumption or on a subproblem decomposition, so it is an explanation-of-form rather than an explanation-of-this-checkpoint — nothing here is meant to predict what a specific model's layer 12 computes, only why whatever it computes should be readable as directions. The model itself is offered as a surrogate: a toy in which claims about linearity and orthogonality can be checked against ground truth.

## How they judged success
Mean cosine similarity of steering differences within a concept, in the unembedding space, the embedding space, and cross-space ("matched" representation), with standard errors over repeated runs — so the criterion is convergence to 1 in the limit of training. Completeness of the account is judged by invariance to the assumptions: whether linearity survives an incomplete context set, an incomplete concept set, a narrower representation than the number of concepts, and a different optimiser. Real-model evidence is a comparison of the same statistic computed on LLaMA-2 against the simulation's pattern, plus a matching/non-matching split on Winograd context pairs.

## What they found
Simulated linearity is near-total and rises with m: mean cosines between 0.97 and 0.997 across m = 3 to 7 for unembedding, embedding and cross-space with SGD, the m = 6 case the highest at 0.997 / 0.985 / 0.990. Adam reaches the same regime — 0.91 to 0.996 — so the effect is not specific to plain gradient descent. Restricting to 50 or 100 context masks over 10 concepts still leaves cosines at 0.974–0.946, i.e. linearity without the complete conditional table. Removing concept vectors (violating positivity) is the one ablation where the story weakens asymmetrically: unembedding linearity holds (0.951 at 10 concepts, 0.896 at 12) while embedding alignment collapses to 0.777 and 0.551, which the authors attribute partly to undertraining on an exponentially larger problem and partly to the zero probabilities themselves. Narrowing the representation dimension below m costs little. Orthogonality of *unrelated* concepts (those separated in the concept graph) is proved under exact probability matching, and is observed in simulation even for concepts that are not separated in the graph — the authors' explanation is that large norms are needed along matched directions while distinct concepts must keep small inner products, which pushes the geometry toward near-orthogonality. In LLaMA-2, same-concept steering vectors align more than different-concept ones, and multilingual sentence pairs align with the corresponding translation concept more than with unrelated ones; the absolute numbers are much smaller than in simulation (Winograd: 0.042 mean for matching pairs against 0.011 for non-matching, maxima 0.161 and 0.081).

## Limits and open problems they name
Injectivity, exact log-odds matching and the shared latent space are assumptions a real model breaks, and the authors say so where orthogonality is proved. The theorems cover subproblems, not the full non-convex training run, and the alignment theorem needs an orthogonal equal-norm initialisation (they argue a Gaussian init makes it approximately true). The LLaMA-2 experiments do not reach the simulated cosine levels, and they flag two explanations without resolving them: natural-language noise, and the possibility that the concepts in question are not one-dimensional, so that cosine is simply the wrong instrument. Concept vectors that never appear as tokens break the positivity assumption and partly break the result.

## What we take from it
Three things, in order of use.

*A normative rule for what our accounts may claim.* This is the cleanest statement in the folder of why a direction-based account is legitimate: linearity is a consequence of the objective's implicit bias, so when we report that a layer represents concept X along direction u, we are describing a form the training problem pushes toward rather than a coincidence — and the paper also names the two ways that claim can fail (the concept is not one-dimensional; the marginal log-odds are not matched because the data never conditions on it). Both are testable, and both should be printed beside the claim.

*A measure we can reuse directly.* Mean cosine among steering differences for a concept, computed separately in the embedding space, the unembedding space, and across the two, is nearly free on a real checkpoint — take counterfactual pairs that differ in one concept, form differences, report the three cosines with a spread over pairs. The matched-representation statistic (embedding and unembedding directions agreeing) is a prediction about weights that no dictionary method in the folder checks, and it is a genuine test: it can come out wrong.

*The ablation ladder as a template for robustness claims.* Complete conditionals, then a random subset of masks, then missing concept support, then narrower width, then a different optimiser — each rung changes one assumption and republishes the same statistic. The value for us is the asymmetric failure it exposes: unembedding linearity was robust to missing support while embedding alignment was not, which is exactly the kind of partial collapse a single overall score would hide. When we test whether our account survives missing coverage (a sparsely probed model, a narrower dictionary, fewer interventions), we should report the components separately and expect them to fail at different rates.

*A warning about the instrument.* The authors themselves note that a low cosine may mean the concept is not one-dimensional rather than that the theory is wrong — which is the same caveat `arxiv-2405.14860` raises from the empirical side. So a high within-concept cosine is evidence of linear form; a low one is not evidence of absence, and our write-ups should not read it as such.
