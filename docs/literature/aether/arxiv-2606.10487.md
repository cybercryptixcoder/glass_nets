# Stop Early, Spend Less: Hidden-State Probes as a Practical Recipe for Streaming Moderation of LLM Outputs

- **Key:** arxiv-2606.10487
- **Authors / organisation:** Huizhen Shu (ModelOneAI), Xuying Li, Piao Xue (yunshanai) — industrial technical report, not an academic lab
- **Date:** 2026-06-09 (v1, single version)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.10487
- **Kind:** paper (technical report, 14 pages, 3 figures, 4 tables, CC BY-NC-SA 4.0)
- **Read from:** full text (v1 HTML, abstract, §§1–10, references, Appendices A–B; Tables 1–4 read as cells; Figures 1–3 read through captions, not as images)
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
A deployed assistant has to screen its own output for unsafe content, and the ordinary way to do that is to run a second model — a moderation API, a Llama Guard, a guardrails framework — over the finished text. Two costs follow: the extra model roughly doubles serving cost, and because it reads a completed response, the user has already received whatever it flags. The authors start from the observation that a serving model already computed a residual stream containing whatever made it produce those tokens, so the classifier can be a small head bolted onto a layer it was going to run anyway. The stated aim is therefore explicitly not a better moderator but a *cheaper* one: a distilled surrogate for a strong guard, judged on cost and timing rather than accuracy, that fires per token inside the decode loop so a response can be halted or rewritten mid-generation. The larger goal is an always-on first-stage screen cheap enough to sit on every streamed response.

## What they studied
One frozen generator, DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B (a reasoning model distilled into a Qwen3 backbone), with probes also trained on Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-30B-A3B-Thinking-2507 but not reported. Unit of analysis is the **per-token hidden state at one mid-network layer**, and the classifier is a tiny head over that vector: three variants are tried — a single linear projection, a two-layer MLP (hidden width 1,024, ReLU), and a "Dual-head" convex mixture of the two with coefficient α. The object being read is a *token is part of an unsafe generation* label, so the target is narrower than a semantics probe: it is a safety judgment the aligned model already encodes. Data: an in-house Chinese compliance corpus (Zhuyi) of 49 fine-grained categories with label and per-category scores assigned by Qwen3Guard-Gen-8B, split into training, 5,000 validation and 2,444 held-out test rows; online evaluation adds BeaverTails and CSSBench.

## How they did it
The generator is frozen (no gradient into the LLM) and only the head trains. Supervision is deliberately lopsided: the loss is a BCE over the *sequence* label but weighted per token by a softmax of the pooled logit, so the head is pushed to fit the handful of positions where the unsafe behaviour actually surfaces rather than every token equally. At inference the per-token logits are collapsed into a sequence score by one of six aggregations (max, top-k mean, window max, percentile, log-sum-exp, softmax-weighted mean), and the decision rule is a threshold on that score. For streaming, a forward hook on the chosen layer inside a vLLM worker forwards each decode step's cached hidden state through the head — one matrix-vector product — and a small running state plus a trigger mode (fire on one crossing; require K cumulative or consecutive crossings; a leaky counter; an excess budget, with an optional warmup threshold) decides whether to stop or rewrite the response right then; a probe interval skips decode steps to cut cost. Two-stage search: a hand-designed sweep over layer, head and aggregation, then an *autonomous* loop in which an LLM agent may edit exactly one training file, keeps a single-variable change only if validation F1 rises, and was run overnight for 177 trials. Finally, the deployment parameters (score mode, trigger, threshold, warmup, K, interval) are chosen by replaying cached per-token score logs through a ~20,000-cell grid, which makes calibration a CPU sub-minute job. A second, separate prototype writes as well as reads: when the trigger fires, a multiple of the linear branch's unit weight vector is subtracted from the hidden state at that step.

## What counts as an explanation here
The weakest form in the folder: a **probe plus a direction**, with no mechanism. The probe is a scalar score per token; the only structural claim is that a single mid-network layer suffices and that most of the signal is linearly readable, with the MLP branch adding a modest amount on the categories where the signal is not linearly separable. The linear branch's weight vector is offered as a usable direction in residual space, which is asserted by construction (a linear read-out is a direction) rather than demonstrated to name a coherent feature. Nothing here predicts a behaviour beyond an unsafe/not-unsafe verdict, and the authors do not claim otherwise: the probe "recovers the guard's verdicts", not the guard's reasons.

## How they judged success
Offline: accuracy, binary F1, precision, recall and AUC on the held-out Zhuyi split — but against the *guard's* labels, since the probe was trained on them, so this measures distillation agreement rather than correctness, a caveat the paper states plainly. Online: F1, precision and recall, plus operational signals (average stop step, average tokens saved, probe-call savings) across a probe-interval sweep. Cost and latency are scored head-to-head against two guard models on 1,000 BeaverTails examples carrying their native human labels — a referee none of the three systems was trained on — reporting added VRAM, added tokens, FLOPs and in P50/P95/P99 latency. Baselines: a post-hoc Qwen3Guard-Gen-8B moderator and a streaming Qwen3Guard-Stream-8B moderator, on the same 8B generator. No significance testing, no confidence intervals, no seed variance reported, and single reported backbone.

## What they found
Layer choice is sharp and shallow: sweeping an MLP-token probe with max aggregation, every metric climbs steeply between layers 18 and 21 and plateaus around 21 (F1 0.941, AUC 0.942 on the 2,444-row test split), with no further gain past layer 24 — that is 21 of roughly 36 layers. The linear head loses real ground at the same layer (F1 0.892 against 0.941), and the loss concentrates on the obfuscation categories where the unsafe signal is least linearly separable, which is the one place in the paper where the non-linearity of the representation is measured rather than asserted. Aggregation matters more than the authors expected: max is best, top-k mean and window max within 0.005 F1 of it, but the 90th-percentile rule collapses to F1 0.097 at precision 1.0 — on long responses the safe prefix's own 90th percentile sits above the unsafe suffix's scores — and log-sum-exp lands in between at 0.584. The autonomous search lifted validation F1 from 0.9264 to 0.9495 (+0.0231, about a quarter of the remaining error) in 177 trials with 11 accepted and 163 rejected; two changes carried almost all of it — training-time top-k pooling over the 16 largest token logits (mirroring max at inference, because the unsafe signal lives in a small fraction of the response) and the Dual-head mixture at α = 0.8 — while 1,536/2,048-wide MLPs, three-layer MLPs, LayerNorm, focal loss, label smoothing, attention pooling, residual and gated blocks, dual-path aggregation and seed swaps were all tested at least three times and rejected. Full-data retraining reached validation F1 0.9746 (AUC 0.9865, precision 0.985, recall 0.965), and all seven parallel layer jobs (18–24) converged above 0.97, so the layer choice is flat once trained properly — the hand-designed layer sensitivity was a property of the small probe, not the representation.

On the honest referee the probe is the weaker classifier and the paper says so in those words: against native human labels both guards score F1 ≈ 0.85 (post-hoc 0.855, streaming 0.846), while the probe at its default threshold is strongly precision-biased (F1 0.504, precision 0.930, recall 0.346) and only reaches 0.795 after a per-task threshold recalibration. The case for deploying it is entirely cost and timing: 8 MB of weights and about 0.6 GFLOP per request against 14–16 GB and 1.3–8.0 TFLOP for either guard, and roughly 0.1 ms added tail latency against a serial 448–602 ms post-hoc stage or 39–54 ms for the streaming guard, which also needs a second GPU. Streaming on Zhuyi, the probe interrupts about 92% of unsafe sequences within their first 20% of generated tokens; the residual failures concentrate in the obfuscation categories, where unsafe content only crystallises late. Halving the probe rate (probing every second token) costs at most ±1 F1 point and *improves* precision — false positives roughly halve — after which F1 decays monotonically: at a rate of one probe per 100 tokens, precision stays at or above 0.70 while recall falls to 0.19–0.27. The authors explain the knee with a density argument: on these data risky tokens are about 1% of a 1,024-token generation, so once the sampling interval exceeds roughly one over that density whole unsafe spans fall between probes.

## Limits and open problems they name
The probe is trained on a guard's labels and is explicitly not expected to beat its teacher, so every in-domain F1 is an agreement score; the one human-labelled comparison leaves it about five points short even after recalibration. Only one backbone's numbers are reported, transfer to a model without RLHF alignment is untested, and the online experiments still use the *hand-designed* MLP head, not the better full-data Dual-head checkpoint, which has not been run through the serving worker. Thresholds were tuned per dataset on a fixed validation set and the paper says deployment needs per-task recalibration. No adaptive attack was tested, and the authors name the danger themselves: a threshold on a line in residual space can be evaded by anyone who knows the line, which is the same mechanism their steering prototype exploits. English performance is about 15 F1 points below the Chinese in-domain numbers, the obfuscation and homophone categories are the persistent weak spot, and the steering half of the read/write story is a prototype with no quantitative evaluation of refusal versus safe completion versus jailbreak recovery.

## What we take from it
This is the folder's clearest worked example of a **probe used as a decision instrument rather than as evidence about the model**, and what it demonstrates is mostly negative for us.

(1) **An agreement score is not an accuracy, and the gap is large**: F1 0.941 against guard labels becomes 0.504 at the probe's default threshold against native human labels, and 0.795 only after recalibration. Any probe result we report against labels that a model or judge produced inherits exactly this inflation, and the remedy is the one used here — a small set labelled by a referee nobody was trained on, with the operating point printed beside the accuracy.

(2) **A threshold crossing count is a fine target and the interval sweep is the right cost curve**: because halving the probe rate *raises* precision (temporal smoothing) while recall only collapses once the interval exceeds roughly one over the density of the target events, the useful curve is F1 against calls, with the knee explained by a density estimate rather than fitted. That is a cheap and general shape for any per-token read-out we build.

(3) **This paper is why borderline probe/steering work is hard to publish**: no CIs, no seeds, no adaptive attack, single backbone, an architecture search whose accepted chain is reported but whose validation subsets are reused across 177 trials. It is the strongest argument in the folder that a probe paper is judged on its controls, and it supplies the warning that motivates those controls.

(4) **The layer claim is the one result worth carrying**: with a strong enough head, every layer from 18 to 24 converges to F1 above 0.97, so the "best layer" that the small-probe sweep localised at 21 is an artefact of the probe's capacity. Best-layer-by-probe-sweep is a claim about the probe, not the representation — a rule this folder already holds from the steering side and now has a probe-side demonstration of, though a weak one: the two designs also differ in training budget (10,000 cached rows against ~600,000 full-split rows), so capacity and data are confounded.
