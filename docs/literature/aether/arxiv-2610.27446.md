# Decision Sufficiency of Learned Representation Interfaces for Safe Generative Control

- **Key:** arxiv-2610.27446
- **Authors / organisation:** Jipeng Li, Yanning Shen — University of California, Irvine
- **Date:** 2026-10-20
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27446
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
A controller sitting on top of a language model picks one of a few response strategies — answer, ask a clarifying question, or decline — from inside a deployment policy. If that controller reads selected internal features rather than the whole situation, a bad decision tells you nothing on its own: the features may not contain the fact, the controller may be restricted to rules too poor to use it, or it may simply be badly fitted. The paper asks how to separate those three possibilities *exactly* on a fixed audit sample, so that "this interface is not good enough" becomes a statement about what the representation permits rather than about the classifier's training. It is a step toward deciding which internal readout is worth building a safety controller on at all.

## What they studied
Three families: public residual-stream SAEs in Qwen2.5-1.5B (layers 17/18/19/21) and in Qwen2.5-0.5B, and locally trained TopK SAEs at layer 17 of SmolLM2-1.7B and layer 23 of Phi-4-mini-instruct, each trained twice with different initialisation and data order on the same activation corpus. The unit of analysis is not a feature but an **interface**: a chosen small set of SAE coordinates presented to the controller as on/off indicators (B from 2 up to 64, B = 2 primary), optionally together with the governing policy text. The full audited state is the situation, the policy and the recorded evidence; the decision is one of three continuations (assist, ask, decline) from a fixed library.

## How they did it
Each state is a synthetic request with a governing policy drawn from 240 states in 20 policy areas; twelve areas are used to choose layers, thresholds and feature rankings and eight (96 states) are held out before any generation or scoring. Every state is rendered under all three actions, giving a loss entry per state and action from graded automatic scores (five dimensions from 0 to 3, safety and policy each weighted 0.30, uselessness 0.15, factuality 0.10, overall 0.15) and, for SmolLM2, from two independent human raters over 528 items with 100 jointly adjudicated. Three or four risks are then computed on that table: the minimum with the full state, the minimum over **all** rules seeing only the interface, the minimum inside an allowed rule family, and the fitted rule's risk. Because the interface and the state space are finite, the second quantity is exact — cells are the interface's preimages and the best rule picks the best conditional mean continuation in each cell — so the authors read off information, accessibility and fitting gaps as literal differences. They then go structural: a cell is usable only if one distribution over continuations keeps regret below a tolerance at every state inside it, groups that cannot are "minimal conflicts" (bounded by the number of continuations via Helly), and the number of outputs an interface needs is the weak chromatic number of the conflict hypergraph, solved by constraint generation with an MILP. Controls vary evaluator, feature selector (against 200 random selectors and occupancy-matched ones), model scale, rule family and fitting sample, and a constructed three-state table demonstrates a conflict every pairwise check would miss.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **quantity, not a description**: an exact decision-theoretic decomposition of excess loss into missing information, rule restriction and fitting error, plus a certificate of *why* the information is missing (a specific set of states sharing an observation that need incompatible strategies). It is complete with respect to the recorded table and the declared continuation library, and deliberately silent on everything else. The claim it supports is predictive only on the audited sample: a positive information gap means no controller reading that interface can match the full state on these scores, and nothing about future responses.

## How they judged success
The measure is minimum regret over a recorded loss table, with the ordering full-state ≤ best-interface ≤ best-in-family ≤ fitted guaranteed as a theorem. Uncertainty comes from bootstraps that resample whole policy areas (1,500 draws for Qwen, 2,000 and eight areas for Phi), reported as individual 95% intervals and one-sided lower bounds; a separate proposition bounds how a group margin can shrink under a uniform bound on loss-estimation error, certifying a conflict only after selection. Sanity interfaces (constant, shuffled-code, and an oracle that shows the best action) are run through the identical pipeline to check the measurement, and the paper states repeatedly that a zero fraction of nonpositive draws is not a p-value.

## What they found
With policy text supplied, two on/off SAE indicators still lose information: on the 96 held-out SmolLM2 states the human-rated gap is 0.1166 and 0.1082 for the two SAEs (lower bounds 0.0935, 0.0901), and on the original 80 states 0.0345 and 0.0353 — so the gap reproduces across seeds and survives the most generous input the authors give the controller. For Qwen at layer 17 with two indicators and no policy text, the full-state minimum is 0.338 against 0.494 for the code, splitting into an information gap of 0.156, an accessibility gap of 0.036 and a fitting gap of 0.011; at layer 19 with sixteen indicators the three shrink to 0.060 / 0.047 / 0.022. The rule family moves only the middle term (constant 0.143 → affine 0.047 → pairwise 0.007 → zero for lookup), and going from 16 to 144 fitting situations drives the fitting gap from 0.130 to zero — the two failures that look like information loss are both removable, the information gap is not. Robustness is uniform: every one of 200 random selectors and forty occupancy-matched controls gives a positive gap; a second evaluator gives 0.134 where the primary gives 0.156 on the same responses; Qwen2.5-0.5B gives 0.210; deleting any single policy area leaves all 192 checks positive (minimum 0.0236). The constant interface loses 0.208 and a shuffled-code one 0.200 against the real interface's 0.128, with an oracle at zero. The structural half is where the paper is most careful: the constructed triple (pairwise margins zero, triple margin one third) proves pairwise auditing is insufficient in principle, but the natural-language conflict it searched for did **not** replicate — zero separation under two fresh paraphrase replicas, five generation replicas and three judge prompts — so the phenomenon is demonstrated, not measured, in real text.

## Limits and open problems they name
The optima are computed on recorded scores, so a positive gap is a statement about the audit table, not about unknown losses on future responses; minimizing noisy scores can move the gap even when no score is biased. The comparison does not localise where information was lost — model activation, SAE encoding, coordinate selection or the thresholding are separate stages and are not distinguished. Automatic and human losses agree only loosely (Spearman 0.379) with tied optimal actions in 90 of 176 states, so the human arm is one contrast, not a calibration. Most of the human-rating contrast was added to the study after the original design, the Qwen arm with policy supplied is recovered from rounded notebook summaries, and the older 42-state capacity matrix is unavailable, so that curve cannot be recomputed. The families also differ in generation and scoring protocols, so the table is a set of within-protocol results rather than a model ranking.

## What we take from it
A **measure** and a **framing** worth importing wholesale: any claim that a representation "contains" the information a downstream rule needs can be audited exactly, on a finite labelled sample, by comparing the best possible rule on the interface against the best possible rule on the full state — and then splitting the deficit so that the part caused by a weak classifier is not charged to the representation. Two habits follow for our own battery. Print all four levels rather than a single probe score, because the gap between the best-in-family and fitted rules is where most "the probe failed" findings actually live, and it is removable by more data. And borrow the conflict construction: when we report that a cell, cluster or feature bucket cannot support a distinction, the honest evidence is a *group* of items in that cell with mutually incompatible best actions, since every pair can share an optimum while the group cannot. The warning is equally useful — their own natural-language conflict failed replication, which is exactly the discipline our entries keep recommending before a structural claim is written down.
