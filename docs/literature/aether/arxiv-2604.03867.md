# Where to Steer: Input-Dependent Layer Selection for Steering Improves LLM Alignment

- **Key:** arxiv-2604.03867
- **Authors / organisation:** Soham Gadgil, Chris Lin, Su-In Lee; Department of Computer Science, University of Washington
- **Date:** 2026-04
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.03867
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (arXiv HTML v2; body, both main tables, and Appendix A - K read; supplementary figures not inspected as images)
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Steering-vector work almost always picks one layer for a behaviour and reuses it for every input, which implicitly assumes the place where a behaviour lives is a property of the behaviour rather than of the prompt. The authors ask whether that assumption costs anything: if different prompts route a behaviour through different depths, a globally fixed injection site would be optimal for the average prompt and wrong for many individual ones. The larger goal is alignment at inference time, where steering is the cheap knob, so a free gain in its reliability is worth a lot. They want to show the variation exists, bound how large the gain from exploiting it would be, and then supply a cheap predictor that captures part of it.

## What they studied
Three chat models: Llama-2-7B-Chat (32 layers), Qwen-1.5-14B-Chat (40 layers) as the main pair, and Qwen3-32B (64 layers) as a scale check. Data is 13 Model-Written-Evaluation steering datasets of 1,000 prompts each - eight persona sets answered with Yes/No, five advanced-AI-risk sets with sentence-level answer choices - plus two open-ended sets (TruthfulQA, 817 examples; a 1,000-example safety-filtered slice of PKU-SafeRLHF). The unit of analysis is a **layer per example**: a 40/10/50 train/val/test split per behaviour, with each example carrying its own optimal injection layer as a label. Two steering-vector families are covered, a static one (contrastive activation addition, CAA) and an input-dependent one (learning-to-steer, L2S), so the layer choice can be shown to be a separate axis from the vector-extraction choice.

## How they did it
First a constructed existence proof: a three-layer token-to-token model over four tokens, with four embeddings chosen by hand, in which the target behaviour is a non-monotonic function of the output logits - so no single layer is optimal for all four inputs, and in two of the four cases steering at the sub-optimal layer moves the behaviour the wrong way. Then an empirical sweep on the real models: for each example and each layer, steer at the last token position across a multiplier grid of alpha in {-1.5, -1.0, -0.5, 0.0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5}, and score steerability as the slope of a least-squares fit of the log-probability difference between the positive and negative answers against alpha. The arg-max layer over that sweep becomes the example's gold label. Layers that are optimal for no training example are pruned (32 down to about 24 on average for Llama, 40 down to about 35 for Qwen). A shallow MLP is then trained by cross-entropy with weight decay to predict the layer from a prompt embedding - four encoder candidates are compared on UMAP separability plus silhouette and on end-task accuracy, and text-embedding-3-large wins with a silhouette of 0.64, with bert-base-uncased re-run as the open-source substitute. The fixed-layer baseline gets the same sweep but keeps the single layer with the highest mean steerability across the whole dataset, which is the fair comparison because it is the same information budget. Out-of-distribution tests inject positive or negative behaviour text into the system message or the user message, train only on the base distribution, and evaluate on the four variants. Generation quality is judged by an LLM rater on fluency, repetition and coherence.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not a mechanistic account and not a circuit - the deliverable is a **small predictor that maps a prompt to an injection depth**, i.e. a routing rule over a model's layers. Understanding is operational: the claim is that the optimal intervention site is a function of the input, and the evidence is that a cheap classifier of that function beats any constant. It is deliberately partial - the paper says nothing about which features are being written, only where in depth the write should land - and it is predictive in a narrow sense: the layer is predicted and then acted on, so the prediction is tested by the downstream steering result rather than by its own accuracy. A secondary explanatory device is the hand-built three-layer model, offered as a proof that the phenomenon is not an artefact of scale.

## How they judged success
Steerability (slope of the log-prob difference against steering multiplier) and the proportion of examples with positive steerability, both averaged over behaviours with 95% confidence intervals over five runs. The headline comparison is against the fixed-layer baseline for the same extraction method, in-distribution and under four prompt perturbations. Controls and checks: oracle-layering (per-example arg-max) as the upper bound, encoders compared on two axes, a Top-2 and Top-3 multi-layer extension, a sequence effect where steering a second layer acts on activations already modified by the first, an LLM-judged fluency/repetition/coherence panel at multiplier +1, and a label-frequency analysis of how many layers the predictor actually has to choose between.

## What they found
Oracle per-example layering buys a lot of headroom: steerability rises by 55% on Llama-2-7B-Chat and 86% on Qwen-1.5-14B-Chat over the fixed layer, with the optimal layer deviating from the fixed one by 3.8 layers on average on Llama and 6.5 on Qwen, and the optimal indices scattering across early, middle and late depths. The learned predictor recovers only a slice of that: in-distribution, CAA on Llama goes from 1.259 to 1.502 steerability and 0.754 to 0.846 on the steerable proportion; L2S on Llama from 2.098 to 2.363 and 0.899 to 0.918; CAA on Qwen 1.493 to 1.675; L2S on Qwen 1.888 to 2.071 and 0.875 to 0.918. Under prompt shift the gains persist and sometimes widen (Llama CAA 1.422 to 1.821 for a positive system message; L2S 2.456 to 2.802) and the paper's most useful single result is that fixed-layer steering is sometimes **harmful** there and the predictor repairs it: negative steerability for narcissism under a positive system prompt and for openness under a negative user prompt on Qwen becomes positive once the layer is chosen per input. In open-ended generation the effect is large but noisier - Llama safety goes from -0.045 for fixed CAA to 0.857 with the predictor, and from -0.059 to 0.328 for L2S, while Llama truthfulness with L2S jumps from 0.886 to 3.175 - and the steerable proportion gains in that setting are small (0.638 to 0.647 on Llama truthfulness), so the slope metric moves far more than the count of examples that can be steered at all. Multi-layer steering helps CAA but not L2S on multiple-choice sets, and helps both on open-ended sets. The predictors themselves are weak - about 45% accuracy on TruthfulQA and about 40% on the safety set - and the authors argue the gains survive that because many near-optimal layers exist per example: the second and third most steerable layers still beat the fixed baseline while the fourth does not, which motivates a relabelling scheme that assigns each example to the most globally frequent layer among its top-k rather than its individual arg-max, and that relabelling helps further (Llama L2S to 2.417 with relabelled labels against 2.363 for plain arg-max). Generation quality is flat: every judged mean stays above 4.5 on the five-point scale. Cost is negligible - 0.8 seconds to fit the CAA router, and inference grows by well under a second per example (2.01 to 2.24 seconds on Llama CAA).

## Limits and open problems they name
The layer predictors are only moderately accurate, attributed to limited training data; the authors defend this rather than fix it, and the defence rests on the near-optimality of neighbours, which means the method's ceiling is partly unknown. Pruning layers that are never optimal creates a dependency on the training set's layer distribution, which the authors flag as an out-of-distribution risk. Evaluation is single-objective - one behaviour at a time - so nothing is known about routing when two behaviours want different depths. Multi-layer extension uses one sequential protocol with no optimisation over which layers or how many. The three-layer proof is a construction, not a characterisation, so it shows input-dependence is possible without saying how often it matters in a real network.

## What we take from it
A **framing and a measure**, and a warning that applies directly to us. The framing: the layer at which an intervention is best placed is a function of the input, so any white-box model that publishes a single "best layer" for a behaviour should publish the distribution of per-input best layers beside it, and should be expected to be wrong on the tails. The measure: the per-example oracle as a ceiling and the fixed-site baseline on the same information budget, which is how a reader can tell whether an input-dependent method has captured most of the available gain or a sliver of it - here it is a sliver of the oracle's 55%/86% but enough to convert negative steerability to positive. The warning, which we should carry into our own selection work: **the predictor recovers a small fraction of oracle headroom and the paper still reports large aggregate gains, partly because near-optimal layers cluster** - a "we predict where to intervene" claim is only as informative as the margin between the layer it picks and the layers it does not, and a metric like the steerability slope can improve substantially while the count of examples that can be steered at all barely moves, so both numbers belong in the same row. This is the mechanism-shaped complement to the folder's layer-selection entries (TopoSteer's validation-free scalar, LayerNavigator's discriminability criterion): those rank layers per behaviour, this ranks them per prompt, and the two axes are separable.
