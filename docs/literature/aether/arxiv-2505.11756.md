## What they wanted, and why
Sparse autoencoders are supposed to split a dense activation into interpretable linear directions, and the field's working assumption is that they succeed whenever the activations really are sparse linear combinations of underlying features. The authors think that assumption hides a failure mode that is switched on by two conditions they believe hold in every real LLM SAE: the dictionary is narrower than the number of true features, and the features are correlated. They want to name that failure (hedging), show it is a property of the reconstruction objective rather than of an unlucky fit, give a way to measure how much of it a trained SAE carries, and use the diagnosis to repair a known architecture. The larger goal is explanatory rather than incremental: they are trying to account for why SAEs keep losing to much simpler supervised baselines.

## What they studied
Synthetic dictionaries of orthogonal true features with controlled co-firing statistics — 4-feature toy models, then a deliberately minimal 2-feature / 50-dimension world with a single-latent SAE — plus real SAEs trained on Gemma-2-2b (layer 12) and Llama-3.2-1b (layer 7), widths 128 to 65,536, L1, TopK and BatchTopK architectures. The unit of analysis is the individual latent and, more precisely, the *decoder column* and *encoder row* of a latent, compared against the ground-truth feature directions by cosine similarity, and compared against itself before and after the dictionary is widened. The contrast with feature absorption is explicit throughout: the paper's Table 1 sets the two side by side, and every claim below is a claim about how a latent's *decoder* moves when the dictionary around it changes.

## How they did it
Toy worlds are generated by drawing a binary activity vector with Bernoulli firing probabilities, then summing the active unit-norm feature directions; hierarchy, positive correlation and anti-correlation are imposed by conditioning one feature's firing probability on another's. SAELens trains the SAE on 15M synthetic activations. The diagnostic for real models is an *extension* experiment: take a trained SAE s0, append N = 64 fresh latents initialised with the same encoder/decoder direction and norm 0.1, keep training both s0 and the extended s1 on the same further 250M tokens, and ask how much the original L decoder columns moved. Because an added latent is child-like (rarer than its parent), an absorbing SAE would revise the parent *encoder* only, while a hedging SAE must revise the *decoder* symmetrically — so a decoder change attributable to the new latents is evidence of hedging and nothing else. To attribute the change to the new latents rather than to noise, the change vector of each old latent is projected onto the span of the 64 new columns and the same projection onto 64 random unit-norm columns is subtracted; the mean over old latents is the hedging degree. The repair keeps the matryoshka nested-prefix loss but scales each level m by its own coefficient, so the inner (narrow, hedging-prone) levels can be down-weighted against the outer one; it is swept as a geometric multiplier, with 10 seeds per setting, on Gemma-2-2b layer 12 at five levels (128, 512, 2,048, 8,192, 32,768).

## What counts as an explanation here
A named mechanism plus a structural signature, not a description of individual latents: hedging is defined as "polysemantic mixture" of true features inside one latent, caused by the MSE term, symmetric across encoder and decoder, and worse the narrower the dictionary — the exact mirror of absorption (gerrymandered latents, caused by the sparsity penalty, encoder/decoder asymmetric, worse the wider the dictionary, requires a hierarchy). The explanation is predictive at the level of a dictionary-wide statistic: knowing a model's width and its features' correlation should let you forecast how much mixing a latent carries, and the sign of the correlation should forecast the sign of the mixing. It is explicitly partial — the authors present one coefficient per level as an improvement, not a solution.

## How they judged success
Toy-model recovery checked against known ground truth (cosine matrices between SAE encoder/decoder and true features, with latents re-sorted for legibility, plus expected-loss curves as a function of the interpolation coefficient between the two true features). Real-model hedging measured by the hedging-degree statistic (their Eq. 7) as a function of width, L0 and layer, against the random-latent projection as its own null. The balance-matryoshka repair is scored on SAEBench by default settings — absorption rate, absorption fraction, feature splitting, SCR, TPP, K-sparse probing — plus the paper's own k=1 sparse-probing F1 on a Penn-Treebank parts-of-speech set they build, with 10 seeds and one standard deviation shaded.

## What they found
In the single-latent toy world, mixing appears the moment any correlation exists at all, and its magnitude tracks the correlation over a sweep from −0.5 to 0.5 while its *sign* follows the sign of the correlation; with fully independent features the latent is clean. The latent is initialised at the correct direction so any drift is gradient pressure, not a bad local basin. A full-width SAE recovers the true features even under the same correlation and the same low sparsity penalty, which is the control that makes width the cause. Expected-loss curves for the hierarchical toy have their minimum strictly between the two features, so the honest answer is not merely competitively beaten but actively preferred by the objective. In real SAEs, hedging degree falls monotonically with width and is markedly elevated at 4,096 latents and below, with no width they tested — up to 65,536 — reaching zero; BatchTopK reads as more hedged than L1 at matched settings, which they attribute to the sparsity penalty's ability to undo part of the *positive* hedging; very low L0 raises hedging modestly for BatchTopK; layer is not a large factor. On the repair, an intermediate level multiplier around 0.75 beats both the plain SAE (multiplier 0) and the standard matryoshka SAE (multiplier 1) on TPP, feature splitting and sparse probing, while still scoring well on absorption — with the honest exception that SCR improves at *higher* multipliers, which they flag as the noisiest metric and read as hedging adding noise rather than as evidence against the story.

## Limits and open problems they name
Compute caps the study at 65k latents and 2B-parameter models, and the detection method needs fine-tuning, which is expensive. A single coefficient per level cannot always balance all children — their Appendix A.8 toy with three children at different firing rates cannot be perfectly balanced by any one β — so they expect per-latent coefficients would do better and leave it open. Balance-matryoshka is presented as a starting point. The link from hedging to the measured baselines gap (AxBench, sparse probing) is stated as suspicion, not measured.

## What we take from it
Three things. First, a *measure* we can reuse nearly for free on our own dictionaries: append a matched block of latents, continue training, and read off how much old decoder columns move toward the new ones, with a random-column projection subtracted as the null — a paired, within-dictionary test of "is this latent a mixture", which is stronger than the one-shot cosine diagnostic entry `arxiv-2508.16560` recommends. Second, a *warning* that transferable to our own accounts: width is not neutral, so any statement of the form "latent X tracks concept Y" is conditioned on a width budget, and a narrow account will systematically report mixed latents that a wider one resolves — the same latent will look different at a different budget without the model changing. Third, a *framing* worth stealing for our predictive goal: hedging and absorption are opposite in sign, so a design choice that fixes one worsens the other, and the paper's answer is not to pick a side but to publish the coefficient that trades them and show which metrics move. Also note the tiny experiment that carries the whole argument (two features, one latent, an expected-loss curve with a nonzero minimum) — the cheapest possible falsification of a dictionary's own assumption, and one we should run before trusting any feature-level claim.
