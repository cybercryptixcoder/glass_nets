## What they wanted, and why
Asking *which training examples made this output likely* is the routine first question in auditing or correcting a language model, and for dense transformers it is expensive, approximate and post hoc. The authors' diagnosis is that this is an architectural fact rather than a solver problem: a dense output head distributes each training example's influence across every parameter, so recovering it means inverting a badly conditioned Hessian over billions of dimensions, and the gradient-based approximations that do this also assume a deterministic convex objective that modern training does not satisfy. Their proposal is to stop estimating and start designing — put the interpretable unit inside the prediction pathway so that attribution becomes retrieval in a space the model already exposes. The paper builds that model, argues it does not cost predictive quality, and then re-derives the attribution theory in the new coordinates.

## What they studied
A GPT-style decoder-only backbone (124M/355M/774M/1.558B, block size 1024) with the final dense output pathway replaced by a sparse prototype reconstruction — called PRISM. Data: TinyStories for the diagnostic sections, FineWeb-Edu and a code- and science-enriched subset of Nemotron-CC for scaling (10B to 50B tokens, 19,073 optimizer steps at a 524,288-token global batch per 10B). Prototype banks of K = 512 to 16,384, top-k = 8 to 256 active per position. Unit of analysis: the prototype — a learned hidden-state vector carrying a fixed vocabulary signature through the shared, tied LM head — plus the residual branch that absorbs whatever the prototypes do not reconstruct.

## How they did it
Architecture first: for hidden state z_t, take cosine similarity to every prototype, ReLU it (so activations are non-negative), keep the top k, and reconstruct ẑ_t as the activation-weighted sum; the logits decompose exactly as W·r_t + Σ a_t,i·(W·p_i), i.e. a residual term plus one additive term per active prototype. Prototypes are the only thing that has to be learned for the readout, so they place them in hidden space rather than adding a K×V prototype-to-vocabulary classifier. Four losses: cross-entropy, a reconstruction penalty on r_t, and a *symmetric* pair of clustering terms — one pulling each prototype toward its nearest token state in the batch, the other pulling each token state toward its nearest prototype — which they describe as the mechanism that keeps prototypes near real activations instead of drifting off-manifold like an unconstrained sparse dictionary. They then run three studies. (1) TinyStories ablations: clustering strength, top-k and K, each scored on CE, prototype coverage, residual share, ΔCE with the residual removed, ΔCE with the prototypes removed, and a "weighted rank" of the target token among active prototypes. (2) A dense-initialisation test: take a pretrained dense TinyStories checkpoint, bolt on the PRISM head, and finetune either the head plus prototypes or also a small adapter, under a dictionary-only objective versus the full clustering objective versus an R1-only ablation. (3) Attribution: derive the prototype-space Hessian under a fixed-support assumption, then score training positions by an influence-style inner product where the *training* side is a gradient over only the k active prototypes and can therefore be cached as sparse records, and compare query-time cost and ranking overlap against EK-FAC on the same 20GB GPU. Two applied workflows follow: a frozen-model linear controller over active-prototype features for multiple-choice tasks, and category-level prototype suppression at inference.

## What counts as an explanation here
A trained decomposition rather than a reconstruction of one: the explanation is the *active prototype mixture* — which prototypes fired, with what weight, which vocabulary tokens each one's fixed signature supports, and which training neighbourhoods that prototype's highest-activation contexts came from. It is partial by design and says so: the residual is an explicit, inspectable "not explained by prototypes" channel, and prototype share is reported as a number (0.941 for a from-scratch 124M model) rather than assumed to be 1. It is meant to be predictive in a narrow sense — the logit decomposition is exact arithmetic, and ablation/amplification of one channel changes the logits by the amount the decomposition says it should — but the semantic label of each prototype is a Claude-generated summary of surface behaviour, not part of the claim.

## How they judged success
Language-modelling: validation CE and perplexity against a dense GPT matched on backbone, tokenizer, optimizer and token budget; zero-shot accuracy on seven LM-Eval-Harness tasks (HellaSwag, OpenBookQA, WinoGrande, ARC-Challenge, ARC-Easy, BoolQ, PIQA) averaged. Grounding: R1 (batch-wise nearest-token coverage of prototypes, accumulated over a validation stream) and R2 (how close token states sit to their nearest prototype), plus residual share and reconstruction error. Interpretability ablations are deliberately CE-matched: the argument is only interesting if grounding moves while CE does not. For attribution, three baselines in one ladder — ignoring curvature, diagonal stiffness, full conjugate-gradient solve in prototype space — with full K×d prototype-space scoring as the internal reference, EK-FAC as an external dense reference (used for overlap, explicitly *not* as ground truth), and matched-storage random projections of EK-FAC fingerprints as the cost control. A separate SAE fidelity check trains matched top-k SAEs on final hidden states of the dense, dictionary-control and native-PRISM models and reports both relative MSE and the CE increase when the reconstruction is substituted back into the prediction interface. Downstream controllers are selected on a validation split and reported on the official evaluation split; alignment is judged by an LLM judge (Mistral-Small-24B-Instruct-2501) on 50,000 paired generations with a two-axis 0–2 rubric.

## What they found
On TinyStories, clustering is nearly free at the language-modelling level and expensive to skip at the interpretability level: CE moves from 1.2435 with no clustering to 1.2513 with standard clustering, while prototype coverage goes 0.179 → 0.987 and weighted rank 61.6 → 15.4. Sparsity buys specificity (top-k = 8 gives weighted rank 5.3 versus 15.4 at k = 16) and a larger bank buys granularity (K = 4096 gives 8.1). Removing the prototype branch costs far more CE than removing the residual in every row, which is their evidence that the prototype path carries most of the computation on ordinary continuations — and simultaneously the honest caveat that "interpretable by design" here means "the readable channel is the load-bearing one *on this distribution*". Bolting prototypes onto a dense checkpoint works but weakly: the best finetuned variant reaches R1/R2 of 0.473/0.264 at prototype share 0.722, against 0.987/0.869 and 0.941 when trained from scratch at comparable CE. Native training also reorganises the backbone, not just the head — zero-overlap of active sets between sampled token pairs rises from 6.6% (dictionary control) to 26.8%, nearby final states share prototypes 1.365× more often than random pairs versus 1.006× for the dictionary, sparse reconstruction of the final state costs +0.262 CE versus +0.332 and +0.470, and hidden-state cosine similarity predicts final prototype overlap with AUC rising from 0.718 to 0.939 across late layers where the dense model goes 0.296 to 0.597. On attribution they get their headline: the local clustering stiffness raises the diagonal scale D_k, the normalised coupling matrix's condition number falls from 6.18e7 without clustering to 4.56e2 at the strongest setting on the same TinyStories setup, and the top-5% degree mass of the co-usage graph drops from 0.946 to 0.651; cached diagonal scoring keeps about 80% of the full prototype-space top-5% training sequences, cached scoring runs 470× faster than the tuned EK-FAC baseline (the authors' own framing is a 100–1000× regime), and full K×d prototype scoring without the cache is only about 2× faster than EK-FAC — so the win is caching sparse records, not the smaller subspace. Scaling: at 1.6B on FineWeb, PRISM matches the dense average at 10B tokens (48.95 vs 48.89) and reaches 49.94 at 20B; on Nemotron-CC it starts behind (47.32 vs 49.67 at 10B) and closes most of the gap by 50B tokens (49.75). Overhead is small — 26.2M extra parameters, 1.7% at XL, and 97.3% of GPT training throughput on one H200. The controller adds +2.72 mean points on the four non-binary multiple-choice tasks against +0.32 for an SAE controller built on the same checkpoint, with worked examples where the correction is legible as suppressing one distractor prototype and boosting another. NSFW suppression — 86 of 16,384 prototypes flagged by keyword-matching their vocabulary signatures, then down-weighted at α = −5.0 — drops the mean judge score from 0.668 to 0.154 with text quality flat (1.010 vs 1.003), and from 2.000 to 0.144 on the 555 pairs where the base model was maximally explicit.

## Limits and open problems they name
No ground-truth attribution anywhere: EK-FAC is a reference for overlap, not truth, and "500× faster" is faster at producing scores nobody has validated against leave-one-out retraining — they say retraining studies are the next step and that their own estimand is the trained model's local response, not the counterfactual retrained model. The coverage and specificity numbers come from TinyStories, a corpus small enough that a 1024-prototype bank can be inspected. Prototype labels are LLM-generated from top-logit signatures and nearest contexts, with a second LLM pass assigning coarse categories — a navigation aid the authors repeatedly call lightweight, and a clear re-entry point for the folder's complaint that a description is only as good as its distribution. The NSFW flagging is keyword-matching over vocabulary signatures and the authors admit it catches general gender/demographic prototypes; the judge is a single LLM with a 0–2 rubric. Curvature is treated under a frozen-support, frozen-winner approximation, so the theorems describe a local regime that the paper does not verify holds during training. Everything sits at the output interface: a prototype explains the last layer's mixture, not where the mixture came from — §7.3's "deep prototype" model with prototypes inside transformer blocks is explicitly future work. Residual share on the TinyStories ablations sits at 0.118–0.160 (with 0.142 in the no-clustering row), and the FineWeb/Nemotron scale-1.6B rows report residual reconstruction error of 0.0587–0.0756 rather than a share — so the fraction of the hidden state with no prototype-level explanation is a modest but non-trivial slice on the diagnostic setting and not directly comparable at scale.

## What we take from it
A framing and a warning, both shipped with numbers. The framing — the one we should steal — is **design the estimand into the architecture**: if we want attribution we can trust, the cheapest way to get it is a model whose prediction is a mixture over coordinates that are trained to stay near data, so that "which training examples" is answered by a sparse retrieval we can cache rather than by an inverse Hessian we cannot. Their symmetric clustering pair is the specific, cheap trick: a term pulling each unit to its nearest example and a term pulling each example to its nearest unit, both in the same forward pass, which is what turns a sparse dictionary into a grounded prototype bank — and their ablation shows the two directions are not interchangeable (R1-only reaches 0.473 coverage, nowhere near the symmetric 0.987). The warning is about what this buys us epistemically: **an interpretable channel can be interpretable and non-load-bearing at the same time** — the residual share and the "ΔCE no resid / no proto" pair are the two numbers that decide which, and PRISM only wins that test on TinyStories continuations. Instruments worth copying: weighted rank of the target among active units (a granularity metric that needs no labels), zero-overlap of active sets between sampled positions plus the nearby-state overlap ratio (a cheap test of whether a dictionary partitions the input space at all — 6.6% versus 26.8% is a large, training-attributable difference), and eval-loss-delta under sparse reconstruction substituted into the head (does the sparse code preserve *behaviour*, not just Euclidean error). Also worth copying as a habit: reporting an internal approximation ladder against a dense external baseline *and* a matched-cost control, because here the expensive-looking stage (prototype gradients) gave 2× while the boring one (caching) gave the 470×. Notes I add and mark as mine: the co-usage graph G_A is the interesting object nobody inspects — a prototype's influence after conditioning travels along shared usage, so our own prototype-style read-outs need a co-firing graph, not a per-unit list; the drop from κ = 6.18e7 to 4.56e2 is suspiciously large for a loss-term coefficient and would need the residual scale fixed to be comparable — the paper's own framing as "structured ridge" is the honest one, and a uniform ridge of matched scale should have been the third arm of that figure.
