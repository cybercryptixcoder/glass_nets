## What they wanted, and why

SAEs are the standard tool for pulling interpretable features out of network activations, but the set of features they learn changes from seed to seed and between hyperparameter settings, which makes any mechanistic reading of an individual latent unreliable. Earlier fixes are mostly post-hoc: regularise the dictionary, constrain the sparsity pattern, or align/average dictionaries after training. The authors want to remove the ambiguity *inside* the training objective instead, so that two runs land on the same features in the same places by construction rather than by repair. Their instrument is nesting: reconstruct the data using only a prefix of the latent dimensions, for every prefix, so that the dimension index itself carries meaning. The paper's bet is that a canonical index order is what makes per-feature claims reproducible, and that a Matryoshka-style hierarchy enforces that order only weakly because it samples a handful of group sizes and leaves features inside a group exchangeable.

## What they studied

Unit of analysis is the decoder atom (a dictionary column) together with its encoder row, i.e. the whole learned dictionary as an ordered object — not individual neurons of the language model. Models: Gemma-2 2B (layer 12, dictionary size 4096, 80 active per sample, activations from an uncopyrighted portion of the Pile) and Pythia-70M (layer 3, five seeds per model/dataset cell, 50M training tokens, reconstruction loss in the 0.02–0.03 range, on the Pile and on Dolma). Synthetic: a Gaussian toy model (input dimension 80, dictionary 100, sparsity 5, 100k samples, Zipf support prior with exponent 1.2, 10 seeds) and a Zipfian toy extension of the setup used by Song et al. (input dimension 16, 32 atoms, sparsity 3, 50k samples, 5 seeds).

## How they did it

Three objectives are compared on the same data. Standard TopK reconstruction uses all K atoms and keeps the top m codes per sample. The Matryoshka objective partitions the K atoms into a small number of nested groups and, each step, samples one group size and reconstructs using only atoms below that size — 5 groups (fixed or random) on the language-model runs, 8 groups / 4 truncations in the Zipfian toy run. The proposed Ordered SAE (OSAE) generalises this by treating *every* prefix as its own group: it takes the expectation of the prefix-truncated loss over a distribution supported on all indices from 1 to m, so every dimension is used deterministically rather than by sampling. Formally the loss keeps the top-m mask, then multiplies the code by a diagonal mask that zeroes everything past index ℓ, averages the squared reconstruction over ℓ drawn from a full-support distribution.

Theory. They first restate nested dropout in the undercomplete linear case, where the standard autoencoder loss identifies only the principal subspace and the nested version is known to pin the PCA eigenbasis in descending order. They extend it to overcomplete hard-sparse dictionary learning, assuming a unit-norm ground-truth dictionary with spark greater than twice the sparsity (so the sparse code is unique) and a ground-truth ordering of atoms by how often each is active. Lemma: any minimiser of the nested-dropout loss also minimises the full-prefix loss. Theorem: if additionally the true codes are nonnegative (killing sign ambiguity), any global minimiser of the nested loss equals the true dictionary and codes exactly — the permutation that uniqueness leaves free is pinned because only the identity preserves the assumed sparsity ordering.

Training practice. Gradients onto late indices shrink geometrically with the prefix distribution, so they add *unit sweeping*, borrowed from the nested-dropout paper: once a low-index unit has effectively converged, freeze its encoder row and decoder column (keeping it in the forward pass), unfreeze one more unit every T epochs after a short burn-in, and renormalise decoder columns after each freeze. They also warm up the truncation size from K down to the target m, which they say materially improves convergence for the ordered and Matryoshka arms.

Evaluation. Two metrics on two dictionaries D, D′ with unit-norm columns. *Stability*: maximal average cosine between matched atoms under a permutation, maximised with the Hungarian algorithm — the same reliability measure as Fel et al.'s archetypal-SAE paper. *Orderedness*: take the Hungarian permutation that matches D's atoms into D′, then Spearman-correlate that permutation with the identity index sequence; 1.0 means the two runs agree on order. On the toy model they report stability between the 45 seed pairs, stability against the ground truth, orderedness against the ground truth, and held-out MSE. On real models they report the same quantities as *prefix* curves — the metric computed using only the first p features — because the ordering claim is about the early, high-frequency part of the dictionary. A later appendix swaps decoder cosine for Pearson correlation between whole activation streams over the evaluation set, matched by Hungarian, to check that the picture is not an artefact of decoder geometry.

## What counts as an explanation here

Not a natural-language account of any feature, and not a circuit. The "explanation" is a *canonical ordering of the dictionary*: index j is claimed to mean "the j-th most frequently used atom", so a per-unit claim becomes portable across runs and hyperparameters without a matching step. The identifiability theorem is the idealised guarantee — exact recovery of ordered atoms under spark, nonnegativity and a correct ordering prior — and the paper is explicit that real runs get a *partial* version of that: order agreement on early indices, not exact recovery. It predicts something concrete and checkable: agreement between runs should be high exactly where the index is low, and it should decay along the index.

## How they judged success

Stability (mean matched cosine under Hungarian matching, either between seeds or against ground truth), orderedness (Spearman correlation between the matching permutation and index order), both computed at truncated prefix lengths; global held-out reconstruction MSE; and the novel-feature percentage from SAE stitching — take a latent from a 65536-wide SAE, splice it into the corresponding 4096-wide SAE, and count whether reconstruction improves (novel, the small SAE missed it), worsens (redundant), or is unchanged. Confidence bands are 95% intervals over seed pairs (36 pairs per method on Gemma-2; 10 pairs on Pythia; 45 pairs on the toy model) and over seeds against ground truth. In the Zipfian appendix they add a frequency-invariant per-feature reconstruction error (FIFR), which macro-averages the relative error of each feature's contribution over the samples where that feature is truly active, aligned by Hungarian matching on normalised atoms.

## What they found

On the Gaussian toy model, OSAE is better on all four reported numbers: stability between seeds 0.664 (0.0191) against vanilla 0.572 (0.0096), fixed Matryoshka 0.538 (0.0114) and random Matryoshka 0.531 (0.0150); stability against ground truth 0.814 (0.0195) against 0.479 / 0.502 / 0.480; orderedness against ground truth 0.734 (0.0758) against roughly zero for vanilla and 0.119 / 0.054 for the Matryoshka arms; and reconstruction loss 0.00725 (0.00075) against 0.0257 / 0.0339 / 0.0309. The authors say they were surprised that the added inductive bias *lowered* error, and re-ran a Song et al.-style Zipfian sweep to check it without hyperparameter bias, where the ordering is: OSAE keeps consistency comparable to the strongest baselines (stability near 0.9 at low skew for OSAE, TopK and Matryoshka alike), gains positive orderedness (Spearman around 0.5 against ground truth at low skew), and pays with higher global MSE.

On Gemma-2 2B, OSAE gives the highest orderedness across prefix lengths, approaching an average of about 0.8, but overall stability is *lower* than the Matryoshka baselines; the paper's reading is that the significant early features are more stable while stability falls sharply for later indices, crossing under random Matryoshka somewhere between 1024 and 2048 features. Fixed Matryoshka's orderedness sits near zero for the first 128 features — the size of its first group — then jumps to around 0.5, which is a direct picture of what group sampling leaves unconstrained. They note OSAE's reconstruction loss is slightly worse here, and that they compare checkpoints matched on loss to keep the comparison fair.

On Pythia-70M, the same pattern holds on both the Pile and Dolma: OSAE leads on orderedness after prefix 128; on stability it beats random Matryoshka early and crosses over around prefix length 128, while fixed Matryoshka has higher stability but lower orderedness. Cross-dataset runs (one SAE on Pile activations, one on Dolma) lose about 0.1–0.2 relative to same-dataset runs but keep the ordering. The sharpest control is same-seed initialisation across datasets: there OSAE reaches orderedness near 1.0 and full-prefix stability of 0.8 while trained on different data — and the appendix's checkpoint-versus-initialisation curves show random Matryoshka drifting *away* from its initial dictionary over training where OSAE barely moves, which the authors offer as the mechanism (late indices are updated less).

Stitching: stitching a 65536-wide latent into 4096-wide dictionaries, the novel-feature fraction is 73.8% for BatchTopK, 52.7% for random Matryoshka and 33.8% for OSAE, with the reverse "no MSE change" fraction 5.0% / 35.9% / 1.4% — so OSAE leaves fewer genuinely missing features, with the caveat the authors state that random Matryoshka has more non-activating features at the sample size tested.

## Limits and open problems they name

The guarantees need spark, nonnegativity and a *correct* ordering prior; if the imposed order is misspecified the objective over-regularises and suppresses valid alternative bases — which is the deep problem, since the order is imposed by frequency rather than discovered. Cost is higher because covering all prefixes takes more compute. Results are sensitive to the prefix distribution and to unit sweeping. The empirical study is deliberately about mechanism, not a benchmark sweep over tasks or architectures. They also flag unexplained behaviour during training where early-prefix orderedness and stability fall while late-prefix measures rise, which they guess is a distribution or over-training artefact and say needs more ablations.

## What we take from it

A method, a measure and a warning. Method: if we want per-unit status to be comparable across runs without post-hoc matching, impose a canonical order during fitting and *check that the order survives* — a training-time constraint is cheaper and stronger than an alignment step after the fact (compare the Procrustes paper, run 586, where post-hoc rotation did nothing and conditioning helped). Measure: the *orderedness* metric (Spearman between the matched permutation and index) is a genuinely new axis beside set overlap and behavioural correlation — it scores the run for agreeing on *which slot a unit occupies*, and the folder has never scored that before. It also makes a prediction we can use: agreement should decay along the index, and that decay curve is a diagnostic of how much of the dictionary is actually canonical. Warning: their order comes from an external prior (activation frequency), so a high orderedness score partly measures agreement with a statistic we chose, and the paper's own real-model tables show orderedness up while *overall* stability is down against a baseline — so ordering improves the portability of the declaration without necessarily improving the units themselves. Cross-references: run 583's three-claims split (stability, agreement on firing, survival across resamples) — orderedness is a fourth claim, agreement on position; and the FIFR error in Appendix B.4 is another entry in the folder's denominator thread: global MSE is dominated by frequent features and can look fine while rare features are badly fit, exactly the "headline improves as the tail degrades" coupling that run 586 named.
