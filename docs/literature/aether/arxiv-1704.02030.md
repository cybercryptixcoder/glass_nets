# Using stacking to average Bayesian predictive distributions

- **Key:** arxiv-1704.02030
- **Authors / organisation:** Yuling Yao, Aki Vehtari, Daniel Simpson, Andrew Gelman — Columbia University (Statistics; also Political Science for Gelman), Aalto University (Helsinki Institute for Information Technology, Computer Science), University of Toronto (Statistical Sciences)
- **Date:** 2017-09-16 (v3; v1 2017-04-06)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.02030
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
There are usually several candidate accounts of the same data and none of them is the truth. Averaging them by posterior probability — the textbook Bayesian answer — assumes the truth is inside the candidate list and, more quietly, assumes the prior on each candidate's internal free parameters is meaningful, because the marginal likelihood that produces the averaging weight is dominated by that prior rather than by fit. So a user who widens a flat prior by a factor of ten divides that candidate's weight by roughly ten without any change in the data. The authors want an averaging rule whose weights are chosen by how well the *combined* prediction does on held-out material, so that the weights cannot be bought with prior mass. The larger goal is what to do after several separately-fitted accounts already exist — the practical situation whenever the accounts come from different frameworks or different fitting machinery — and they are explicit that a proper joint "super-model" would be preferred when it can be built, but usually cannot.

## What they studied
No neural models: this is the methodological paper under the whole predictive-weighting lineage. The objects are sets of already-fitted Bayesian models, K in number, which may be parametric regressions, nonparametric densities, or the same model fitted several times from different starting points. Their unit of analysis is the model set plus the data point, and the quantity they care about is the combined posterior predictive density evaluated on a held-out point. Evidence is three simulation families (a Gaussian location mixture with eight fixed candidate means under non-overlapping truth; linear subset regressions in both an M-open and an M-closed arrangement with fifteen candidate univariate/telescoping regressions; a Cauchy two-parameter posterior approximated by variational inference from two distant initial values) plus two real datasets (US Senate voting, n = 94, with two competing measures of the same latent ideological quantity; arsenic well-switching in Bangladesh, n = 3020, with eight logistic, interaction and spline specifications).

## How they did it
Two stages, and the whole method is the choice of what each stage optimises. First they extract, for every candidate and every data point, the leave-one-out predictive density — the probability of that point under the candidate fitted without it — approximated with Pareto-smoothed importance sampling from a single full fit per candidate, with exact refits substituted wherever the importance-ratio tail shape diagnostic exceeds the conventional threshold. Second they choose the non-negative weights summing to one by maximising the average log of the *weighted sum* of the per-point leave-one-out densities: a convex combination of predictive distributions, fitted by leave-one-out, so the weights are the ones that made the mixture predict best and nothing else. Fitting happens inside the log, which is what makes this "stacking of predictive distributions" rather than "stacking of means" (the classic squared-error stacking, which they keep as a baseline and note is the case of a non-strict score). As a cheap alternative they build pseudo-BMA weights from the same leave-one-out numbers — exponentiating each candidate's total — and then a stabilised version that takes the mean of the exponentials computed under Bayesian-bootstrap resamplings of the per-point values, which keeps the weights from collapsing onto one candidate purely because the point count is finite. The stabilised version also seeds the optimiser. They also apply the same machinery to a different object: several approximate posteriors from variational fits with different initialisations, stacked to patch the local-optimum problem.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not a mechanism account at all: the output is a *weight vector over account sets*, plus the combined predictive distribution. Their claim about its status is the transferable part. Stacking weights are declared the M-open projection of the "actual belief" onto the span of the candidate set — that is, the best the convex hull of candidates can do against future data, with no claim that any member is true and no claim that the weights correspond to posterior probability. They are emphatic that the weights are *not* a belief statement, which is exactly why they cannot be inflated by prior choice; the probabilistic reading is given up deliberately in exchange for the weights being about prediction.

## How they judged success
The metric is out-of-sample expected log predictive density, computed on freshly drawn test data under the known generator, over repeated simulations (they report 500 repeats with 200 test points in the mixture arm, 100 repeats with 200 test points in the regression arms), so the comparison is against the generator rather than against a criterion the method itself optimised. Seven methods are compared on the same weights-free footing: predictive-distribution stacking, mean stacking, pseudo-BMA, stabilised pseudo-BMA, best single candidate by leave-one-out, best single candidate by marginal likelihood, and BMA. Controls worth naming: an M-closed arm where one candidate *is* the truth, a correlated-candidate arm that sweeps candidate redundancy, a duplicate-candidate arm that adds identical members to the list, and a comparison against a fully Bayesian mixture model that estimates weights and parameters jointly.

## What they found
The four results that hold up regardless of how one reads the rest. (1) On the correlated/duplicate arm, adding another copy of a candidate doubles its BMA weight and degrades BMA's prediction, while stacking does not move — the weights depend on the span the candidates cover, not on how many times a candidate is listed. (2) Bias towards the single closest candidate is what BMA does asymptotically, and the M-open arm shows it is not merely suboptimal in the limit: pseudo-BMA and BMA both fall behind, and among the selection rules, selecting by marginal likelihood fails even in the M-closed arm where selecting by leave-one-out recovers the full model, because the marginal-likelihood ranking is being decided by the parameter priors. (3) Stabilised pseudo-BMA dominates plain pseudo-BMA and is usually within touching distance of stacking, which is why it is offered as the cheap substitute and the optimiser's starting point. (4) The mixture-model comparison is against a heavier computation and the mixture loses on small samples and at high candidate correlation, taking markedly longer to run — a two-step fit is not just a convenience there, it is more stable.

## Limits and open problems they name
They name four. Small samples make the whole procedure unreliable, and they attribute it to the leave-one-out surrogate rather than to the weights: they report the bias of the leave-one-out estimate growing as the ratio of effective parameters to sample size grows, and state as an empirical rule that the approximation is poor once the sample is smaller than about five times the parameter count — below that, weights are noisy and stacking is visibly unstable. Convex combination is itself an assumption, and in the convolution example no mixture of the candidates can recover the truth even in principle; they defend it only by saying that a large enough candidate list should make the hull close enough, which is an argument about coverage they do not test. Combining separately-fitted models pools no information between fits, so the method is at its best precisely when the candidates have nothing in common — the opposite of the case where a joint model is available. And the hyperparameter for stabilisation (how much the point-count uncertainty is allowed to pull the weights away from the extremes) is left as a direction for strong prior structure in high dimensions rather than settled.

## What we take from it
The canonical warning for every weighted account ensemble in this folder: **a weight is only as honest as the criterion it was fitted to, and every other route to a weight is a route for something else to buy influence.** Here the alternative route is prior mass on nuisance parameters; in our setting it will be probe count, discovery-procedure thresholds, or the arbitrary scale of a screening score. Concretely: (a) fit account weights to a held-out prediction criterion, directly on the combined object, never to a per-account score that is then exponentiated; (b) the exponential-of-total-scores family is not neutral — it is BMA with a different normaliser, it collapses to the closest account in the limit, and it is sensitive to how many near-duplicate accounts are listed, so it is a fallback to be declared as such and never the default; (c) print the duplicate-account sensitivity test — list the same account twice and see whether the weights move, which is a one-line falsification of "my weighting is about coverage, not membership"; (d) print the sample-size-to-parameter ratio next to any leave-one-out-fitted weight, because the paper's own rule of thumb (poor below five to one) is the cleanest quantitative statement in the folder of when a held-out criterion stops meaning anything; (e) when a set of approximate fits disagree, stacking over them is a legitimate way to average out optimiser/initialisation noise, with the caveat that it averages *approximations*, and the paper shows it improves on each single fit without recovering the truth. The warning about a proper joint model is equally usable in reverse: if our accounts share structure, the stacked ensemble is the wrong construction and the shared part should be fitted once.
