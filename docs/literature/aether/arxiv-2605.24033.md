## What they wanted, and why

Circuits are found and then argued for with examples, ablations and prose, which leaves a gap between a plausible circuit and a demonstrated claim about what it computes. The author wants that gap closed by turning circuit claims into bounded propositions given to a solver, so each comes back as either a proof over a declared domain or a specific input on which it fails. The framing point is that Transformers are engineered artifacts: instead of only building better post-hoc tools for existing models, one can design architectures whose claims are cheap to state and check. The scope is deliberately small — a finite task domain, a projection onto a handful of candidate tokens, zero ablation — and the author says so repeatedly rather than letting the reader infer it. It matters to us because our deliverable is a predictor that should come with provable claims about where it holds, and this is the shape of that deliverable, not just of another circuit paper.

## What they studied

Two regimes. (i) Small models: a decoder-only Transformer of roughly 8k parameters on a custom 32-token vocabulary, trained multitask until both tasks reach 100% candidate-token accuracy, on two symbolic sequence tasks with 128 exhaustively enumerable sequences each — quote closing (single versus double quote) and bracket type (square versus curly delimiter). (ii) GPT-2-small scale: the same operator stack trained on OpenWebText, evaluated on OpenWebText and WikiText-103. The unit of analysis is an **extracted circuit edge set on a coarse residual-stream graph** (embeddings, attention outputs, MLP outputs, logits), with the target behaviour reduced to `argmax` over a task-specific candidate-token set rather than equality of the full vocabulary distribution.

## How they did it

Step by step. A behaviour, a finite domain and a candidate-token set are fixed by the researcher; deleted edges in the circuit receive zero contribution (zero-ablation semantics, stated as part of the claim). Four properties are then written as first-order sentences over the domain, and the verifier negates each and hands it to Z3: projected functional equivalence (circuit decision equals a symbolic reference program on every input), edge necessity (for every retained edge, some input exists where removing that edge changes the decision — explicitly *not* global minimality), task-relevant invariance (two inputs related by a declared irrelevant-variation relation get the same decision), and final-residual robustness (the decision cannot flip under any ℓ∞ perturbation of the final residual within ε). Branchy operators are handled with branch certificates: if the traced branch cannot be certified stable, the query returns unknown rather than a proof. Because standard components resist exact encoding, the author builds an SMT-friendly operator stack and trains it — Signed L1 BandNorm in place of LayerNorm (per-side L1 mass projected onto a ball, so scale control is a threshold over sorted coordinates rather than a division), sparsemax in place of softmax, LeakyReLU in place of GELU — and runs a trainability ablation at GPT-2 scale against a locally trained GPT-2 baseline plus three variants (sparsemax only, BandNorm only, full stack). Finally, when a circuit cannot be encoded tractably, they fit an SMT-encodable ReLU surrogate to the circuit over the domain, search a small DSL of counters, toggles and threshold detectors for a symbolic program, and ask the solver for an input where program and surrogate disagree.

## What counts as an explanation here

A circuit plus an ablation rule plus a **bounded proposition**, with the guarantee carried by a chain of links rather than by a score: circuit fidelity to the model, surrogate fidelity to the circuit, program equivalence to the surrogate. The explanatory object is partial in three declared ways (finite domain, candidate-token projection, retained-edge necessity only) and the author treats those restrictions as what makes the claim checkable rather than as compromises. It predicts, but only inside the declared box — and when the box is exhausted and the claim fails, the output is a counterexample, which the paper argues is itself a usable interpretability artifact.

## How they judged success

Solver status per property per task (unsat = proven, sat = refuted with a witness, plus "unknown" when branch certificates fail), preceded by an exhaustive PyTorch enumeration of the controlled circuit over the domain to catch encoder/implementation mismatches. Trainability is judged by OpenWebText validation loss and WikiText-103 perplexity against a locally trained GPT-2 baseline under an identical recipe, with the choice of local rather than released-GPT-2 baseline explained. Surrogate-mediated claims are judged by three separate agreement numbers reported side by side: agreement on the finite fitting dataset, surrogate–teacher agreement over the bounded abstract domain, and program equivalence over that domain.

## What they found

Direct verification succeeds end to end on both small tasks: a 3-edge quote circuit and a 6-edge bracket circuit pass PyTorch validation and are verified for equivalence, invariance, edge necessity and final-residual robustness, with the robustness query quantifying over continuous residuals rather than a token list. Trainability is where the architecture pays: sparsemax alone costs +0.0633 OpenWebText loss and +2.74 WikiText-103 perplexity over a local baseline at 3.1340 / 52.98; BandNorm alone costs +0.1840 and +8.91; the full stack +0.1960 and +9.12, so the normalization replacement carries almost the whole gap and the author compares it unfavourably to published LayerNorm-removal results on pretrained GPT-2. GPT-2-scale direct verification is not attempted as a result — bilinear QK products, attention-weighted values, sparsemax support branching, BandNorm branches and graph density are named as the blockers. The surrogate-mediated route produces the paper's sharpest result: the quote task reaches 100% agreement on the finite dataset *and* 100% surrogate–teacher agreement, and the solver still refutes every program in the template family, returning a concrete three-token input where model and program disagree; the bracket task admits a program that is certified equivalent over the bounded domain.

## Limits and open problems they name

Twelve, and they are unusually explicit: all guarantees are domain-bounded; the small-model equivalence number is itself exhaustively testable so SMT earns its keep only on the existential edge witnesses and the continuous robustness query; verification is over projected decisions, not logits; edge necessity is not minimality; GPT-2-scale SMT is intractable; sparsemax attention is not piecewise-affine once QK products are symbolic; BandNorm is not competitive; surrogate-mediated guarantees inherit surrogate fidelity and are only as strong as its validation (exhaustive versus sampled is a real difference in claim strength); the DSL search is complete only over the chosen template family, so failure to find a program rules nothing out; the domain and projection are supplied by the researcher, so the method does not discover what the circuit represents; proofs are about the exported exact real/rational encoding, not PyTorch floating point; branch-certified queries can return unknown. Mine, logged as mine: there is **no unverified-model control** — the small models are trained to 100% task accuracy, so nothing shows whether the same procedure would certify a circuit in a network that does not implement the behaviour; and there is no planted-circuit arm where the true mechanism is known and the extraction is deliberately given a wrong candidate set. No figure inspected: Figures 1–3 are images, so the two verification-path diagrams and the two delta plots are recorded only as the text describes them, and every number here is a table cell or an explicit sentence.

## What we take from it

Five things. (i) **The claim-first discipline, as an output format**: state domain, projection, ablation semantics and property, then report proven / refuted-with-witness / unknown. That triple is strictly more informative than any faithfulness score this folder records, and "unknown" being a legal answer is the part most interpretability reporting lacks. (ii) **The counterexample as the deliverable of a failed claim** — a 100%-on-the-dataset explanation refuted by one solver-found input is the cheapest possible demonstration that a description has not been tested, and it is the same lesson as the folder's induce-and-repair and matched-negative entries, delivered mechanically. (iii) **The decomposition of a guarantee into links**, each with its own strength, is the honest way to publish a claim that passes through a surrogate — and it names the weak link out loud rather than averaging it away. (iv) **A warning about buying verifiability with architecture**: the price here is 0.18–0.20 validation loss for the normalization swap, i.e. the interpretable-by-design route can cost model quality outright, and the paper's own comparison says removal from pretrained weights currently beats replacement in training. (v) **A design rule for our predictor**: if a prediction is to be solver-checkable we need a finite declared domain (small candidate sets, template families, bounded lengths) — which is exactly the regime our probing setup already lives in, so the cheapest next step for us is to write our claims in this form on the domains we already test on, not to wait for GPT-2-scale tractability.
