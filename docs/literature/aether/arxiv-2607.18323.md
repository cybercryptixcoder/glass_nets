## What they wanted, and why
Almost every method this folder reads has the same inner loop: change one site of the computation, re-evaluate, measure, restore, move to the next site. Doing that exhaustively is what makes patch sweeps, circuit searches and systematic ablations expensive, and the field's usual answer is to make the loop approximate — score all sites from a gradient instead of running them. This paper takes the opposite route: accept exactness, and account for what the loop actually costs. It starts from a single-mutation locality theorem on the authors' own reactive graph engine — a mutation invalidates exactly the mutated node's downstream reachable set — and asks what an *entire workload* of mutations costs, so that an exact sweep can be budgeted instead of avoided. The larger goal is modest but genuine: turn "recomputation cost" from an assumption into a closed form, and mark precisely where the exact route stops being cheaper than the approximate one.

## What they studied
Units are **sites in a computational graph** (nodes or edges of the DAG, not neurons or features): the graph itself, its depth L, a per-layer cost weight, and each site's downstream and upstream reachable sets. Models: a reference engine (NeuroDSL, Julia, CPU, Float32) on synthetic layered graphs of roughly 4,000 nodes, plus — as an extension at the end — two Llama-style GPU transformers at 12 layers/12 heads/width 768 and 24 layers/16 heads/width 1024, each swept exhaustively over every attention-head output and every MLP output (156 and 408 sites). The object of study is cost, counted in nodes invalidated and in wall-clock milliseconds; no behaviour and no interpretation of any unit is ever measured.

## How they did it
Four steps. (1) **Sweep, restored:** for an exhaustive loop over all sites of a layered graph, the paper defines the ratio of (sites × independent full recomputations) to (sum over sites of the exact cone cost), and derives its limit as depth grows, under the assumption that per-layer weight grows like a power of depth with exponent q and a slowly varying factor. The derivation sums the suffix weights by Riemann integration; the mirror case (weight heavy near the input) follows by re-indexing depth. A wall-clock refinement adds a fixed per-site overhead term proportional to graph size, measured as two constants on a 412-node model, and takes the ratio of leading terms. (2) **Sequential and batched grafts:** for a sequence of mutations that are *never undone*, each followed by a re-evaluation, the paper proves by induction that each graft's cost is its original cone plus its own fresh nodes plus the fresh blocks of every earlier graft whose site lies inside it; the total is therefore the isolated sum plus an overcount summed over *comparable site pairs* (pairs where one site is downstream of the other), with extreme values over insertion orders. If instead all grafts are applied before one re-evaluation, the count is the size of the *union* of the cones plus all fresh nodes, independent of order. (3) **Backward pass:** the locality theorem is mirrored by reversing every edge — a mutation affects the gradient of exactly the nodes that can reach it — and the authors note that on an architecture where every node of an earlier layer is an ancestor of every node of a later layer (a plain sequential stack, no long skips), the affected set is the whole graph minus the untouched part of the site's own layer; substituting gives a sweep ratio that tends to one. (4) **Validation:** synthetic layered graphs at depths 20 to 320 with four power-law cost profiles in both orientations for the asymptotic claims; a graph with genuine intra-layer width (parallel branches folded pairwise, so siblings are neither ancestors nor descendants) for the training-mode collapse; a 400-node chain with six grafts of twenty fresh nodes each under three insertion orders plus a batched arm for the exact identities; and an exhaustive head-plus-MLP sweep on the two GPU transformers, where the cone size is available in closed form from the layer count, head count and per-layer node count.

## What counts as an explanation here
Three **closed-form cost identities**, not an explanation of a network. Each is an exact combinatorial statement about how many values must be recomputed for a given intervention programme: a limit family indexed by one number that describes where computation sits along depth; an exact overcount term attached to *pairs of sites*; a union formula for batched edits; and a collapse-to-one result for the backward pass. The claims are complete within their model (a layered graph whose cone equals a depth suffix) and explicitly partial outside it. They predict cost, and only cost — nothing here predicts an effect on outputs, and the paper is careful that the identities govern recomputation, not behaviour. Their one predictive-looking quantity, the sweep ratio, is a read-out of a structural property of the graph (how weight is distributed across depth), which is a narrower thing than an account of a mechanism.

## How they judged success
Two standards, matched to the claim type, and stated as such. **Exact identities** (sequential, batched, mirrored backward locality) are checked at **zero tolerance** on integer invalid-node counts — not "close", exactly equal — across three insertion orders, 18 per-graft measurements plus both order extremes and the batched total, and the two bugs found while getting there are reported. **Asymptotic limits** are checked by **convergence across depth** (five depths per profile) against the predicted value, with the raw measured number printed in the same cell as the prediction. For the transformer extension the standard is a **closed-form cone size against a measured cone** at every one of 564 sites (zero residual) and a **wall-clock ratio against a purely combinatorial prediction** with no timing input, with the agreement quoted as a percentage and the *sign* of the residual used as a diagnostic: a small residual that flips sign between the two configurations is read as per-call noise, whereas a systematic overhead drag would depress both. The final wall-clock test is explicitly framed as nullifying a previously derived ceiling (≈1.79) once the ratio of fixed overhead to per-node cost becomes small.

## What they found
The uniform 2× speedup that reactive engines are usually credited with is **one point in a one-parameter family**, not a constant. With per-layer weight growing like a power of depth with exponent q, measured near the output the sweep ratio converges to (q+2)/(q+1) — 2.0000 measured against 2.0000 predicted at q=0, 1.6722 against 1.6667 at q=0.5, 1.5064 against 1.5000 at q=1, 1.3471 against 1.3333 at q=2 — while with the weight near the input it converges upward, 2.0057, 2.5057, 3.0004, 3.9247 against 2, 2.5, 3 and 4. The wall-clock ceiling computed from the measured interpreter constants is about 1.79, but the transformer extension shows that ceiling is **not binding at scale**: a closed-form cone size reproduces all 564 measured cones with zero residual, the purely combinatorial prediction is 2.145 and 2.073, and the measured wall-clock ratios are 2.121 and 2.101 — within 1.14% and 1.33%, on opposite sides of the prediction. Sequence persistence has an exact price: shallowest-first grafting costs the isolated sum exactly (overcount 0), deepest-first costs the maximum (300 with uniform blocks of 20 over six sites), a random order costs 140, and all 18 per-graft counts match the closed form; batching collapses all three orders to the same 463, against an isolated sum of 1,320, with the union of cones at 343 plus 120 fresh nodes. Under backpropagation the ratio collapses toward one — 1.0526 at depth 10 down to 1.0031 at depth 160 — at a rate independent of the cost profile that generated the whole q-family. The bookkeeping question the authors say has no theorem (does per-mutation rewiring drift with history at fixed graph size?) is **measured and comes out negative at this scale**: 0.033 to 0.036 ms across halves of a growing chain, and 0.0243 ms in both halves of a constant-size control.

## Limits and open problems they name
The aggregate results are about **recomputation counts**, not time; wall-clock enters only through two constants measured on one engine, one precision and one graph size, and the two GPU configurations locate where the overhead stops mattering for this engine on this hardware rather than generally. The layered model assumes each site's cone is a depth suffix, which **long skip connections violate** — such architectures fall outside the closed forms (the per-site sequential identity still holds, since it assumes no layering). The training-mode collapse is proved for architectures where layers are fully ordered by ancestry, and the **intermediate regime with some long skips — where the ratio sits strictly between one and the forward-only limit — is stated as open**. The bookkeeping-drift measurement is one run of 100 mutations at one size trajectory, reported as absence of detected drift at that scale, not as a bound. And the paper's motivating alternative, gradient-based screening, is acknowledged to buy a far larger reduction than any factor derived here, at the cost of exactness; the paper does not attempt to price the false negatives that switching to it introduces.

## What we take from it
Mostly a **budgeting rule and a boundary marker**, with one genuinely transferable measurement discipline.

(1) **A workload-level cost claim is not a per-call constant times the call count.** The sweep ratio being a function of a single structural index of the graph is the cleanest statement in the folder that "we can afford to verify everything exactly" is a claim about the *shape* of the model as much as about the engine: the same engine gives you a 1.35× saving on one weight profile and a 3.9× saving on another. Mechanically: when our account reports the cost of an exhaustive verification pass, it reports it as a measured number for *our* models under *our* profile, with the profile's shape stated, and never as N × per-call cost.

(2) **Persistence is where exactness gets expensive, and batching is the free fix — but only if nothing in the pair is comparable.** The exact overcount summed over comparable site pairs, and the fact that batched application costs the union regardless of order, gives us a concrete procedural rule for multi-site interventions, which is exactly the shape our own work takes when we patch several units at once: **apply multi-site edits batched rather than one-at-a-time-with-a-measurement-between, and where a running measurement is needed, order sites upstream-first so the overcount is zero**; and **report the pairwise comparability structure of the site set** (how many pairs are ancestor-descendant) next to the cost, because that number, not the site count, is what the cost depends on.

(3) **A derived overhead ceiling is a claim about a ratio, and it must be re-tested in the regime it was not measured in.** The 1.79 ceiling is derived honestly from measured constants and then *dissolved* by measurement at transformer scale — the authors' own criterion is that the residual flips sign, which distinguishes noise from a systematic drag. This is the transferable measurement discipline: **when we derive a correction term from constants measured in one regime, we (a) state the ratio the correction depends on, not the constants, and (b) test the correction in a second regime and use the sign of the residual, not its magnitude, to decide whether the term is still active.** A correction that only depresses in one direction cannot be noise, and a correction that changes sign between configurations is not doing work.

(4) **Localisation of the exact sweep to inference only is a hard boundary for our method choice.** The backward-locality mirror and the collapse to one mean the exact route is cheap precisely where the approximate route is also unavailable at scale: gradient screening needs a backward pass, and under a backward pass the reactive advantage is gone on sequential architectures. The practical warning for us: **frame the exact-verification budget as an inference-time budget and do not silently attribute it to a training-time or gradient-assisted workflow** — the two regimes have different cost structures and the same claim cannot straddle them.

**No matched-unit baseline is involved anywhere**, which is what run 575 asked of this paper and what it cannot deliver: the units here are graph nodes and the only quantity measured is recomputation count. Its contribution is upstream of the folder's standing thread — it tells us what it costs to check every unit exactly, which is the precondition for the repeated-resample and held-out-unit audits runs 567, 574 and 575 have all demanded.
