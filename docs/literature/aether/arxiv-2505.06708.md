- **Key:** arxiv-2505.06708

- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Gating is everywhere in modern sequence models — recurrent cells, state-space layers, linear attention, and by now softmax attention itself — but the field has largely carried it as inherited design rather than as an explained component. The authors' complaint is specific: where gating appears bundled with something else (a top-k head routing scheme, a sparse attention pattern), the published gains cannot be assigned to the gate at all. They want to know what a gate in a standard transformer attention layer actually does, and to say so in terms of quantities that can be measured, not in terms of the intuition it inherited. The larger goal is the one this folder keeps meeting: an architectural addition that is reported as a bundle cannot be explained, and cannot be ported to a model whose heads we want to read.

## What they studied
Fifteen-billion-parameter mixture-of-experts models (2.54B activated, 128 experts, top-8) and 1.7B dense models, trained on up to 3.5 trillion tokens with 4096-token contexts and grouped-query attention. Thirty gating variants in total, enumerated along five axes: where the gate sits (after the query, key or value projection, after the scaled-dot-product attention output, after the final output projection), how fine-grained the score is (one scalar per head, or a vector per element), whether heads share the gate, multiplicative versus additive, and sigmoid versus SiLU. The unit of analysis is therefore a *position in the block*, with heads, and secondarily head outputs, as the things being modulated.

## How they did it
The core comparison is a ladder of trained models in which only the gating configuration changes, and it is deliberately run against parameter-matched alternatives rather than against the bare baseline. For each gating row they add extra key-value heads, extra query heads, or extra experts until the added parameters meet or exceed what the gate costs, so a win cannot be a capacity win. Having found the winning position they then run a diagnostic series on it: replace the sigmoid with a rescaled variant that keeps the non-linearity but forbids small scores; drop the input-dependence by zero-initialising the gate to a learned per-head constant; share one score across heads; and substitute an RMSNorm at the same position as a parameter-free non-linearity. They measure three things across these variants — average gate score, the largest hidden-state activation per layer, and the share of attention mass the first token receives — and read the pattern across the three together. Stability is tested by pushing maximum learning rate and batch size up on a 48-layer 1.7B model and recording which runs diverge, with sandwich normalisation and hard clipping as comparison stabilisers. Context length is extended by raising the RoPE base and continuing for 80B tokens at 32k, then YaRN to 128k, scored on RULER.

## What counts as an explanation here
A mechanism statement of two named parts — a non-linearity inserted into a path that was otherwise a single low-rank linear map, and input-dependent sparsity in the modulation score — each supported by its own ablation, plus a measured consequence (the sink proportion). It is explicitly partial: the authors build the non-linearity arm on an algebraic rewrite showing that the value projection composed with the output projection collapses into one rank-limited map shared further under grouped-query attention, so any non-linearity placed between them raises expressiveness; and the sparsity arm is supported by swapping in a non-sparse gate rather than by a proof. Nothing here predicts a specific head's behaviour; it predicts that a gate at one position should suppress a global statistic and that a gate elsewhere should not.

## How they judged success
Held-out perplexity averaged over six domains, plus Hellaswag, MMLU, GSM8k, HumanEval, C-eval and CMMLU in few-shot mode; and separately, training stability (divergence or not under a raised learning rate) and RULER scores at six context lengths. The controls are the parameter-expansion baselines, the non-sparse and input-independent gates, the head-shared gate, and the parameter-free RMSNorm at the same position. Gate-score mean, per-layer maximum activation, and first-token attention share are the intermediate measurements that carry the mechanism claim. Wall-clock cost of the gate is reported as under 2% latency.

## What they found
The single best position is a head-specific elementwise sigmoid gate applied to the attention output, before the output projection: with 400B tokens on the 15A2B mixture-of-experts model it reaches average perplexity 5.761 against 6.026 for the baseline, with MMLU 60.82 against 58.79, while every parameter-expansion baseline stays above 5.95 — and the same row costs 201M added parameters, so the comparison is not outright unfair to capacity but is not decisive on its own. A headwise gate at the same position costs 1.6M and recovers most of the gain (5.792 perplexity, MMLU 60.05), which is the paper's most striking cheapness result. Gating after the value projection is second (5.820) and gating after the query, the key or the output projection lands close to baseline. Head-shared gating at the winning position is worse than head-specific (5.801 with 201M parameters, versus 5.761 with the same parameter count head-specific), additive gating is worse than multiplicative, and SiLU is worse than sigmoid. Downstream of the winning choice, the diagnostic series separates the two named factors: a parameter-free RMSNorm at the same position also cuts perplexity (5.847), which is the non-linearity arm with no gate at all; an input-independent sigmoid gate raises the mean score to 0.335 and sits at 5.917; and the non-sparse sigmoid, whose scores are confined above one half, lands at 5.900 with the first-token attention share back at 0.451 against 0.048 for the sparse gate. So the non-linearity arm moves perplexity on its own, and only the sparse query-dependent arm moves the sink. On the stability side, the baseline at a doubled learning rate on the 48-layer model fails to converge while the gated model trains and improves; sandwich normalisation also restores convergence but with negligible gain, and hard clipping at 300 or 100 does not restore it at all. In the long-context extension, the gated model reaches 58.82 on RULER at 128k against 31.65 for the baseline, and 72.88 against 37.94 at 32k after the RoPE change. Appendix A.1 contains the finding the authors themselves call the reason for the paper: in the switch-head family, reducing the routing to a single expert — that is, deleting the selection mechanism entirely and leaving only the sigmoid on the value output — gives the best result of that family on both perplexity and benchmarks, so the routing was never the load-bearing part.

## Limits and open problems they name
They state that the effect of non-linearity on attention dynamics and on training overall is under-explored, and that they have no rigorous account of why removing the sink helps length extension — the proposed story, that a gated model regulates information flow through the gate rather than through a sink, is a hypothesis. The paper's own measurements show the mechanism claim is not clean in either direction: value-position gating removes the massive activations but leaves the sink, so massive activations are not necessary for a sink; and the deep connection between sparsity, massive activations and sink proportion is left to future work, with the caveat that everything is measured at 1.7B and 15B scale. Appendix A.5 concedes a related point: clipping the attention and feed-forward outputs into the residual does not prevent divergence at the higher learning rate, so the stability gain is not simply "keep the residual small." All thirty variants are single runs at one seed per configuration with the token budgets stated per table, and there is no variance reporting across seeds anywhere in the document.

## What we take from it
Three things. **A method:** the position-sweep with parameter-matched rivals and then one targeted ablations series per named factor is the template for testing any component our white-box model wants to claim is load-bearing — the non-linearity arm and the sparsity arm here are separated by a parameter-free substitute (RMSNorm) and by a score-range constraint (the non-sparse sigmoid), both cheaper than training a second architecture. **A warning:** a component published as part of a bundle may not be the part doing the work, and this paper's own Appendix A.1 is the demonstration — the routing, which is what the prior work was named for, contributes nothing once the gate is present. Anything we read out as "this head routes" or "this mechanism selects" should be checked by collapsing the selection to one option and re-measuring. **A framing for the sink cluster in this folder:** a gate that never fires on most elements and fires sharply on a few is the architectural inverse of the sink, and this entry supplies the empirical bridge — mean attention share on the first token falls from 46.7% to 4.8% across layers with the gate, and the per-layer picture puts the baseline's sink at 83% on the first token in layer 21 against 4% with the gate. That is the intervention the spectral, neuron-level and gradient-side accounts of the sink in runs 704-708 did not have: an addition to the architecture, trained from scratch, that removes the object without touching the residual prefix.
