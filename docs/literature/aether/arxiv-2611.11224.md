## What they wanted, and why

Explanation methods that assign per-feature scores are widely used but heuristic: nothing formally guarantees the reported features are enough. A stronger standard is a *provably sufficient* explanation — a subset of features such that fixing them preserves the prediction for every admissible completion of the rest — and the strongest version of it is cardinally minimal, the smallest such subset. For general networks this is known to be intractable, but a recent result made it tractable for neural additive models (NAMs), which by construction carry no feature interactions. The authors ask what happens to that tractability once interactions are put back in, with the aim of locating exactly which structural property of a model makes certified explanation search cheap.

## What they studied

Models of unit analysis are **interaction scopes**: an any-order NAM writes the score as a sum of learned components, each depending on a designated subset of input features, with the model's "order" being the largest scope size. Between the extremes of first-order NAMs (singletons only, tractable) and fully general networks (arbitrary interactions, intractable) they study networks built from bounded-order scopes. Empirics use nine public tabular benchmarks (Adult, Bike Sharing, Breast Cancer, California Housing, COMPAS, Diabetes, German Credit, Heart Disease, HELOC; 8 to 30 features, 61 to 9,769 explained test points) with ReLU any-order NAMs, and one data-discovered variant built from interactions detected by a sparse-interaction network.

## How they did it

Step by step: (1) define sufficiency over a local ball around the input, with two minimality notions — cardinal (smallest size) and subset (no proper subset still sufficient); (2) prove the decision problem for k-order any-order NAMs is hard at the second level of the polynomial hierarchy for a fixed constant k, by reduction from the general-network case; (3) since exact minimum search is therefore unavoidable-exponential in the worst case, look for structure that restores linear query counts. Two structures are found. The first strengthens the notion: *strong* sufficiency lets every interaction component pick its own private completion of the unobserved features rather than requiring one globally consistent completion, which makes the total worst-case margin loss decompose as a sum over components. Each component's minimal deviation is then bounded with a neural-network verifier (alpha,beta-CROWN), and an integer program with one binary variable per feature and one per (component, free set) pair searches for the smallest explanation; optimistic and pessimistic versions of the program bracket the answer and a bisection loop narrows any interval wide enough to leave the optimum ambiguous, returning only when the two agree. The second structure is sparsity: overlapping scopes are merged into connected components of a feature-interaction graph, and if the largest merged component is bounded by a constant the merged components are disjoint, so ordinary sufficiency decomposes over them and the same solver pipeline returns genuinely cardinal-minimal explanations for the original model, with union-find preprocessing as the only added cost. Baselines are two verifier-backed greedy remove-and-verify variants (canonical order, sensitivity-guided order), plus, in the appendix, a QuickXplain-style divide-and-conquer method, an exact brute-force search, and a minimum-hitting-set encoding solved as MaxSAT — with the verifier held fixed across all methods so that gains can only come from how structure is exploited.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **certified feature subset** with a formal guarantee: prediction preserved for all admissible completions, plus a minimality proof (cardinal under the strengthened notion, or cardinal for the original model under sparsity). It is not a description, not a score vector, and not a circuit — it is a certificate about the *input* side of the model. Crucially it carries a lower bound as well as the set: when the program converges the authors can state that no smaller sufficient subset exists, which a greedy certificate never states.

## How they judged success

Three measures: mean explanation size (as a percentage of features, with standard deviations and full per-example size distributions rather than a single aggregate), mean wall-clock runtime (with medians where skewed), and the number of instances *solved* inside a per-example budget — the last being the honest counterweight to size, since a method that returns a small set only on easy inputs is not comparable. Accuracy of the underlying models is reported alongside, and one ablation correlates the size gap against greedy with the model's decision margin per example (Pearson, per dataset and pooled). Two appendix controls matter most: a comparison against exact cardinal-minimal baselines where they are affordable, and a data-discovered sparsity check that asks whether small merged components arise when the scopes are *detected* rather than imposed.

## What they found

Interactions destroy the tractability outright: even constant-order scopes restore the hardness of the general case, so no polynomial number of verification queries suffices in the worst case. Under strong sufficiency the pipeline is linear-round and competitive — best mean runtime on most of the nine benchmarks and mean sizes close to greedy, with the largest wins where the greedy search is most order-sensitive (HELOC 38.4% versus 72.0% and 77.5%; Adult 25.7% versus 45.2% and 52.8%) and small losses elsewhere (Breast Cancer 4.0% versus 3.5% and 2.7%; California Housing 19.7% versus 16.7% and 16.0%). Under sparsity it wins on size on all nine, sometimes by more than half (German Credit 31.3% versus 66.3% and 54.8%; COMPAS 19.7% versus 31.5%; HELOC 21.1% versus 55.2% and 62.3%), and the two lowest-dimensional benchmarks show it landing near the true optimum that brute force recovers (California Housing 1.57 versus 1.26; Diabetes 0.82 versus 0.62) while brute force times out entirely at 20 features. Against the divide-and-conquer baseline on Adult the sizes tie (25.7% versus 25.5%) but the median runtime differs by an order of magnitude (18.3s versus 332.1s) and only one method solves every instance. Increasing order raises accuracy monotonically on Bike Sharing (0.9163, 0.9180, 0.9295 at orders 2, 3, 4) while mean certified size grows (2.82, 2.90, 3.37) and mean solve time roughly quadruples per step, until at order 4 only 1,096 of 3,450 examples converge inside an hour. Unrestricted (whole-domain) sufficiency on Heart Disease grows every method's certificate (29.1% to 61.2% for theirs) but preserves the ordering and the solve rate (60/61 versus 51/61 and 13/61). Data-detected scopes do produce the small regime the theory needs: components of size 3-4 out of 8 and 13 features, with every instance converging.

## Limits and open problems they name

The dependence on verification and integer-programming solvers is not an implementation detail — the hardness theorem makes solver calls unavoidable, and current verifiers do not scale to state-of-the-art networks, so the contribution is a wider tractable frontier rather than removal of the bottleneck. Strong sufficiency deliberately optimises a stricter quantity and carries no size guarantee against a plain subset-minimal baseline, which the size table confirms on a minority of datasets. The sparsity condition is imposed by construction in the main experiments; the data-discovered check is run on two benchmarks only. Order 4 is reported as a practical wall, with the order-4 statistics computed on the 32% of examples that converged and flagged as probably biased toward easier inputs. The explanation-size gap over greedy is only weakly explained by the decision margin (pooled correlation 0.158, strongly positive on COMPAS and Heart Disease, flat or negative on three others), and the authors leave the complementary factor — the spread of alternative sufficient subsets under randomised orderings — to future work.

## What we take from it

A **framing and a warning**, plus one reusable measure. The framing: this is the folder's first entry where the *interaction structure itself* is the variable that decides whether a model's internals can be read cheaply — the same reduction, run on a first-order model, costs a linear number of queries, and run on a model with scopes of size two, costs exponentially many. The warning: it is a hardness result about *explanation search*, not about identifiability, and it is read here as one — no statement in it says a decomposition is unique or that the component functions mean anything. The measure worth stealing: **"solved inside budget" reported as a numerator and denominator next to every mean**, because the order-4 row (1,096 of 3,450) and the restricted-domain row (60 of 61 versus 13 of 61) both show that a mean computed only over converged instances is a statement about which instances converged. Second measure: the exact-baseline appendix, which reports where the method lands relative to the true optimum on the only benchmarks where the optimum is computable, and says plainly that greedy baselines stand in elsewhere *because* exact search does not scale.
