# pico-WAM: Evaluating Counterfactual Prediction and Planning Error in a Compact World Action Model

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.01039
- **Authors / organisation:** Keivalya Pandya; Khoury College of Computer Sciences, Northeastern University
- **Date:** 2026-10-31
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.01039
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

When a small action-conditioned world model is put to work as a controller, the failure is a single number — the agent does not move the block — and any number of internal stages could be responsible. The author's question is which stage: the model proposes candidate action sequences, imagines their outcomes through a learned transition model, and ranks them with a cost function, and a bad episode can come from any of the three. The paper's stated motivation is that world-model papers usually report control performance while their models learn only from actions experts took, whereas a planner must price actions nobody took, so training data coverage and search design are entangled in every headline. The aim is a deliberately small, legible model in which each stage can be swapped for an exact simulator component, so the arm that changes control can be identified rather than inferred. It is framed as a diagnostic protocol rather than a claim of architecture superiority.

## What they studied

One model, called pico-WAM, on one manipulation task plus a smaller second task. Inputs are a 64x64 image, a fixed instruction and an eight-dimensional supplied physical state (agent position and velocity, block centre and orientation as a cosine/sine pair). A three-layer CNN with 16 spatial-softmax keypoints, a GRU and a state MLP feed a fusion network producing a 128-dimensional latent; a policy head with three 512-unit layers predicts eight-action chunks, a residual-MLP transition model advances the latent, and a linear readout maps latents to standardised physical state. The optional image decoder is switched off and the instruction is constant, so perception and language generalisation are out of scope by design. The unit of analysis is the **decision step**: propose 256 candidates, imagine each for 12 steps, score with an agent-and-block cost, execute the first action of the winner and repeat. Data are the LeRobot Push-T demonstrations — 206 episodes, 25,444 transitions at 10 Hz, keypoints supplying block pose and controller replay recovering agent velocity — split into 164 training, 21 validation and 21 test demonstrations. The second study replaces the latent model with a plain two-layer 256-unit state-space dynamics model on Meta-World door-open (39-dimensional state, 4-dimensional actions, scripted and noisy-expert episodes).

## How they did it

Training is a matched five-condition comparison. A behaviour-cloning-only baseline learns expert action chunks; a "current" condition adds current-state reconstruction; the full model adds multi-step future state and future-latent losses at horizon 5. Each of the latter two is trained either on demonstration windows alone or on equal mixtures of demonstration windows and counterfactual branches generated in the true simulator. Within a run all conditions share initial parameters, action minibatches, batch size and 30,000 Adam updates, so the only difference is the objective and the added data; 15 training seeds are used. The predeclared primary quantity is an interaction contrast: the difference between restart-from-policy and warm-start-from-previous-plan coverage, computed for branch-trained and demonstration-trained models, and subtracted. Planning is then dissected by a crossed factorial: proposals and scoring are each either learned or exact, giving four arms (learned-proposal/learned-score, learned/exact, exact/learned, exact/exact), evaluated on frozen branch-trained checkpoints. A third family of ablations retrains with one modification each — blocked auxiliary gradients into the encoder, image-only or state-only inputs, no instruction, dropped future-state loss, dropped latent loss — to find which architectural pieces carry the behaviour. Diagnostics are reported separately from control: prediction error is measured under recorded actions and under random actions (the latter against a zero-action reference error so a model can be scored as worse than doing nothing), candidate ranking by rank correlation with true cost, top-32 overlap and selection regret on 1,911 shared states, and plan progress by comparing predicted to realised movement. Imitation baselines (a tuned behaviour-cloning sweep and Diffusion Policy) are compared as a floor that planning must beat.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **component attribution by substitution**, not a representation-level explanation. Nothing is said about what individual latents, keypoints or features encode; the understanding offered is a stage-level causal claim of the form "of the two stages we varied, this one moves the outcome and that one does not", established by replacing one stage with the ground-truth simulator while holding the other fixed. It is explicitly partial: the author writes that the interventions explain behaviour in a controlled setting and do not establish a new architecture or a general superiority of planning. It predicts something narrow and useful — that a diagnostic which changes both stages at once will overstate the benefit of whichever stage is easier to fix.

## How they judged success

Mean coverage (a continuous measure of block-to-target closeness), reported as an average over 15 training runs with per-run spread and paired 95% intervals, and binary success as a secondary measure with unadjusted intervals. Control uses 200 shared final evaluation starts per condition per run, with warm and restart search sharing checkpoints. The primary test is the predeclared data-by-search interaction; four further coverage contrasts and all success contrasts are secondary. Checks against protocol drift are unusually explicit: a five-seed development study triggered a predeclared precision rule and its final bank was never evaluated; all checkpoints were frozen before final evaluation; prediction and ranking diagnostics are computed on state populations disjoint from the control starts; retrospective leave-one-run-out sensitivity and a 50,000-draw paired bootstrap are reported; a supplementary archive regenerates all eight figures and the saved Figure 1 actions in a fresh CPU environment. The author states plainly that containing zero in a secondary interval is not evidence of equivalence.

## What they found

**Counterfactual branches do not change which search initialisation wins; they enlarge an existing advantage, and they matter mostly off the demonstration manifold.** With demonstration-only training, warm-start coverage is 21.3% and restart 43.8%; with branches, warm rises to 51.1% and restart to 78.4%, so the restart advantage goes from 22.5 to 27.3 points and the predeclared interaction is 4.8 points, positive in 12 of 15 runs. Without branch data both planners fall below the same checkpoint's reactive policy, which scores 72.1% coverage and 22.6% success. The prediction diagnostics explain why: under recorded expert actions the two models are indistinguishable (state error 0.011 versus 0.012 on held-out demonstrations, difference interval straddling zero), whereas under random actions the demonstration-only model's block error is 0.025 — worse than the 0.023 zero-action reference — and the branch-trained model's is 0.007, i.e. it is the only arm that improves on predicting nothing, with block error in pixels falling from 14.8 to 7.1. The pattern repeats on 50 fresh noisy-expert episodes (0.041 versus 0.015).

**Ranking improves a great deal and control only moderately, and the gap is the entry's central measurement.** Branch training lifts rank correlation with true cost from 0.49 to 0.80, top-32 overlap from 0.40 to 0.65 and cuts selection regret from 7.56 to 2.77 cost units, improving all 15 runs. Yet chosen plans stay systematically optimistic: a branch-trained restart plan predicts 10.9 px of progress and achieves 1.2 px. The author notes that regret rewards approaching the block as well as moving it, so the aggregate improvement does not separate gaining position from gaining manipulation.

**Swapping the scorer, not the proposer, is what moves control.** Replacing the whole model with the simulator raises restart coverage to 89.9%, but that changes both stages. Exact scoring with learned policy proposals adds 12.2 points with positive gains in every run, whereas exact proposals under learned scoring produce no detectable improvement. The whole learned pipeline therefore sits about 11 points below the best single substitution it could have made. Even with exact dynamics, warm start stalls at 49.4%, so initialisation is not a scoring problem.

**The representation is a convenience, not an advantage — and one ablation is the sharpest number in the paper.** Blocking prediction-loss gradients into the shared encoder leaves reactive behaviour inconclusive (5.1 points, interval spanning zero) but costs 44.3 points of restart coverage, i.e. latents shaped only by imitation cannot support search. Removing instructions or the future-state and latent losses costs a few points at most. Masking the supplied physical state and using images alone costs 31.5 points reactively, so most of the capability here is state information rather than perception. A separately trained state-space dynamics MLP shows no detectable coverage difference from the shared latent model while achieving a **higher** success rate (12.0 points), and the author reads this as the shared design being "a diagnostic design choice rather than a guaranteed performance advantage".

**Better ranking need not beat imitation.** Planning never shows a demonstrable coverage advantage over behaviour cloning (0.1, 0.2, -1.4 and 2.7 points across the matched, horizon-matched, original-environment and tuned-baseline comparisons, all intervals containing zero), though it does beat cloning on binary success by roughly 8 to 10 points. Against Diffusion Policy, planning is at -1.0 coverage and -16.7 success. The door-opening study shows the data-side mechanism transfers: adding noisy rollouts to scripted-expert data lowers selection regret across all ten runs (7.15 to 4.23 in units of 1e-4, paired interval -3.94 to -1.90) and lifts planner success from 78.6% to 88.2% — but both planners trail the reactive cloning policy at 98.6%.

**A dataset caveat that changes how the numbers should be read:** no recorded frame in the LeRobot Push-T demonstrations succeeds, mean maximum coverage is 84.8%, and the human recording termination policy is unknown. The paper therefore studies learning from incomplete demonstrations, and its behaviour-cloning ceiling of 78.3% coverage with 23.3% success is a property of the data as much as of the method.

## Limits and open problems they name

One task for the main study, with a second architecture and task in the smaller door-opening arm, and every generalisation beyond them is disclaimed. The planner uses supplied ground-truth state at inference, so nothing here speaks to perception from images alone. Intervals capture run-to-run variability only, not dataset or task uncertainty; secondary intervals are unadjusted. Test demonstrations had appeared in earlier exploratory work, so fresh control starts and fresh prediction episodes are the cleaner checks. The author lists the roles of planning horizon, cost shaping and search breadth as open, and says identifying which contact or state errors drive each failure remains unresolved. The supplementary reproduction check regenerates figures and a miniature pipeline but does not establish full numerical reproduction of training. Compute is reported as implementation-specific measurement, not an efficiency claim.

## What we take from it

Four things, in descending order of how directly they apply to a predictive white-box model. **A construction:** hold a stage fixed and swap one other stage for the ground-truth system. The crossed factorial is the cleanest version of this in the folder, and its result is a warning — the naive swap of the whole model looks like an 11-point capability, while the single substitution that carries it accounts for 12.2 of those points and the other substitution accounts for none. **A diagnostic rule:** a predictor's error under the distribution it was trained on says nothing about its error under the distribution a planner will query it with. This paper's scored model is indistinguishable from its control under recorded actions and strictly worse than a constant under random ones, so every prediction our model publishes about behaviour under intervention carries an off-support arm with a do-nothing reference error printed beside it. **A measurement discipline:** the *interaction* contrast, predeclared, with a leave-one-run-out sensitivity and a frozen evaluation bank, is the cheapest way to attribute a gain to the interaction of two factors rather than to either. **A ceiling to name:** the datum ceiling is part of the result, not a footnote — a cloning policy that tops out at 84.8% coverage makes every downstream comparison conditional, and a report that printed planner coverage without that number would invite the wrong conclusion.
