## What they wanted, and why
Gradient-based circuit discovery is the only route to edge-level attribution that scales past tiny graphs: two forward passes and one backward pass replace one forward pass per edge. The authors' complaint is that the two established versions of that route are broken in two different ways in the same place — at the *path* along which gradients are averaged. The original edge-attribution patching evaluates the derivative at a single point, which can be exactly zero at the clean activation and therefore silently marks a load-bearing edge as unimportant. Integrated gradients fixes the single point by averaging along a straight line, but the straight line is chosen without reference to the model, so it spends most of its samples in flat regions where the loss barely moves and the gradient is again near zero. Their framing is that the averaging procedure must be *model-dependent*: where the path goes is a design choice, and a badly chosen path is why attribution scores are noisy. The larger goal is the standard one for this literature — smaller, more faithful circuits found faster — but the specific contribution is a statement about attribution estimators rather than about transformers.

## What they studied
GPT-2 Small (117M parameters, 32,491 edges in the computational graph), GPT-2 Medium (345M, 231,877 edges) and GPT-2 XL (1.5B, 2,235,025 edges). Unit of analysis: edges between attention heads and MLPs in the residual graph, with nodes pruned afterwards. Six tasks from the EAP-IG suite — IOI, subject-verb agreement, gender bias, capital-to-country, hypernymy, and greater-than — each with a clean/corrupted prompt pair and a task-specific scalar: logit difference for the first three, probability difference for the rest. Positive and negative example counts match the earlier EAP-IG preprocessing, and three of the six tasks also have hand-annotated reference circuits to compare against.

## How they did it
Every method in the family scores an edge by an estimate of the loss change when that edge's activation is swapped from clean to corrupted, built out of the activation difference multiplied by a gradient. Plain EAP uses the gradient at the clean activation; EAP-IG averages the gradient at `m` evenly spaced points on the straight line between the clean and corrupted activations. The authors first diagnose this by tracing the loss and its gradient along that straight line for a single IOI edge and showing the gradient sits near zero over two intervals of the interpolation parameter while the score only moves in a narrow middle band. They then define a saturation region as a segment of the integration path where the loss gradient is near zero and a saturation effect as the accumulation of score there. Their replacement, GradPath, builds the path step by step: at each of `T` steps it takes one gradient-descent step on the *loss difference to the corrupted activation*, normalises the step, and moves the interpolation point that way, so the path bends to follow where the prediction is actually changing instead of travelling in a straight line. The edge score is then the same finite-sum accumulation as EAP-IG but along this adaptive path. Circuits are formed by ranking edges and taking the top fraction at a fixed sparsity, pruning isolated nodes, then evaluating by corrupting every edge outside the circuit. The only hyperparameter is `T`, swept from 3 to 20 on two tasks; `T = 1` reduces to EAP and `T = 2` to averaging the clean and corrupted gradients, which is the useful property that makes the sweep a continuum from the old method to the new one.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not an explanation of the model's computation — the paper produces an edge ranking and a subgraph, with no functional interpretation of the components, no feature descriptions and no pseudocode. The object of study is the *estimator*: the claim is that EAP-IG's scores are a biased estimator of the loss change because the averaging path is chosen independently of the model, and that a path chosen to avoid zero-gradient regions is a better estimator of the same target quantity. It is partial by construction: the circuit is a top-k subgraph at a chosen sparsity, and the paper's own account of failure is that the absolute scores can rank noise and adversarial-pattern edges above useful ones.

## How they judged success
The headline metric is the Normalised Faithfulness Score (NFS): the circuit's task metric with all non-circuit edges corrupted, minus the fully corrupted baseline, divided by the gap between the clean and corrupted baselines, so 100% means the circuit reproduces the full model's behaviour and 0% means it reproduces nothing. Faithfulness is measured across sparsity levels from 96.9% to 99.9% of edges removed. Secondary: precision and recall of the recovered nodes and edges against the hand-annotated IOI and greater-than circuits, plotted as PR curves; wall-clock time at 97.5% sparsity; and a step-count ablation on two tasks. Baselines are EAP and EAP-IG with the same ranking, selection and evaluation code, which is the right control — the estimator is the only thing that changes.

## What they found
At 97.5% edge sparsity on GPT-2 Small, IOI goes from 56.9% (EAP) and 62.4% (EAP-IG) to 80.1% (EAP-GP), and the greater-than task to 99.8% from 96.3% and 97.6%. SVA shows the widest spread of the six — 96.14% against 84.57% — while gender-bias, capital-country and greater-than stay within a couple of points of EAP-IG and sometimes below it. The paper's own explanation of that split is the most informative result in it: tasks that need a direct local association between two positions (a profession and a pronoun, a capital and its country) are nearly solved by every estimator, whereas the tasks that need multi-step composition (IOI, hypernymy, SVA) are where the path matters. Plain EAP collapses to an unfaithful, usually empty, circuit above 99% sparsity because it emits parentless heads that node pruning then deletes. Step count matters only briefly: three to five steps already reach near-final faithfulness and more steps can *hurt*, which the authors attribute to the normalised step size growing as the gradient norm shrinks, so the path starts oscillating in a region where directions are unstable. The cost is about five times EAP-IG's runtime at equal sparsity, because each step needs its own forward and backward pass; that is stated plainly rather than hidden. The PR comparison is mixed and honestly reported: on IOI the three methods track each other and EAP-GP starts *worse* before crossing over at high recall, which they note may be an artefact of the contested status of MLPs in the manual IOI circuit; on greater-than, where the reference circuit includes MLPs, EAP-GP dominates on both node and edge retrieval.

## Limits and open problems they name
Only GPT-2 checkpoints, all under 2B parameters and all of one architecture family; the larger-model section plots faithfulness but the tables that would let a reader recompute it are only partly present in the HTML. The tasks are templated, single-token, contrastive pairs with a hand-built corruption, which is exactly the setting where a logit difference is a clean objective and a gradient along a path is well-behaved. No uncertainty is reported anywhere: no seeds, no confidence bands, no variance across prompt samples, so the 17.7% headline is a point estimate on one draw of each dataset. The claim that saturation causes the failures is argued mechanistically and illustrated on a single inspected edge rather than shown to be the dominant error source across edges. The paper also notes that reducing sparsity or raising the step count can *lower* the metric, which it explains as greedy top-k selection admitting noisy edges — an admission that the selection rule, not the score, is a second uncontrolled knob. The fivefold slowdown is not addressed.

## What we take from it
A method, a measure, and a warning. Method: an adaptive integration path is a cheap upgrade to any attribution patching pipeline we run, and it is worth having because it makes the estimator's dependence on the baseline explicit — where you send the interpolation is part of the estimate. Measure: NFS-at-fixed-sparsity is the right way to compare two attribution estimators, because it holds the *selection* rule constant and varies only the *score*; any comparison of our probe rankings to a baseline should keep that separation. Warning, and the sharpest thing here: the size of the whole effect tracks how much composition a task needs. On the two local-association tasks in the suite every estimator is within a few points of every other, so a benchmark run on easy tasks would have shown the new method to be redundant — and this folder's entries routinely report aggregate wins over a six-task suite that contains exactly this mix. Mechanically: **when we report that a new attribution score beats an old one, we report the per-task spread and the spread within the easy tasks, not just the best cell or the mean — because the improvement lives entirely in the compositional tasks, and the local tasks are a floor both methods already clear.** Cross-references: run 760 (a record whose added fields degraded retrieval, so more information is not monotonically better) and the folder's repeated finding that a ranking is only interpretable alongside the selection rule that turns it into a set.
