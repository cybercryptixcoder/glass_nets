## What they wanted, and why

The authors set out to give a mechanism for a fact the field already relies on without explaining: trained ReLU language models are enormously sparse at their MLP activations, and the sparsity is not something anyone asked for. They want to know where it comes from, whether it should be treated as damage or as a free lunch, and whether it can be steered. The motivation is partly practical - the sparsity is already being exploited by fast sparse-times-dense kernels and by prefetching schemes - and partly architectural: if the effect is a side effect of the loss acting on a positively one-sided nonlinearity, then it is a property of the training objective rather than of the data, and it should be visible in any model that lacks mean-centring normalisation, which includes almost every open-weight large language model released after 2023. The larger goal is a story in which weights, activation function, normalisation layer and loss are considered as one coupled system, because the authors argue none of the four is interpretable alone.

## What they studied

Four architectures: a five-layer MLP on CIFAR-10, ResNet-18, MaxViT-Tiny, and GPT-nano (Karpathy's 124M-parameter model) trained on FineWeb. Six activation functions (ReLU, GELU, SiLU, NoisyReLU, SUGARBSiLU, ReLU-squared, with squared GELU variants appearing in the spike table), two sparsification mechanisms, and normalisation variants (none, RMSNorm, LayerNorm, BatchNorm, percentile-based variants). The unit of analysis is the **weight matrix and the per-layer activation distribution** - the mean weight per layer, the mean pre-activation, the fraction of negative pre-activations, the post-activation sparsity, and the maximum activation entering a down-projection - rather than individual neurons or features. For the sparsity-versus-accuracy part the unit is a (model, sparsity) pair, of which they collect 79.

## How they did it

They first prove a claim about the gradient, then measure the consequence. Take any intermediate layer of a ReLU network at initialisation, fold every layer above it into a single effective linear map, and show that the rows of that map are zero-mean in expectation over random initialisation while the expected inner product of any two rows is **non-negative** - the argument is that conditioning two random vectors on both surviving a ReLU gate forces them to share a positive component along the input direction, so they become positively correlated. With that in hand, the expected derivative of MSE and of softmax cross-entropy with respect to a pre-activation is non-negative (strictly positive when the pre-activation is positive), so gradient descent, which steps against the gradient, pushes downstream weights negative. Because the shift is largest early, when the loss is largest, and then decays, the drift is predicted to happen in the first iterations and persist. They then measure drift in four ways: the per-layer mean weight over training steps; a normalised drift score (mean absolute change from initial values divided by the initial standard deviation of the layer's weights); the same trajectory under SGD versus momentum and under several learning rates; and the covariance between the per-neuron gradient and the input, which the proof assumes to be negligible and which they check is orders of magnitude smaller than the weight mean. The drift prediction is tested on random Gaussian inputs and labels as well as real data, to separate optimisation from data, and it is checked on MLP, ResNet-18, MP-SENet and MaxViT, and across ReLU, GELU and SiLU. The sparsity question is then made causal by adding two controls that set the sparsity level directly: **Top-K** (keep the largest fraction, hard-zero the rest, paired with GELU because ReLU cannot be pushed *below* its natural sparsity floor) and **Percentile Centering**, their own layer-norm variant that subtracts a target quantile of the pre-activation instead of the mean, so that a chosen fraction falls below zero before the ReLU. Sweeping the target level gives performance as a function of sparsity, and they fit a three-parameter power law to the pooled normalised points. Finally they run the reverse move - train with GELU, then fine-tune to ReLU for one epoch ("ReLUfication") - and examine activation spikes layer by layer, adding clipping and a frozen-statistics scheme for throughput.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **mechanism with a proof and a measured trajectory**: a signed, self-reinforcing feedback loop in which a one-sided nonlinearity biases the expected gradient, gradient descent shifts the weights negative, the negative weights push pre-activations negative, and the nonlinearity then maps those pre-activations to zero (ReLU) or dampens them (GELU, SiLU) - which in turn reinforces the gradient bias. It is explicitly a partial explanation: it explains *where the sparsity comes from* and *how much is harmless*, and it does not pretend to explain why transformers tolerate far more of it than an MLP, nor why one normalisation variant improves image quality. The power law fitted to the sparsity sweep is a different kind of object - a descriptive curve with three free parameters, not a mechanism - and the paper treats it that way.

## How they judged success

Consistency between the analytic predictions and four independent measurements per claim: sign of the expected gradient (theory), mean weight per layer over training steps (trajectory), fraction of negative pre-activations (a consequence, measured across the full architecture-by-activation grid), and post-activation sparsity (the terminal quantity). Controls that matter for us: training on fully random inputs to rule out data structure; comparison against mean-centring normalisation, which should and does break the drift; Top-K on GELU to separate the sparsity *level* from the sparsity *mechanism*; ordinary versus structured (per-channel) sparsity to test whether the shape of the zeroing matters; a three-seed check on the spike and squared-activation claims; and a regression that reports how much of the variance in normalised performance is explained by sparsity alone (R² 0.565) versus by adding the mechanism label (0.618). Throughput claims are normalised against optimised PyTorch kernels and explicitly described as a lower bound because the implementation is not custom-kernelised.

## What they found

The drift is real and early: on random Gaussian inputs, with no data structure to exploit, the per-layer weight means go negative for all three activations, most strongly for ReLU and in a depth ordering where earlier layers drift further (the very first layer does not drift, because its inputs have not yet passed through an activation). Momentum accelerates drift and drives it to its asymptote fast while SGD moves slowly and near-linearly; within an optimiser, larger learning rates drift further. The consequence shows up as a large fraction of negative pre-activations - between roughly 60% and 80% in nearly every architecture-and-activation configuration in Table 1 - and as post-activation sparsity of about 89% in the ReLU language model. The one configuration where the drift disappears is ResNet-18 **with** batch normalisation (43.6% negative pre-activations), which is the paper's cleanest evidence that mean-centring is the thing that suppresses it. The sparsity sweep gives a plateau and then a cliff: performance is essentially flat below about 70% sparsity and collapses past it, the fitted curve has A ≈ 0.978 (near-complete retention at zero sparsity), B ≈ 0.635 (maximum possible drop) and an exponent of 16.7 that makes the transition sharp; the cliff position is architecture-dependent, with skip connections helping - a plain MLP is at chance at 95% sparsity while the same MLP with a skip connection still scores 27.1%, and GPT-nano's loss stays nearly flat out to about 91%. Unstructured sparsity hurts less than per-channel sparsity. Among activations, GELU is the best general baseline but produces no sparsity, ReLU-squared wins on the language model but collapses to chance on an unnormalised MLP and on ResNet with batch norm, and ReLUfication (one epoch of GELU-to-ReLU fine-tuning) reaches 55-74% post-activation sparsity for under two accuracy points - the authors' practical recommendation, justified by the observation that the negative pre-activation fraction barely changes after conversion, i.e. the drift had already shoved the weights and ReLU is only truncating values that GELU already kept near zero. On spikes: large positive activations appear in early layers of the language model regardless of activation, they originate in the up-projection, ReLU-squared amplifies them by a factor of roughly 4 to 25 with one run exceeding 1000, clipping at 50 removes them without hurting loss while clipping at 15 costs performance, and of six pretrained models only GPT-2 and BERT-base show layer maxima 9-12 times the median while vision models stay under 4 times - so the spike is a language-model property, not a transformer-MLP property. Freezing the normalisation statistics after a 50,000-step warm-up recovers full throughput (1.00-1.03x on a diffusion transformer, 1.09-1.34x on MaxViT) with quality within 2% relative, and percentile centring with GELU improved FID from 49.40 to 48.21 on the diffusion model, a result the authors say they cannot explain.

## Limits and open problems they name

The proofs hold strictly at initialisation only; the extension beyond the first steps is argued informally. The formal results cover ReLU and the smooth activations are supported empirically alone. Language modelling is tested with a single 124M-parameter model on one corpus, and the clipping thresholds are chosen empirically and look architecture-dependent. The frozen-statistics scheme is only tested on fixed-size inputs and measured with a naive implementation. Two findings are left unexplained outright: why transformers are so much more robust to aggressive sparsification than an MLP, and why percentile normalisation with GELU improves image quality. The authors also flag that sparsity is measured at activation outputs only, so attention layers are excluded from the sparsity accounting.

## What we take from it

Four things, and the first is the reason this entry is rated central.

**A warning that our own unit statistics may be reading the optimiser, not the concept.** The sparsity and the sign of the weight distribution in an MLP block are set by a gradient bias that exists before any data-specific learning has occurred, and it is present on random inputs. Any interpretability reading of a ReLU network that treats "this neuron is silent most of the time" or "activations sit near zero" as evidence about a *feature* or about *task structure* is, in the regime this paper describes, reporting the consequence of a loss-times-nonlinearity interaction. Mechanical rule for us: **before attributing a low firing rate, a near-zero activation mean or a high activation sparsity to a semantic cause, check the mean of the downstream weight matrix and the fraction of negative pre-activations for that layer, and check whether the same pattern survives a mean-centring interlayer on frozen activations - because a statistic that moves when we subtract a constant is a statement about input geometry and optimisation, not about what the model learned.** Cross-reference run 701 and run 702 (the offset / mean-over-spread ratio as the same quantity one level upstream) and run 699 (latents positioned by chat-format boilerplate rather than meaning): four consecutive entries in this folder now say the same thing at four different levels - a unit's reading is co-determined by things that are not the unit.

**A causal control for "how much of this is harmless", which is the cheapest version of the intervention we keep asking for.** Instead of arguing about whether the emergent sparsity is a bug, the authors *set it* - by percentile centring for ReLU and by Top-K for GELU - and read performance off the resulting curve, then check that the mechanism of setting it barely matters once the level is fixed (ΔR² of only 0.053). Mechanical rule for us: **when we report that some structural property of a model (sparsity, rank, a dictionary's utilisation) correlates with or predicts capability, we also report the curve obtained by forcing that property to a range of values with at least one mechanism other than the model's own, because a correlation between an emergent property and quality is not evidence that the property is doing useful work - and a plateau followed by a cliff means our correlation is being read off the plateau where it carries almost no information.** Cross-reference run 695 (the construction baseline that made a score interpretable) and run 700 (a gain needs a null that spends the same budget).

**A diagnostic that costs one epoch and reverses an architecture choice - the analogue of "ReLUfication" for our read-outs.** A model trained with a smooth activation can be converted to a sparse one by brief fine-tuning with almost no loss, *because the weights had already drifted*; conversion did not create the near-zero region, it disclosed it. The transferable move is that a post-hoc swap of a read-out (or of a decoder's nonlinearity) at fixed weights can reveal how much of an effect was latent in the weights versus created by the training of the read-out itself. Mechanical rule for us: **where we can swap a component of a fitted read-out post hoc while holding everything upstream fixed, we do, and we report the pre-swap value beside the post-swap one - because a component that "improves" a read-out may only be disclosing a structure that was already present, and a component that changes nothing was never doing the work we attributed to it.**

**A rule about where an outlier lives, which we need before we trust any top-activating-context argument.** The spikes appear in the up-projection and are amplified by squaring, the early layers are where they concentrate, they survive normalisation in reduced form, and they are absent in vision models with the same block structure - which the authors read, following the first-token-attention work, as evidence that the spikes attach to tokens with a functional role. That is a candidate confound for every "this latent fires on the first token" observation in this folder. Mechanical rule for us: **any argument built on a latent's or neuron's largest activations is rerun with those positions clipped or excluded and with the per-layer input range reported, because the biggest activations in a language model's early MLP blocks can be a layer-level population effect concentrated on a few function-bearing tokens rather than a property of the concept under study.** Cross-reference run 694 (read versus write direction specificity) and run 696 (an activation-only ranking beaten by a shuffled null): the same warning that the top of a ranking may be structure, not semantics.
