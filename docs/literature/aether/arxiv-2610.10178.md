# Do Vision-Language-Action Models Understand Instructions? A Mechanistic Interpretability Study on Language Grounding

- **Key:** arxiv-2610.10178
- **Authors / organisation:** Theodor Wulff, Angelo Cangelosi (Department of Computer Science, The University of Manchester)
- **Date:** 2026-10-07
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10178
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (arXiv HTML v1, 59,417 clean chars / 1,147 lines; abstract, sections 1-9, Appendix A implementation notes, Appendix B random-object list, Appendix C synonym/hypernym table; figures 1-5 read via captions only, no plot inspected as an image)
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Robot policies built as vision-language-action models are marketed as generalising across task phrasings, but nothing guarantees the action decoder is actually using the words. A VLA has no grounding module: the only language it ever sees reaches the action expert as conditioning embeddings from a vision-language backbone, so whatever language understanding exists is inherited, and any weakness in it is a hidden bottleneck on behaviour in the physical world. The authors want to answer a narrower and more falsifiable question than "does the model follow instructions": does altering the language change the internal computation and its causal footprint on the predicted action, and at which layers. They deliberately avoid testing whether the model interprets an instruction *correctly*, evaluating every perturbed run against the original demonstration instead. The larger motivation is deployment robustness - a robot that ignores its instructions is dangerous in a way a benchmark score will not show - and the paper's contribution is a measurement pipeline for causal-and-representational sensitivity in flow-matching action experts.

## What they studied
Two open-weight state-of-the-art VLAs with publicly released LIBERO-10 fine-tuned checkpoints. π0.5 pairs a SigLIP (400M) vision encoder and a Gemma (2.6B) language model with a 300M-parameter action expert that shares the transformer stack and interacts with the backbone through block-wise causally masked joint self-attention. GR00T N1.7 uses a Cosmos-Reason2-2B backbone and injects visuolinguistic embeddings into a 32-layer alternating cross-/self-attention diffusion transformer through dedicated cross-attention, and its cross-attention layers are masked so that only every fourth layer (default n = 4) ingests fresh linguistic information. Data: 10,000 steps sampled from LIBERO-10 offline demonstrations (378 episodes, roughly 101,000 steps, ten compositional long-horizon tasks), each step carrying two camera views (wrist, table), a proprioceptive vector and the instruction. The unit of analysis is the residual stream of each action-expert layer, patched immediately after the self-attention output projection, at every token position; the scalar the effect is measured on is the conditional flow-matching L2 error between target and predicted velocity.

## How they did it
Each clean instruction is corrupted in five nested ways, ordered by assumed severity: a synonym swap; a hypernym (semantic scaling, "apple" to "fruit"); an inversion of standalone directional modifiers ("left" to "right", excluding phrasal-verb particles); substitution of a noun with one of fifty objects that appear in no LIBERO scene, rendering the task unsolvable by design; and an empty instruction. Synonym and hypernym candidates were LLM-generated and then checked against ConceptNet and WordNet, and samples where a perturbation does not apply (a directionless instruction under directional corruption) are dropped from that experiment rather than patched in some way. For each pair they run a clean pass and a corrupted pass to cache activations, then attribution patching: a first-order Taylor estimate of the loss change under substituting the corrupted activation with the clean one, from two forward passes and one backward pass. Because that estimate is only a gradient approximation, the layers it nominates are re-measured under exact activation patching, one extra forward pass per layer, and the two are compared directly. Representational change is tracked separately with centred kernel alignment (CKA) between clean and perturbed residual streams at each layer, which asks whether the pairwise similarity structure of the activations moved, not whether the output did. Everything runs on a single A100 with nnsight batching both passes in one tracing context, a model wrapper hiding architecture-specific layer paths, and seed 42 for all randomisers including the 10,000-step subsample.

## What counts as an explanation here
A per-layer, per-token causal heatmap of language sensitivity, checked against exact patching, read alongside a per-layer representational-similarity curve. The explanation is a localisation: which layers of the action expert route the linguistic difference into the action prediction, and how strongly. It is explicitly partial - it localises sensitivity to a *type* of perturbation, not to a unit, feature or circuit, and it does not say what the model does with the words, only that changing them changes the computation. Its predictive content is weakest-but-real: the paper offers one mechanistic hypothesis it can test cheaply (periodic spikes at cross-attention placement), and it predicts, correctly in their data, that the representational curve and the causal curve need not agree.

## How they judged success
The main instruments are (a) the magnitude and layer profile of attribution-patching effects per perturbation type, aggregated over the sampled population; (b) Pearson and Spearman correlations between attribution patching and activation patching effects, per model, reported with p-values, plus a variant that drops directional perturbations; (c) per-layer CKA between clean and perturbed runs; and (d) a qualitative check that the expected architectural signature appears (periodicity at the cross-attention cadence for GR00T). There is no downstream task-success arm, no held-out split of the instruction set, no intervention with a behavioural outcome, and the author's proposed severity ordering of the five perturbations functions as a prediction the two curves are then compared against rather than as a benchmark.

## What they found
Sensitivity is model-specific in both magnitude and locus. Directional inversions produce the largest attribution effects in the early layers of both models (layer 1 for π0.5; layers 3, 5 and 7 for GR00T), while replacing a noun with an object absent from the scene has an effect close to that of a synonym or hypernym swap - whether the named object exists barely matters. Dropping the instruction entirely (empty string) produces the strongest reaction in the late layers of GR00T. For π0.5, attribution concentrates near the earliest layers and again past layer 12, and verb-level perturbations count slightly more than noun-level; GR00T is the reverse, more reactive to noun phrases, and its cross-attention signature shows up as an alternating elevation across the token sequence after each cross-attention layer plus a decaying early-layer profile that the authors credit to the every-fourth-layer masking. Some late layers in both models carry *negative* attribution to language corruption, which the authors read as possible self-repair in the sense of a downstream substitution effect compensating upstream.

The central result is a dissociation. In GR00T, directional perturbations drive some of the largest causal effects of any type while CKA shows the residual-stream geometry barely moved; the authors' hypothesis is that directional modifiers sit in a tight, similar-context region of embedding space, so a small representational displacement can sit on a steep part of the loss. Elsewhere causal and representational effects track one another and follow the assumed severity ordering, with π0.5's geometry overall more stable than GR00T's and only the empty-string and directional conditions moving it visibly.

The methodological finding is that attribution patching's reliability is model-dependent. For GR00T it tracks activation patching closely (Pearson r = 0.946, p = 8.26 × 10⁻⁴⁹; Spearman ρ = 0.972, p = 2.48 × 10⁻⁶²). For π0.5 there is no significant relationship (Pearson r = 0.089, p = 4.90 × 10⁻¹; Spearman ρ = 0.097, p = 4.48 × 10⁻¹), and the failure is worst for directional corruption: in layers 2, 3, 5 and 14 the approximation returns a near-zero to slightly positive effect (0.0002 to 0.0013) where exact patching finds a strongly negative one (an interval the strip prints as -0.05 to 0.35). Dropping directional perturbations lifts π0.5's Spearman to 0.251 (p = 6.73 × 10⁻²) — roughly two and a half times higher — while Pearson stays at 0.097, so the rank ordering improves and the magnitude estimate does not. The authors' reading is that linearisation fails further from the output and that directional shifts trigger a threshold or saturation effect that a first-order estimate cannot see.

## Limits and open problems they name
Synthetic perturbations only: no real-world phrasing variance, ambiguity or compositionality, and the LIBERO-10 instructions are short, templated and object-centric in scenes constrained enough that visual context can paper over linguistic ambiguity, which likely understates language sensitivity. Perturbed predictions are always scored against the original demonstration, so results show that language influences the action, not that the model uses it appropriately; closed-loop rollouts or counterfactual targets for meaning-changing edits are named as the missing test. The authors also concede that the link between layer profile and conditioning architecture is a hypothesis, since the two models differ in more than their conditioning mechanism, and single-word effect magnitudes are read off heatmap colour rather than tabulated.

## What we take from it
Two things, both about instrument choice. (1) **The proxy-versus-causal split has to be re-established per architecture rather than per paper, and the same first-order estimator can be near-exact in one model and uninformative in another** - r = 0.946 against 0.089 on the same task, the same data and the same patching point, differing only in how the model is conditioned. Nothing in the method or the metric predicted which model it would fail on; only running the exact arm did. Mechanically for us: **any gradient-based attribution our white-box model publishes carries an exact-intervention verification arm run on the same components, on every architecture it is applied to, and the correlation between approximation and ground truth is printed with the model name beside it; an approximation validated on one architecture is re-validated, never inherited.** Corollary: a proxy can keep the ranking and lose the magnitude, so the two correlations are likely to be strong and weak respectively - Spearman 0.251 against Pearson 0.097 here. (2) **Causal effect and representational change are two measurements with no obligation to agree, and the paper's dissociation gives the rule for reading them.** A large causal effect with a near-floor CKA shift means the representation barely moved but the model is standing on a steep part of its loss in that direction - which is a statement about the *output's* sensitivity, not about the representation's content. A large CKA shift with a small causal effect means the representation moved where nothing downstream reads it. Mechanically for us: **every representation-distance statistic our model reports for a perturbation is paired with the causal effect of the same perturbation at the same layer, reported as a two-dimensional pair (distance, effect) rather than merged into a "sensitivity" score, because the two failure modes - insensitive representation and unmatched causal routing - look identical under any single number.** The paper's own framing of its relation to a null-prompt study is the version worth keeping in our writing: a model can be internally sensitive to an input while still recovering behaviour without it, so an internal-sensitivity claim and a behavioural-necessity claim are separate rows in any table we publish.

A third, cheaper item: the five-way perturbation ladder (synonym, hypernym, direction inversion, out-of-scene object, empty) is a reusable probe battery for our own models, ordered by how much semantics the edit removes rather than by how much the string changes, and the out-of-scene-object condition is a neat control - it makes the task impossible without changing the semantic category the model must process.
