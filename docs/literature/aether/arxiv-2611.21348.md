# Representational Change Under Self-Consuming Training: A Sparse Autoencoder Analysis

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.21348
- **Authors / organisation:** Suchit Gupte, Zhongteng Cai, Xueru Zhang, Mohammad Mahdi Khalili — The Ohio State University (Department of Computer Science)
- **Date:** 2026-11-16
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21348
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (HTML v1, §§1–8, appendices A–B read in part; figures via captions)
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Models are increasingly trained on text produced by earlier models, and the accumulation of that recursion is usually studied from the outside — output diversity, likelihood, downstream scores. The authors ask the complementary inside question: when a model changes this way, do the *internals* move, and do the interpretability tools trained on the old internals keep working? They use sparse autoencoders as the instrument, since an SAE is a fixed read-out of a model's activations and its fate under retraining is a clean test of whether a feature dictionary is a stable object or a snapshot. The larger goal is a discipline for interpretability on models that keep moving: the paper's closing position is that a dictionary's fit and a dictionary's usefulness are separate questions and must be checked separately, which is the same split our own white-box model has to keep straight when a model is fine-tuned under it.

## What they studied
Pythia-160M (layer 8 of 12, the SAEBench convention) and Gemma-2-2B (layers 5, 12 and 19, to get depth variation), each run through five generations of self-consuming training. Unit of analysis: the SAE latent dictionary, evaluated at the level of the whole dictionary (reconstruction, sparsity, explained variance), the level of individual latents (sparse probing with a few selected latents versus all of them), and the level of causal feature use (two ablation benchmarks). SAE families swept on Pythia: BatchTopK at several widths, and BatchTopK / JumpReLU / Matryoshka-BatchTopK at fixed width, so the effect is not specific to one design; Gemma uses width-matched JumpReLU SAEs.

## How they did it
Each model starts from one pretrained checkpoint and two lineages run in parallel for five generations with identical token budget, step count and optimiser: a **synthetic** lineage where generation *n* is trained one epoch on text sampled from generation *n−1* itself (greedy-free sampling, top-p only, no real data mixed in), and a **matched real** control trained on fresh, non-overlapping slices of a pile-derived corpus, with a fixed held-out slice kept out of both. Every reported quantity is a **damage** score — synthetic result minus the generation-matched control, sign-corrected so that positive always means the synthetic lineage is worse — so continued training itself is subtracted out by construction and damage is zero at generation 0. The SAE from generation 0 is then evaluated two ways: **zero-shot**, held frozen and applied to every later generation, and **fine-tuned**, re-adapted from the generation-0 initialisation (not retrained from scratch) on each generation's activations, over three fine-tuning seeds. Metrics come from SAEBench: sparsity (active latents per token), reconstruction MSE, explained variance; sparse probing over 35 one-versus-all classification tasks across five datasets with probes fitted on the top few latents **and** on the full latent vector, plus a residual-stream probe of matched dimension as the baseline; and two causal ablations (spurious-correlation removal and targeted probe perturbation) with selected latents zero-ablated and the original reconstruction error added back so only the targeted latents change. Alongside the experiments the paper gives a linear-autoencoder analysis: an optimal rank-r linear autoencoder recovers the top-r principal subspace of the activation covariance, so a frozen dictionary is a fixed subspace, its reconstruction cost as the representation drifts is bounded by the principal angles between the old and current subspaces via the eigengap, and reconstruction constrains only the subspace and not the basis inside it.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not a mechanism of any behaviour — a **measurement discipline**, plus one analytic model. The analytic piece is a *staleness account*: the degradation of a frozen dictionary is decomposed into a term for how far the reconstruction-relevant subspace has rotated and a term for how hard the shifted representation is to reconstruct at all, and the matched control turns the difference between the two lineages into an exact additive decomposition of the synthetic–real gap. The empirical piece is a claim about the form of drift: it is a property of the dictionary (mismatch that adaptation repairs) rather than of the representation (representational damage that adaptation cannot repair), with a separate, weaker claim about how task-relevant information is *arranged* inside the latents. It is explicitly partial and explicitly one-directional: the authors present the rotation argument as showing why reconstruction recovery cannot certify feature recovery, not as a prediction that feature degradation will occur.

## How they judged success
Damage relative to the generation-matched real-data control at each generation, across core SAEBench metrics (sparsity, reconstruction MSE, explained variance) for every width, architecture and layer; sparse-probing accuracy on the top-k latents versus the full SAE representation versus a residual-stream baseline of the same dimension; spurious-correlation-removal and targeted-probe-perturbation scores over a sweep of ablation budgets; fine-tuning run with three seeds and reported as mean plus spread; and a behavioural sanity check that the recursion actually bites — held-out perplexity for both lineages, which rises along the synthetic lineage while the control plateaus (Pythia) or improves (Gemma).

## What they found
The recursion degrades behaviour as expected: by generation 5 the Pythia synthetic lineage is about 1.6× the control's perplexity, Gemma about 1.4×. Zero-shot dictionaries go stale progressively — damage in sparsity, explained variance and MSE grows every generation for every Pythia width and architecture, with the widest dictionary worst. Fine-tuning removes most of the reconstruction and explained-variance gap, leaving only a small residual sparsity gap (most visible for JumpReLU), which is what the staleness decomposition predicts: the shifted activations are still fine for sparse reconstruction once the dictionary is allowed to move. On Pythia the feature level then separates from the reconstruction level: top-k sparse-probing damage grows with generation and **survives fine-tuning**, most strongly for the smallest probe sizes, while probing the full latent vector shows little or no damage — the information is still in the representation but no longer concentrated in a few latents. On Gemma the drift is strongly depth-dependent — the early layer barely moves, the middle layer develops a moderate gap, the late layer the largest and fastest-growing damage — and fine-tuning repairs reconstruction at all three layers, but the feature-level metrics do not agree with each other: sparse-probing damage is clearest at the middle layer and largely repaired, spurious-correlation removal shows residual damage that adaptation does not fully remove (larger deeper), and targeted probe perturbation shows no consistent direction at all, changing sign across generations and layers.

## Limits and open problems they name
Two model families, five generations, one real-data lineage and one synthetic lineage each, one training seed per lineage — so the error bars measure SAE fine-tuning variation and not variation across model-training runs, which is the sharpest limitation for reading generations as a trend. Pure synthetic training with no real/synthetic mixture, chosen to isolate the effect but less realistic than what actually happens. One Pythia layer and three Gemma layers rather than a full sweep. The SAE is adapted rather than retrained, so the results say what happens to an existing tool and not what a fresh dictionary would find. The metrics are standardised SAEBench families and do not cover every aspect of semantic interpretability. Cross-model comparison of effect sizes is cautioned: Gemma trains entirely in bfloat16, so its effective step size may be smaller than nominal. The depth dependence is established but not explained — the authors point at the observation that early features track token-level structure while deep features are more conceptual as a plausible reason, and say they did not identify the mechanism. The rotation argument is stated as a possibility, not a necessity.

## What we take from it
A **measure, a decomposition and a warning**, all three directly usable. The measure is the **damage score against a matched control**: any claim that some property of a model's internals changed because of X needs a control arm that received the same budget of ordinary training, and the sign convention (positive always worse, zero at the start) makes a table of generations readable at a glance — our battery should report drift this way rather than as raw metric values. The decomposition is the part worth importing wholesale: for a frozen read-out, separate *stale dictionary* (a mismatch that adaptation repairs, bounded by principal-angle rotation against the eigengap) from *representation damage* (what adaptation cannot repair), and report both, because here the first is most of the reconstruction gap and the second is where the feature-level loss lives. That maps onto our own probes exactly: a probe whose accuracy drops after a fine-tune may be stale rather than invalid, and the cheap check is to refit it and see what stays broken. The warning is the sharpest single sentence in the paper's frame — **reconstruction is a constraint on a subspace, not on the coordinates inside it**: an orthogonal change of basis leaves reconstruction identical and full-representation probing exactly invariant while scrambling which individual latents carry what, which is a proof-shaped reason that our sparse-probe, single-feature and top-k claims are the ones that rot first when a model moves, and that a pooled or full-space probe will hide it. Practical corollary for our monitoring: keep a top-k sparse-probe column beside every full-representation column, since the gap between them is the only cheap detector of redistribution. The negative result to remember is the metric disagreement on Gemma — localisation, causal removal and probe perturbation need not move together, so a single "feature usefulness" number would have reported either nothing or the wrong thing; coreference arxiv-2609.35686 (probing and intervention dissociate) and arxiv-2611.20371 (fit and interventional faithfulness dissociate), and note this paper's own variant of the confound: a probe that keeps working because the information is *still there* is not evidence that the feature it was reading is *still the same feature*.
