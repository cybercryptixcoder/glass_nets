# When Attribution Patching Lies: Diagnosis and a Second-Order Correction

- **Key:** arxiv-2606.09899
- **Authors / organisation:** Luyang Zhang; Jialu Wang (Carnegie Mellon University; University of California, Santa Cruz)
- **Date:** 2026-06-05 (v1)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.09899
- **Kind:** paper

- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

Attribution patching is the workhorse of modern circuit work: one backward pass gives a cheap first-order estimate of how much each component's activation matters, and those estimates are then used as the *evidence* for circuit membership. The authors ask when that estimate can be trusted, how large its error can be for a given component, and whether the error can be repaired without paying for exact interventions everywhere. Their starting suspicion is that the field's existing repairs are aimed at the wrong object — they correct the nonlinearity at the patched component, while the error may originate in what the rest of the network does with the perturbation. The larger goal is to convert a widely used heuristic into an instrument with a built-in validity check: keep the cheap screen, know which of its outputs are suspect, and spend real compute only on those.

## What they studied

Attention heads (outputs before the out-projection), pre-activation and post-activation MLP neurons, whole MLP layer outputs, and the full residual vector, across seven checkpoints in five families: GPT-2 small, Pythia-410M and Pythia-2.8B (GeLU), Qwen2.5-1.5B (SwiGLU), Gemma-2-2B and Gemma-2-9B (GeGLU), Llama-3.1-8B (SwiGLU). The unit of analysis is a single (component, prompt) pair scored on a scalar read-out — the log-probability of the correct next token — under a matched-input perturbation. Three task settings: Indirect Object Identification (name-swap corruption, 50 templated prompts), Greater-Than (random-token replacement at position 3, 200 prompts), and generic factual completion (20 prompts, extended to 55 and 35 for selected sweeps). One appendix extends the unit to SAE features (JumpReLU, 768 to 24,576 features, GPT-2 layers 5 and 9).

## How they did it

The argument is a Taylor decomposition rather than a new attribution rule. Write the true patching effect as the change in the read-out when a clean activation is replaced by its counterfactual, and the attribution-patching estimate as the gradient dotted with the perturbation. The gap is then exactly the second-order term — a Hessian quadratic form along the patch direction — plus a cubic remainder. Under a path-local assumption that the Hessian is Lipschitz along the segment between the two activations, the remainder is bounded by a constant times the cube of the perturbation norm, which they verify empirically by probing the Hessian at three interpolation points. From this they define a *reliability score*: the absolute quadratic term normalised by the size of the first-order estimate. It is computable without ever measuring the true effect, and the proposition says the relative error is pinned by it up to a slack factor. Correction is one Hessian-vector product — two backward passes with the first graph retained — which removes the quadratic term and leaves only the cubic remainder. Because a single step overshoots when the perturbation is outside the local convergence radius, a multi-step variant splits the patch into K equal sub-steps and accumulates a quadratic correction at each intermediate point, which should shrink the residual as one over K squared; this is measured, not assumed. Finally, the selective workflow computes the reliability score for every component, applies the correction only to those above a threshold, and a second proposition bounds the total error of that mixture. Comparisons are against exact activation patching (the ground truth), Integrated Gradients, Integrated Hessians and a softmax-gradient-repair method, at matched numbers of backward passes, with a primary metric of relative error over the five highest-effect components; inference is paired bootstrap over prompts with 1,000 resamples.

## What counts as an explanation here

Not a mechanistic account of any behaviour — the paper explains the *error* of a measurement tool. Its product is a diagnostic and a correction: a per-component score saying whether a first-order estimate is likely to be wrong, a second-order estimate that is provably closer, and an error budget for a pipeline that corrects only a subset. It is meant to be complete for the narrow question of numerical reliability of activation-level attribution, and deliberately silent on circuit completeness or minimality. Its predictions are sharp and falsifiable: the network-level curvature should exceed local activation curvature with little correlation, local-curvature repairs should therefore fail (and can fail worse than doing nothing), the score should concentrate where the true error concentrates, and the multi-step residual should fall at the predicted rate.

## How they judged success

Top-5 relative error against activation-patching ground truth (mean absolute error over the five largest-effect components, normalised by the ground-truth score range), with prompt-level medians and bootstrap intervals; Kendall rank correlation and NDCG over all heads for the global ranking; top-k head overlap at the known circuit size; the Mechanistic Interpretability Benchmark's circuit performance recovery and metric-deviation areas for circuit-level faithfulness; detection metrics (rank correlation between the score and true relative error, ROC area for flagging errors above 50%) for the diagnostic; exact-recovery cases at low curvature; robustness across three corruption styles (random token, cross-prompt resample, zero corruption) and a naturalistic entity-swap corruption; a prompt-count stability curve; and wall-clock timings on a single GPU for the full and selective pipelines.

## What they found

The dominant error is the network's downstream response, not the local nonlinearity. Network-level curvature exceeds the local activation curvature by 2–66 times across three families with essentially no correlation, and correcting with the local second derivative produces errors above 750% — worse than leaving the estimate alone. That single fact explains why earlier local repairs are structurally incomplete: most of the downstream path is linear, so it carries little of the local curvature unless a downstream nonlinearity happens to align with the patch direction. The correction works broadly. Standard one-step Hessian correction cuts top-5 relative error by roughly 72–90% on attention heads and 56–67% on pre-activation neurons, and by about two thirds on the residual stream, while post-activation neurons barely move (2–7%) — exactly as expected once the nonlinearity has already been applied. On Pythia-410M it beats both prior repairs under matched conditions: head error 4.4% to 1.0% and neuron error 35.2% to 11.7%, against 34.1% and 40.9% reductions for the softmax-repair and region-matching baselines on heads, and the neural-curvature baseline degrades head recovery in 26 of 27 task-and-threshold settings, by as much as 21.8 points on the largest model's factual completion. Multi-step correction tracks the predicted one-over-K-squared decay: on the one setting where the perturbation clearly exceeds the local radius (Pythia-410M IOI), the single step catastrophically overshoots, raising error from 22.3% to 47.5%, while multi-step recovers monotonically to 18.0% at five sub-steps, with 5 outperforming integrated gradients at seven times that method's cost on the same task, and 18.0% versus 17.3% meaning the two are close rather than one dominating. At matched cost the multi-step variant ties integrated gradients on seven of nine tasks and wins on the two IOI settings; integrated Hessians trail it by 3.8–7.2 points because their path weighting puts a coefficient of about one quarter on the quadratic term where the exact Taylor correction needs one half — a mismatch that survives accurate curvature estimation. At 8B scale integrated gradients is quoted as about 25 GPU-days per task and integrated Hessians as undemonstrated, while one-step correction finishes in single-digit GPU-hours and cuts error by 5–57% there, with the multi-step variant reaching an 82% reduction on the 8B IOI task. The diagnostic behaves as advertised: the score concentrates precisely in the layers that form the IOI circuit, and where it is stratified it detects components with greater than 50% error at areas above 0.97 in the middle and upper quartiles of perturbation and effect size (pooled area 0.70, rank correlation 0.48, 89% recall at 36% precision when flagging 40% of components). Selective correction captures 91% of the full correction's gain at 26% of its cost, and at a fixed threshold flags between 7% and 19% of components while cutting median error by 10–30%. Circuit recovery improves where it matters most, at the boundaries: on GPT-2 IOI the top-20 overlap goes from 95% to 100%, driven by a single duplicate-token head whose effect was underestimated 4.4 times and which the score flagged strongly (rank 7 in ground truth, 27 under first-order attribution, 12 after correction). At circuit level, however, the picture is mixed and the authors say so: multi-step correction improves faithfulness on Gemma-2-2B IOI, Gemma-2-2B multiple-choice and GPT-2 IOI, but *worsens* it on Llama-3.1-8B IOI, where corrected edge scores over-concentrate on already-dominant edges and the recovered circuit exceeds the model's own performance at mid-sparsity, and on a distributed arithmetic task the joint input-level interpolation path beats every edge-level method by a wide margin. On SAE features the same correction reduces per-feature error by 81–86% on GPT-2 IOI at two layers.

## Limits and open problems they name

Single-token perturbations are the main scope: extension to multi-token semantic corruptions is future work, with only a preliminary entity-swap test (54% and 59% error reduction). Gains are demonstrated to 9B parameters. The correction improves component-level accuracy but says nothing about circuit completeness or minimality, and it interacts badly with circuit-level fidelity on some tasks, as above. The selective pipeline's bound is stated for independently patched components; joint patches introduce cross terms that affect aggregate circuit metrics and would require a quadratic number of Hessian-vector products to measure. The smoothness assumption is verified empirically rather than proven for transformers, and the third-order constant is a path-local lower bound probed in one direction, holding for 82% of GPT-2 and 95% of Gemma-2-2B component-prompt pairs with median slack of 3.4 and 7.6 times. SAE curvature deserves separate treatment because the encoder's own nonlinearity is an additional source. I would add, marked as mine: the ground truth throughout is single-component activation patching against a scalar log-probability read-out, so "correct" here means correct for that protocol — the correction makes a cheaper estimator agree with a more expensive one, and both can be wrong about the mechanism; the recovery wins are reported at the thresholds where the answer is already nearly known (a 95%-to-100% top-20 overlap on a task whose circuit is published), so the practical value is concentrated in the ranking boundary and is unmeasured on a task without ground truth; and the reliability score's pooled detection quality (area 0.70) is much weaker than its stratified quality, so an unthresholded use of it would flag a large fraction of components.

## What we take from it

A measure, a diagnostic and a warning, all three reusable as-is. The **measure**: relative error of an attribution estimate over the top-effect components against exact patching, at matched backward-pass cost — a scoring protocol that makes any two localisation methods comparable and prices accuracy in the same currency as compute, which is what our own attribution claims should report. The **diagnostic**: the reliability score is computable for every component without the ground truth, so it turns "our ranking may be off" into a per-component flag, and its layered concentration gives a cheap map of where a circuit is *numerically* fragile as opposed to merely important. The **warning** is the one that generalises furthest beyond this paper: **a first-order read-out is only as good as the nonlinearity the perturbation traverses downstream, and the fix is a second-order term, not a better-behaved first-order rule.** That reframes a pattern this folder keeps meeting — sites that score high under gradient attribution and are inert under intervention, and attribution graphs that are predictive in aggregate but wrong at specific nodes — as expected numerical behaviour rather than as evidence that linear-attribution explanations are conceptually false. Second, the mixed circuit-level results are the honest lesson: a per-component estimator can become strictly more accurate and still make a *worse* circuit, because accuracy at the top of a ranking and reranking at its boundary are different objectives, and because distributed tasks need joint rather than independent scoring. Any of our write-ups that goes from "component estimates improved" to "the circuit is more faithful" inherits an unreplicated step. Third, a practical shortcut worth keeping: the multi-step residual's one-over-K-squared shape is a *predicted* rate rather than a tuned hyperparameter, so a correction whose error does not fall on that schedule is a signal that the local-smoothness premise has failed at that site — a free self-audit for any second-order correction we ever implement.
