# Routes of Reasoning: Reading and Reshaping Test-Time Compute in MoE Models

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.05919
- **Authors / organisation:** Shengjie Wang, Mohsen Fayyaz, Nanyun Peng; University of California, Los Angeles
- **Date:** 2026-11-04
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05919
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

Reasoning models spend test-time compute on several different activities inside one response — proposing an approach, deriving, recalling, checking, restating — and the paper's starting observation is that much of that spend happens *after* the answer is already right. Existing levers (token budgets, brevity prompts, activation steering) regulate how long a trace is, not which activity is being paid for. The authors ask whether the router of a sparse Mixture-of-Experts model, which is the only place a model chooses a small subset of parameter blocks per token, can serve as a view of that choice: read the behaviour of the current span from routing, check that the association is not dataset-specific, then use it to edit inference. It is a step toward the folder's larger goal from a different side — instead of naming features in the residual stream, it names *functions of a reasoning step* and looks for them in the one component that decides which weights run.

## What they studied

Four open-weight MoE reasoning models, all instruction-tuned: Qwen3-30B-A3B (48 MoE layers, 128 routed experts, top-8, width 2048), Qwen3.6-35B-A3B (40 layers, 256 routed experts, top-8 plus one shared expert), Gemma-4-26B-A4B-it (30 layers, 128 routed, top-8) and GPT-OSS-120B (36 layers, 128 routed, top-4, about 5.1B active). Readout work is confined to Qwen3-30B-A3B; steering runs on all four. The unit of analysis has two forms: a **token's routing state** (its 128 or 256 pre-top-k router logits, or the binary selection indicators) and, at the level of association, a **layer–expert pair**. Labels are six reasoning behaviours — planning, execution, restatement, fact, reflection, conclusion — assigned per sentence/clause span by an LLM judge shown the whole trace, then projected onto tokens. Readout data are 330 traces over GSM8K, NuminaMath and AIME 2023 (78,955 segments); the cross-dataset association corpus adds MATH500, LiveCodeBench, GPQA, MMLU-Pro and CommonsenseQA to reach 330 traces per source across five domains.

## How they did it

Everything is linear and training-free. Router logits are centred within each token and an L2-regularised multinomial logistic probe is fitted, with regularisation strength and layer chosen on a dev split and macro-F1 reported on held-out tokens with problem-bootstrap intervals. The same recipe is run on 2,048-dimensional residual states at the same positions so the router can be compared against the hidden state under matched split, preprocessing and tuning budget, and the number of tokens per trace used by the token probes is capped at 200.

Two surface checks follow: a segment-level readout compared against probes on input embeddings, TF-IDF and trace position, and a drop-the-first-five-tokens test that asks whether the signal lives in the opening words that announce a behaviour. For expert identification, each expert is scored by a dataset-relative risk difference — its increase in selection probability under a behaviour, net of its baseline usage in that dataset — giving a 128-expert profile per behaviour per layer per dataset, and top-10 sets are ranked by absolute risk difference. Profiles are never pooled across domains: every comparison of "same behaviour" versus "different behaviour" is between profiles drawn from different datasets, which is what lets the correlation measure behaviour rather than domain. Top-10 sets are then used to rank segments in a target dataset the set was not selected on, and a separate supervised probe on selection fractions tests whether domain-general behaviour classification is possible at all.

Steering is the (de)activation operator inherited from prior work: at chosen layers, the selected experts' log-probabilities are pushed above the running maximum (activation) or below the minimum (deactivation) by a margin, and the softmax, top-k and gate renormalisation are recomputed, leaving shared experts untouched. Expert sets and layers are selected on a development split disjoint from each test benchmark, and compared against unmodified routing, an equally sized random expert set with the same edit, and a brevity system prompt. The mechanism analysis cuts each trace at its first answer and steers only one side, then, at real stage boundaries, rewinds one stage (~16 tokens) and regenerates ~170 tokens twice from the same seed with and without suppression.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **function-to-parts map plus a lever**: this layer's experts are disproportionately recruited by spans that do X, the set generalises to unseen domains, and suppressing it changes how much of X the model spends. It is explicitly partial and comparative — the router is claimed to carry about as much behaviour information as the residual stream at half the width, not more — and it is a readout-plus-intervention pair rather than a circuit: nothing is said about what an expert computes or how the selected set composes. The authors state the ceiling of their own claim directly: association guides a useful intervention but does not establish what an expert causally controls. The mechanism story ("planning launches each round of re-checking") is a stated interpretation supported by a cut at the first answer, an off-by-one-stage regeneration, and a release experiment, not by a predicted quantity.

## How they judged success

Readout: held-out macro-F1 at a dev-selected layer, with 2,000-resample problem-bootstrap 95% intervals, paired intervals for the router-versus-residual comparison, and a majority-class baseline (0.096). Surface controls: segment-level F1 against embeddings, TF-IDF and position; first-five-token versus rest-of-segment pooling; a width- and coverage-matched control (residual states from the same nine layers projected to 128 dimensions each). Association: same- versus different-behaviour profile correlation with a within-trace label-permutation null; cross-dataset Jaccard of top-10 sets against a permuted-label null of 0.202; share of routing mass the sets carry; source-to-target segment-ranking AUC over 180 directed source–target–behaviour comparisons per layer; and a supervised transfer probe on selection fractions from four layers. Steering: Δ generated tokens and Δ accuracy in 16 model–benchmark settings, each configuration fixed on a development split, against three baselines above; a behaviour-by-behaviour suppression comparison on 100 MATH500 problems; a first-answer cut; and a keyword-coded count of the next plan after a fixed cut.

## What they found

**Readout.** At a single token, one router layer (L12, chosen on dev) reaches 0.608 macro-F1 on held-out tokens against 0.096 for the most-frequent behaviour, while the best residual layer reaches 0.658. Concatenating router logits over a pre-specified nine-layer window (L8–L16, 1,152 dimensions) closes the gap at 0.659 — a paired gain of 5.1 points over one router layer ([4.1, 6.1]) and within 0.1 point of the residual probe ([−0.8, 1.0]) at 56% of its width; the width-matched residual control reaches 0.657, so parity is not an artefact of covering nine layers, and widening the window does not help. **Surface.** Router features beat input embeddings (0.624), TF-IDF (0.566) and trace position (0.124) at segment level (0.709); pooling after dropping the first five tokens gives 0.677, above 0.626 from those five tokens alone and near the 0.693 of the full segment. Mean-pooling router states over a segment gives 0.708 at L12 and 0.731 over the window, against 0.743 from the best residual layer. **Association.** At L12, same-behaviour profile correlation is 0.729 against −0.107 for different behaviours; top-10 sets have mean cross-dataset Jaccard 0.511 against a 0.202 null, six of the top ten are shared by all six datasets for planning, reflection and conclusion, and the sets carry 15–23% of routing mass. Source-to-target AUC at L36 averages 0.768 (reflection 0.863, conclusion 0.811, restatement 0.745, execution 0.631) and stays at 0.7254 overall after dropping segment openings; the supervised transfer probe reaches 0.561 macro-F1 across domains against 0.701 within a dataset and 0.096 for majority class. **Steering.** All sixteen settings shorten reasoning: median −30.4% tokens, up to −63.3% (Qwen3.6 on AICrypto); accuracy is unchanged or higher in fourteen settings and down by at most 0.5 points in the other two. Savings are largest where traces are most padded (−43.2% on the easier MATH500 and AICrypto, −24.9% on AIME and GPQA-Diamond), and on AIME accuracy *rises* 2.1–3.3 points in three of the four models. Random equal-size edits *increase* tokens in 14 of 16 settings while often hurting accuracy, and the internal edit beats the brevity prompt on tokens in all 16 settings. **Mechanism.** Every behaviour-targeted set that worked turned out to suppress planning: on 100 MATH500 problems, −200 planning pairs cuts tokens 22%, whereas suppression of reflection (+5%) and restatement (+1%, with more than twice the post-answer checking rounds) lengthens traces, and suppressing execution lengthens them 52% or, harder, breaks accuracy to 40%. Cutting at the first answer shows where it acts: before the cut suppression changes almost nothing (first answer at 422 tokens against 441), while after it, suppression alone reproduces the full effect and lifts accuracy from 84% to 97%. Regenerating from a stage boundary with the same seed, planning is not silenced but redirected — wrap-up plans outnumber new angles 68 to 11 under suppression against 23 to 23 unmodified, and thinking ends within ~170 tokens 17% of the time instead of 5%; suppressing for one window only (wrap-ups 43 to 21) barely moves the 6% end rate, because the postponed check returns when suppression lifts.

## Limits and open problems they name

Every label comes from a single LLM judge with no human agreement reported, and the hardest boundary (planning versus reflection when a sentence both doubts and proposes) is the one the associations rely on; a different codebook could move them. Steering makes reasoning cheaper, not better — boosting behaviour-associated experts did not reliably improve accuracy, so the router is a lever on how much is computed rather than on whether the answer is right. The largest model studied is 120B, well below current open MoE scales, so generality is untested. They also note that association does not establish what an expert causally controls, and leave longer-horizon and agentic tasks for future work.

## What we take from it

1. **A matched-width, matched-coverage comparator for any "cheap state carries it too" claim.** The router-versus-residual test is only convincing because the competing readout was given the same nine layers, the same 1,152 dimensions (PCA-projected) and the same tuning budget, and the paired interval was printed. Our own white-box model will keep making this shape of claim — a small readout of some intermediate state matching a big one — and this is the recipe that makes it falsifiable.
2. **The dataset-relative risk difference as the association score**, and the permutation null beside it. Subtracting each dataset's own baseline selection rate is what stops "this expert is used a lot on math" from being read as "this expert is for planning"; the within-trace label shuffle preserves trace membership and label counts, so the null is hit by the exact confound the score is trying to remove. This is a reporting pattern we can lift wholesale for our own component-to-behaviour associations.
3. **The cut-at-the-first-answer design as a locality instrument.** Splitting a trace at the point where the answer is known, and steering one side only, converts "the edit shortens reasoning" into "the edit acts on post-answer computation" — and the same-prefix, same-seed regeneration then shows what the model does instead of what it stops doing. Both are cheap and both are about *where* an effect lives, which is the question our own interventions keep leaving open.
4. **A warning about the direction of a win:** here every successful configuration suppressed *planning*, the one behaviour that looked most necessary and least offensive. Their explanation (planning opens each round of re-checking, so suppressing it removes whole rounds rather than the checks inside them) is the general lesson: the component whose removal shortens a process may be the *organiser* of the repeated part, not the repeated part itself, and the behaviour-target's name is a poor guide to where the lever is.
