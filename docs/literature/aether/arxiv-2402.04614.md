# Faithfulness vs. Plausibility: On the (Un)Reliability of Explanations from Large Language Models

- **Key:** arxiv-2402.04614
- **Authors / organisation:** Chirag Agarwal, Sree Harsha Tanneru, Himabindu Lakkaraju — Harvard University (Cambridge, MA); correspondence to the first author at a Harvard Business School address
- **Date:** 2024-02 (v1 2024-02-07; this version v3 2024-03-14)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04614
- **Kind:** essay (position review / call for community action)
- **Read from:** full text (v3 HTML, ~50k chars, read end to end; no experiments to read — see Limits)
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
The authors want the field to stop treating a *readable* explanation as evidence of a *correct* one. They observe that language models are trained on huge human text corpora and then tuned with human feedback, so producing text that a person finds sensible is the thing they are optimised for; describing their own computation is not. Their goal is a vocabulary for that gap and a research agenda to close it, with the argument that in high-stakes deployment — medicine, law, finance — an explanation that convinces without being true is worse than no explanation, because it removes the reviewer's reason to check. The larger aim is transparent deployment of LLMs rather than continued deployment on the strength of plausible prose.

## What they studied
No models, datasets or experiments of their own. The object of study is the *explanations themselves* — the paper's defined class of self-explanations (SEs), which covers three forms: chain-of-thought, token-importance highlighting and counterfactual "if you changed this word" statements. Two properties are defined by stipulation and then examined for the three forms: plausibility (does a human find it coherent with human reasoning) and faithfulness (does it represent the model's actual reasoning). Case material is illustrative only: arithmetic and general-knowledge chains, a sentiment sentence with a highlighted word, a truthfulness-checking example where option order is swapped, and a safety exchange with a jailbreak-by-framing pair — all produced with a mid-2023 chat model.

## How they did it
The method is conceptual and argumentative, not experimental. They (1) define self-explanations and name the three variants with a worked example of each; (2) define plausibility and faithfulness as the two axes; (3) survey how existing work has *measured* faithfulness, restricting themselves to black-box tests because billion-parameter or closed models make gradients, saliency maps and internal probes impractical: the swap-the-options and perturb-the-highlighted-token designs attributed to one group, and the truncate-the-chain halfway / inject-a-mistake-into-the-chain designs attributed to another, the latter scored as an area-over-the-curve of consistency against the fraction of the explanation shown or corrupted; (4) argue that current training pushes these two axes apart; (5) walk through the two off-diagonal quadrants with a scenario for each; (6) list what the community should build. The comparison they draw throughout is between what an explanation claims and what the model does under an intervention on the input or on the explanation — but they perform no such intervention.

## What counts as an explanation here
Their unit is the model's own natural-language account of its answer, in the three forms above. The understanding on offer is *taxonomic and normative*: two definitions, a two-by-two of which property an application needs, and an argument about how training creates the mismatch. Nothing here predicts behaviour or is meant to; the paper is explicit that no agreed faithfulness metric exists and that measuring it is an open problem. The one predictive-looking claim is structural: because the objective rewards reviewer-pleasing text, plausibility can be raised independently of faithfulness, so the two columns have to be reported separately rather than assumed to move together.

## How they judged success
No metric, baseline or benchmark of their own — there is nothing to score. The evidence adduced is prior literature plus hand-built examples, and the paper's own success criterion is the reader's acceptance of the distinction and of the agenda it implies. Where it describes measurement it does so in the terms of the cited work: fraction of predictions that change after perturbing features the explanation called unimportant or important; area-over-the-curve in the fraction of explanation shown or corrupted. Their explicitly stated gap is that no standardised faithfulness benchmark exists and that "faithful" has no consensus operationalisation.

## What they found
Argument rather than result. (1) Plausibility is nearly free: models are trained on human text and rewarded by human raters, so coherent-sounding reasoning is the default setting, and the optimization target they call RLHF plausibly pulls directly away from faithful self-report. (2) Faithfulness is expensive to establish and currently unmeasured — the field's existing tools only *disprove* it (a counterexample) or proxy it (post-hoc reasoning), so absent a positive test the honest reading of any self-explanation is "plausible, unverified". (3) Their two off-diagonal examples are the useful content: a chain that reasons about a medically plausible feature while the true driver is something spurious like the day of the week, and a safety case where the model names the offending concept while refusing, then complies when the same concept arrives wrapped in an emotional framing — evidence, as they read it, that the refusal came from a surface filter rather than from the consideration the explanation cited. (4) The demanded property depends on the use: a diagnostician needs faithfulness even when it is unreadable, a learner needs plausibility even when it is untrue.

## Limits and open problems they name
It is a position paper with no experiments, no metric and no number — every empirical claim is borrowed. The definitions are stipulative and, as they concede, offer no way to *test* faithfulness in general; no benchmark standardises it; the causal story about RLHF is offered as a suspicion. The remedies they propose are directions rather than methods: domain fine-tuning on curated explanation data, in-context exemplars of faithful explanations, and mechanistic interpretability mapping internal components to reasoning steps. Notably, the third is the one route they name that could ever ground a faithfulness score, and they do not connect it to the black-box tests they describe — nor do they note that a mechanistic read-out would make the swap-the-options test unnecessary rather than merely supportive.

## What we take from it
**A framing and a warning, plus one cheap habit.** (1) *Framing:* for our white-box account, this is the paper that states the criterion our read-outs have to satisfy — an explanation counts as explaining when it tracks what the network does under intervention, not when it reads well. Cite it for the vocabulary, not for evidence, and say so. (2) *Warning:* both of its off-diagonal examples are ones our own probes could have caught, and neither was caught by reading the explanation; the day-of-week case is exactly the failure a probe on a spurious feature would expose, and the safety case is the folder's refusal-from-a-shallow-signal pattern (`arxiv-2406.09289`, `arxiv-2505.23556`). (3) *Habit:* report plausibility and faithfulness as two columns. When we generate a natural-language read-out, the plausibility column costs a human or model rating and the faithfulness column costs an intervention; a single "quality" score silently averages them, which is the paper's complaint in miniature. **Shortcut:** the swap-the-options probe is a two-line input edit and is the cheapest faithfulness check in this folder — swap option order and see whether the stated reason changes while the answer should not. **Do not** import its implicit claim that because faithfulness is unmeasured, no explanation is trustworthy; that is a statement about the state of evaluation, and `arxiv-2403.10949` and `arxiv-2505.13763` show two working instruments.
