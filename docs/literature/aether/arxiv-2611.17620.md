# Safety Side Effects of Preventative Persona Steering: An Empirical Audit

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.17620

## What they wanted, and why
A persona vector is a direction in activation space for a temperament-level behaviour such as sycophancy or hallucination. Two ways to remove the behaviour follow: subtract the vector at generation time, or add it during fine-tuning so the optimizer never has to encode the trait at all, then drop the vector before evaluation. The second route has been recommended on the grounds that it leaves general capability untouched. The authors' complaint is that this recommendation was validated on capability and on the target trait, and never on whether the model still refuses things it should refuse. The goal is an audit: hold the recommendation to the standard a deployment team would hold it to, where a model that is more capable and less sycophantic but newly compliant with harmful requests has not been improved. It is a step toward treating a steering vector as a change to the whole model rather than to one behaviour.

## What they studied
Three instruction-tuned models: Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (the two the recommendation was made on) and Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct as a scale test. Two traits: sycophancy and hallucination. The unit of intervention is a single residual-stream layer per model per trait — layer 20 (sycophancy) and 16 (hallucination) on Qwen-7B, layer 16 for both on Llama-8B, and layers scaled by relative depth for the 14B (34 and 27), since no published layer choice covered it. The unit of measurement is the whole model's behaviour on three safety benchmarks plus trait expression, coherence and MMLU.

## How they did it
The persona vectors were extracted with the cited pipeline left unmodified, so any difference between conditions is attributable to how the vector is applied rather than how it was derived. Three conditions were compared per trait per model. (1) A fine-tuned base: train on the trait data with no steering, which isolates the effect of steering from the effect of fine-tuning. (2) Preventative steering: add the vector to the residual stream through a forward hook during that same fine-tuning, and remove it before evaluation. (3) Inference-time steering: subtract the vector from the residual stream of the untouched Instruct model during generation, no weight change. The steering coefficient was swept coarsely for both steered conditions. At every coefficient the preventative arm was scored on attack success (HarmBench, with its official classifier), a rubric-scored jailbreak quality measure (StrongREJECT, scored by GPT-4o-mini) and unsafe-prompt compliance (XSTest, labelled full refusal / partial / full compliance by GPT-4), alongside trait expression and coherence from the cited scoring pipeline and MMLU. The inference-time arm was scored on trait expression and coherence only. Judge labels were hand-spot-checked to separate genuine harmful compliance from incoherent output.

## What counts as an explanation here
A causal one, in the strongest form the folder records: the explanation of the trait is a single direction, and the test of that explanation is a two-sided intervention — one that removes it at inference and one that prevents it from being written into the weights at all. The claim is not that the direction *is* the behaviour but that adding or subtracting it is sufficient to move the behaviour, and the audit's contribution is to widen what "the behaviour" means from the target trait to refusal behaviour in general. It is explicitly partial: one direction, one layer, no claim about what else shares the direction.

## How they judged success
Success for the original recommendation is a *non-result*: MMLU and coherence flat while the trait drops. The audit measures the same sweeps on three refusal benchmarks, counts a failure when a safety metric moves in a worse direction at coefficients where capability and trait suppression both look fine, and treats benchmark disagreement as a finding rather than noise. There are no confidence intervals and no repeated seeds — each sweep point is a single run — and the authors say so.

## What they found
Hallucination steering moves refusal far more than it moves hallucination. Sweeping from the low to the high coefficient, HarmBench attack success falls from 61.0% to 22.0% on Llama-3.1-8B, from 72.3% to 4.4% on Qwen-7B and from 91.2% to 6.3% on Qwen-14B, with StrongREJECT following the same trend. Over the same range trait expression stays at 70% or above on every model, and most of the safety shift has already happened at a coefficient where trait expression is still above 96% on all three. Capability gives no warning: coherence is flat or slightly rising across every sweep, and MMLU moves by at most 2.1 points within any sweep (69.6–70.3%, 58.0–62.9%, 74.7–76.2% for the three models). Two qualifications. The effect is non-monotonic on Llama, where attack success bottoms at 14.5% and climbs back to 22.0%. And XSTest does not follow the other two benchmarks: on both Qwen models HarmBench and StrongREJECT approach zero while XSTest compliance levels off near 25–30% (7B) and 37–43% (14B), so refusal is restored for the attack classes those benchmarks cover but not for all unsafe requests.

Sycophancy steering is the opposite shape: it suppresses its own trait more (trait expression 90.5% → 59.8% on Qwen-7B, 88.9% → 27.9% on Llama-8B) but moves safety only modestly, and the direction of movement depends on model and benchmark. On Llama, attack success rises 8.2% → 11.9% while XSTest compliance falls 8.5% → about 6.5%. On Qwen-7B both HarmBench (20% → 14%) and StrongREJECT (17% → 5%) fall while XSTest rises slightly. On Qwen-14B HarmBench ends where it began at 48%, StrongREJECT falls 67% → 61%, and XSTest compliance rises 42% → 59%. In every model at least one benchmark moves opposite to another.

The baseline itself is set by the fine-tuning data, not by the vector. With no steering, attack success on the same base model is 8% versus 61% on Llama, 20% versus 72% on Qwen-7B and 48% versus 91% on Qwen-14B, for sycophancy versus hallucination data. Training on the hallucination corpus appears to erode refusal by itself, so much of what preventative hallucination steering does may be to undo that erosion rather than to leave a neutral model alone.

## Limits and open problems they name
Single run per sweep point, a coarse sweep, and no confidence intervals, so nothing is claimed about the shape of the dose–response curve. The 14B layer choices were scaled from the 7B rather than selected, so that model's results may reflect a suboptimal layer. The three benchmarks probe different things and the findings may not extend to other benchmarks, adaptive attacks or other model families. The two methods cannot be compared at matched trait expression: inference-time steering is applied to the original Instruct model, whose trait level never reaches the fine-tuned model's range, so its sweep never arrives at the regime preventative steering lives in. And by the authors' reading the vectors are probably partly entangled with refusal behaviour — which means the observed effect cannot yet be attributed to a specific shared component.

## What we take from it
Three things. First, a **metric-set warning**: an intervention can leave the metric everyone validates on flat (MMLU moved ≤ 2.1 points here) and move a deployed behaviour by tens of points, and the metric that stayed flat is the one the recommendation was made on. For our battery, every steering result should carry a small panel of behaviours none of which is the target, because a single side-effect metric can agree with the target's success and still miss the change that matters. Second, **a zero-steering arm for fine-tuning studies**: the trait-data baseline (attack success 91% on Qwen-14B with no vector at all) is where most of the safety damage already sits, so "steering reduced harm" can mean "steering partially undid what the training data broke", and the arm costs one extra training run we are already doing. Third, **a benchmark-disagreement finding worth a habit**: here the sign of the effect flips between benchmarks in all three models, so a safety claim drawn from one benchmark is a claim about one benchmark's attack distribution — the cheap fix is to report the sign per benchmark and treat a sign flip as the finding rather than as noise. For our white-box model this is a reminder that a direction's behavioural footprint is wider than its label, and that the width has to be measured, not assumed.
