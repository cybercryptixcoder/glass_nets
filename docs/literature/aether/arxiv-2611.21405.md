# Shaping AI's Beliefs Before It Generates: Human Sense of Agency in Creative AI

## What they wanted, and why
Generative media tools resolve every detail a prompt leaves unspecified, and once a polished artefact exists the user's thinking tends to orbit it — the paper calls this anchoring, and argues it demotes the person from author to editor of the machine's interpretation. The authors' hypothesis is that the loss of agency is partly a matter of *when* the human is allowed to intervene: critique after generation is late, so the anchor is already set by the model. They therefore propose moving the human's intervention upstream, to the model's interpretation of the request, before any pixels or frames exist. The larger goal is a design account of shared agency between a person and a generative system, with the system's own uncertainty made into something a person can inspect and edit. The paper is explicit that its empirical part is a probe meant to generate hypotheses, not to confirm them.

## What they studied
Eight participants (2 female, 6 male) recruited inside one institution, five of them AI researchers or engineers, two creative interaction designers and one UX researcher; AI artists were deliberately excluded. The object of study is not a network's internals but a working multi-modal application, the Proactive Co-Creator (PaCC), which sits in front of Gemini text, image and video models and interposes an editable representation of the model's reading of the prompt. The unit of analysis is the participant's *felt* sense of agency, decomposed into agency, control, ownership and authorship, each rated across four aspects of creative work — intent, expression, process, outcome.

## How they did it
PaCC inserts a belief-control phase between prompt entry and generation, and it has three mechanisms. First, exactly three atomic clarifying questions per analysis cycle, each offering three to five selectable options plus a "You Decide" choice that delegates that specific decision back to the model. Second, a belief graph parsed out of the prompt (and any reference images) that marks entities the user stated explicitly (solid borders) separately from entities the model inferred (dashed), with typed relationships between them and per-entity attributes drawn from two or three candidate values; edits are applied as incremental JSON deltas rather than by rebuilding the graph. Third, modality-level media attributes — lighting, style, camera angle for images, tone for stories, motion, pacing and duration for video. A proactiveness tier (low, medium, high) modulates how much the model is allowed to invent in both the graph and the attribute panel, and the backend estimates uncertainty over candidate values but hides the raw probabilities from the interface to avoid clutter. Before generation, every edit, answer and selection is compiled into a refined prompt shown as a side-by-side diff for approval; the underlying pipeline is three schema-constrained calls per cycle (graph, clarifications, refinement), run with one call in parallel and a fallback to a faster model when the chosen one errors. In the study, participants saw a 10-minute demo, then spent 30 minutes on an unconstrained self-directed task across text, image and video, then filled in a 20-minute questionnaire rating PaCC overall and each feature on a five-point scale where 1 means diminished, 3 preserved and 5 augmented, alongside greater/equal/lesser comparisons against their habitual tools, followed by a semi-structured discussion.

## What counts as an explanation here
An editable *externalisation of the model's interpretation*: a graph of stated-versus-inferred entities and relationships, a set of surfaced assumptions with candidate alternatives, and a visible set of stylistic defaults, all of which the person can rewrite before generation. It is deliberately partial and deliberately non-numeric — the model's own uncertainty is computed server-side but withheld from the display as "cognitive clutter". It is not offered as a description of why the model would produce a given image; it is offered as a representation rich enough for a person to correct, and its claimed predictive use is limited to predicting what the generation step will treat as fixed.

## How they judged success
Self-report only: mean five-point Likert ratings per dimension and per feature, counts of participants rating each feature greater than, equal to or lesser than their usual tools, standard deviation and range of per-participant means as a dispersion measure, one-sample Cohen's d against the scale midpoint, and Pearson and Spearman correlations between dimensions at both the participant-mean and the item level. There is no control arm, no post-generation-critique baseline run in parallel, and no held-out task; the comparison against existing tools is retrospective. The paper states that its one-sample effect sizes measure distance from the midpoint and not a treatment effect, which is the correct scope for a single-arm design.

## What they found
Agency and process control were rated above the midpoint and above the participants' own tools: overall agency was compared as greater by 5 of 7 who answered, equal by 2, and control 6/2/0; mean overall ratings were 4.09 agency, 3.89 control, 3.22 ownership, 2.75 authorship on the five-point scale, and the dispersion tells a different story from the means — ownership SD 1.49 with a range of 1.2 to 5.0, authorship SD 1.13 over 1.5 to 5.0. Half the participants (P1, P2, P3, P8) reported high ownership with preserved or augmented authorship, while the other half attributed low ownership to exploring without a formed idea, to being steered by AI-generated multiple-choice options, to scepticism about training-data provenance, or to the difficulty of specifying style in words. Clarifying questions were the only feature every participant placed above their current tools on agency (8/0/0 of 8), and prompt refinement scored highest overall but with the widest disagreement (control SD 1.32, range 1.0 to 5.0). Authorship moved least and most variably: five of six who rated both control and authorship put authorship below control. By aspect, conceptual facets won and expression lost — expression was the lowest-rated aspect on all four dimensions (authorship 2.77 there against roughly 3.0 and 3.2 for process and intent). The belief graph split opinion in a way the numbers understate: it was named most useful by P1 and P2 and least useful by P5, P6, P7 and P8, with the objection that its structure did not match how the participants were thinking.

## Limits and open problems they name
n = 8, single arm, single institution, mostly technically literate participants, a 30-minute task, retrospective comparison against familiar tools, and no controlled A/B against a post-generation-critique loop — all stated plainly, and the paper's own framing is that its observations are directional. Beyond what they name: expression is the aspect their text-based controls cannot reach, so the mechanism is a conceptual-agency device with no story for style; the ownership half of the result is a polarisation rather than an effect, and the four reasons given for low ownership are not measured against each other; and the paper's own note that hiding the numbers was to avoid clutter means the uncertainty estimate is computed and never validated as useful to a person.

## What we take from it
A framing and a design warning, not a method. (1) **The reportable version of a single-arm mean is the mean *and* the dispersion *and* the split of the individual ratings**, and this paper shows why in one table: agency 4.09 (SD 0.84) reads like a success while ownership 3.22 (SD 1.49) with a range from 1.2 to 5.0 is a bimodal population summarised by its average. Our interpretation reports have the same hazard whenever a probe score is averaged over items or seeds, and the cheap fix is to publish the range and the per-item split next to the mean, as this paper does for its features. (2) **An interaction layer that hands a person candidate alternatives rather than a single resolved output can itself be read as steering** — four participants named the multiple-choice prompts as the reason they felt no ownership, which is the strongest cautionary datum here for any interface that offers "here are three ways to read your input": options are a constraint as much as a freedom, and which one it is depends on whether the user arrived with a formed intent. (3) **There is a clean instrument contrast worth copying**: the *dialogue* features (clarifying questions, prompt revision) were rated as improving authorship more often (5/1/1 for each) than the *visualisation* features (belief graph 3/2/2, attributes 3/3/1), which is the same structure our own battery meets when a plotted attribution earns less than the numbers it plots — the modality of the explanation, not only its content, changes what a reader can do with it.
