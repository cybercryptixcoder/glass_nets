# BBReach: Tight and Scalable Black-Box Reachability Analysis of Deep Reinforcement Learning Systems

- **Key:** arxiv-2211.11127
- **Authors / organisation:** Jiaxu Tian, Dapeng Zhi, Min Zhang (Shanghai Key Laboratory of Trustworthy Computing, East China Normal University); Si Liu (ETH Zurich); Peixin Wang (University of Oxford); Guy Katz (The Hebrew University of Jerusalem)
- **Date:** 2022-11-21
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.11127
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Reachability analysis is the standard way to prove that a learned controller keeps a system inside safe bounds, but the tools of 2022 all pay the same double price: they over-approximate the dynamics, which is unavoidable, and then also over-approximate the network, which the authors argue is not. Two approximations multiplied and iterated produce sets that grow faster than the truth, so the verdict comes back "unknown" rather than wrong — many unreachable states end up inside the computed reachable set. The second complaint is portability: each tool supports some architectures and activations and collapses on others, so a designer's choice of activation is silently a choice of verifier. The paper's aim is to remove the network term from the error budget entirely, so that what remains is only the dynamics approximation and what remains of the method is agnostic to network size, shape and activation.

## What they studied
Discrete-time deep-reinforcement-learning control systems: a policy network mapped state to action, applied to nonlinear dynamics, over a bounded horizon, for a reach-avoid property (reach the goal region, never enter the unsafe region). Seven reinforcement-learning benchmarks of dimension 2 to 6, with four network configurations per benchmark — two activation functions (tanh, ReLU) crossed with two sizes (two hidden layers of 20 units, three or four of 100, and 200 in one case) — giving 28 instances. The unit of analysis is an **interval box**: abstraction maps the continuous state space onto a uniform grid of boxes, and every state inside one box is treated as sharing one action.

## How they did it
The key move is a training-side commitment, not an analysis-side one. The policy network is retrained so that its input is the abstract state — the box's 2n coordinate vector rather than the n real coordinates — which makes the network constant on each box by construction. That is what licenses the black-box treatment: because every state in a box provably receives the same action, the analysis can ask the network once per box and receive a single action, with no interval, no relaxation and hence no network over-approximation. The reachable-set computation then proceeds step by step: take the boxes of the current reachable set, cut each one along the abstraction grid so that every piece lies inside a single abstract box ("interval segmentation"), look up the action for the enclosing box, and bound the one-step successor of the piece per dimension by solving a max and a min of the dynamics over the piece with the action held fixed. That last step is a real optimisation over a nonlinear function, solved numerically, since the network no longer appears in the objective at all. Cutting produces many boxes, and the successor of each may land anywhere, so the count would explode geometrically with the horizon; the paper's second contribution is to merge boxes before the next step. The adjacency relation is defined in three forms — one box contained in another, two boxes overlapping along exactly one dimension while their other coordinates agree within a threshold, and two boxes separated along exactly one dimension by less than the threshold — and merging takes the minimum bounding rectangle of the merged group. Instead of the brute-force repeated pairwise scan (cubic in the number of boxes), they pre-build an adjacency matrix and merge along connected components with a breadth-first search, which is quadratic; this deliberately assumes adjacency is transitive, so a chain of near neighbours collapses into one box. Initial-set partitioning gives parallel speedup across cores.

## What counts as an explanation here
A sound over-approximation of the reachable set as a union of interval boxes, per time step — the same form the rest of this folder calls a reachable-set account. It is a proof object, not a description or a surrogate: the soundness proof (a lemma per step, induction over trajectory length) is short and rests on the box-constant action. It is not intended to explain *why* the system behaves as it does, and it says nothing about features, directions or circuits — the network is deliberately treated as an opaque lookup. The one structural claim made about the network is a property of the *training procedure*: a network trained on abstract states is piecewise constant on the abstraction grid, and its piece structure is known to the verifier in advance rather than discovered.

## How they judged success
Three yardsticks, plus one ablation. (1) **Verification verdict and time** on all 28 instances against two state-of-the-art tools from the same literature (a Taylor-model propagator and a polynomial-regression approximator), reporting single-core and 20-core times, with a four-way outcome vocabulary: verified, unknown (could not conclude because the computed set was too loose), did-not-finish, and not-applicable (the tool cannot handle that activation). (2) **Visual tightness**: reachable-set boxes overlaid on simulation trajectories, with the criterion that the set is tight when the trajectories fill the boxes; the same picture is used both to judge tightness and to explain why one tool loses on one benchmark. (3) **A controlled ablation of the aggregation step**: interval-box count versus time step, with and without merging, on one benchmark and the same curves for the rest in the appendix; plus a sweep of the abstraction granularity (three finer and three coarser settings per benchmark) with the runtime split across segmentation, over-approximation and aggregation. No false-positive or false-negative counting against an exhaustive sample, and no statistical treatment.

## What they found
All 28 instances verify, against 12 for the Taylor-model tool and 13 for the polynomial-regression tool, which return 1 and 11 unknowns respectively. Where the competitors are simply inapplicable (the ReLU instances for the Taylor tool) the table has a dash, so a large part of the headline is a coverage win rather than a tightness win. On time the pattern is consistent: with 20 cores nearly everything finishes in a few seconds, and the slowest benchmark (a 4-dimensional system) takes 44.25 s at 20 cores and 814.3 s on one; single-core cost is below the competitors in most cells. The result the authors lean on most is the invariance to network size — the 4x200 networks cost about the same as the 3x100 ones, and in a few cells less — which follows directly from the design, since the network appears only as a constant per box. On tightness the honest reading is narrower than the abstract: they report *comparable* sets on most benchmarks, better on the one where the sample-based tool blows up in 6 dimensions, and **worse than the Taylor tool on one 2-dimensional benchmark**, which they attribute to the different routing decisions the differently-trained networks learned — i.e. their set is worse because their *system* is different, not because their method is looser. The ablation is the cleanest number in the paper: without merging the box count grows rapidly with the step, and with it the count stays "extremely small and stable", with aggregation plus one-step over-approximation consuming nearly all the runtime while segmentation is negligible. Granularity behaves as expected on the fine side (very fine grids are much more expensive) and, more interestingly, cost *decreases and then stabilises* as the grid coarsens, so there is a plateau rather than a single tuned optimum.

## Limits and open problems they name
Only interval boxes are used as the abstract domain; the conclusion names richer domains (zonotopes) as future work for further reducing over-approximation. The soundness argument is conditional on the box-constant-action training actually holding on the deployed network — the retraining is theirs and the networks under test are therefore *not* the networks the competitor tools were given, which the paper states plainly. The transitivity assumption behind the merge is a heuristic for efficiency rather than a proved property of the relation, and the threshold that decides adjacency is a preset hyper-parameter. The comparison against competitors is partly a comparison across different systems, and the tightness judgement is visual. No limitations section as such; the closing paragraph is the future-work sentence.

## What we take from it
A **method** and a **warning**, and this is the first paper in the closed-loop thread that makes the sound/cheap trade disappear by moving the cost into training rather than into analysis. The method is the sharpest version of a trick we should reuse directly: if the object you must reason about can be *made* piecewise constant on a grid you choose, then the analysis becomes combinatorial on the grid and analytic only on the dynamics, the exponential does not vanish (box count still multiplies per step) but it becomes a count you can *measure and hold flat* by merging. That is a different answer to the thread's recurring cost question: earlier papers enumerated sign patterns and paid in the network's own parameters (2^n per layer), this one enumerates grid cells and pays only in the reachable set's own footprint, which the merge bounds. And it explains the size-invariance result that no over-approximating tool can match: a 200-unit layer costs the same as a 20-unit one because the layer was never touched. The warning is about comparability, and it is the sharpest instance of it in the folder: the comparison the paper wins is against tools run on *different networks for the same task*, chosen by a training procedure and weight initialisation the authors control (and they say they picked small weights because one competitor degrades with large ones). Verified-vs-unknown counts across differently trained controllers are not a fidelity comparison, they are a demonstration that the method is applicable. Mechanical rule for us: **when the predictive account we build requires the model to have a property the un-analysed model does not have, we either state that the property is part of the artifact we ship or we measure the account on the model as found — because a verifier that quietly retrains its subject has bought its soundness with the system it was asked to check, and a verdict table mixing the two reads as a tightness result while being a coverage result.** Second, small and reusable: the four-way verdict vocabulary here (verified, unknown, did-not-finish, not-applicable) is the honest minimum for any tool comparison, and the "not-applicable" column is the one every benchmark table omits.
