## What they wanted, and why
Forecasters are increasingly fed known-future inputs, and in a controlled system some of those inputs are levers a decision maker can pull, re-pull, and compare before acting. The authors argue that a model conditioned on a prescribed future input is doing something weaker than what a decision maker needs: it must answer how the outcome *changes* when that input is swapped, from the same observed history. They name two separate gaps between that and what standard evaluation checks. First, fitting the trajectory that actually happened does not imply the response to an alternative action is right. Second, even a model with faithful alternative-action responses can rank candidate plans wrongly, because a planner consumes relative orderings rather than per-trajectory accuracy. The larger goal is to make intervention fidelity and decision quality first-class evaluation targets and to show a model trained on factual data alone can serve both.

## What they studied
Four simulator-grounded controlled systems, each with a hand-specified observation / action / target split: CityLearn (a single building, scalar battery charge/discharge, predicting state of charge and net electricity consumption; 28/1/2 dims, 24-step history, 12-step horizon), SUMO Cologne1 (single intersection, four-phase one-hot action, predicting directional queues, waiting time, arrivals; 25/4/6), a GreenLight tomato greenhouse (six continuous actuators, predicting CO2, temperature, humidity; 36/6/3, 96/16), and gym-invmgmt serial inventory with backlog (three replenishment quantities, predicting backlog and three echelon inventories; 15/3/4). So actions are continuous-scalar, one-hot-categorical, and bounded-continuous in different domains. The unit of the method is a **per-variable latent state** — every observed variable gets its own 144-dimensional slot, with a learned variable identity embedding — and the unit of the evaluation is a **held-out history crossed with a candidate action bank**, scored at four levels: factual prediction, intervention effect, offline decision, closed-loop planning.

## How they did it
The model is a latent state-space filter built around one transition that is reused verbatim for filtering and for rollout. A shared observation encoder reads each current value together with its first difference; a separate action encoder reads the *full* action vector plus its difference and produces a distinct control representation for each variable slot. The transition block is small — two blocks, four-head self-attention across variables with a learned scalar gate on the cross-variable message, a bounded sigmoid retention gate, and a SwiGLU candidate update — and writes the new state as a gated residual. During the observed history, each predicted state is corrected by an observation-driven update (a tanh delta scaled by a sigmoid gate). At the history boundary a frozen six-layer pretrained temporal backbone (EIDOS) reads the standardized target histories and, through a 60,321-parameter residual adapter whose scalar gate starts at exactly zero, nudges the terminal state; because the gate starts at zero the adapted model begins with identical predictions, so adaptation can only be a residual change. Rollout then applies the *same* transition under candidate future actions with no observation correction at all. The head decodes ordered quantiles (0.1/0.5/0.9) around an anchor of the last observed target. Training is two-stage and both stages optimize the same factual pinball loss: Stage A learns the encoders, transition, correction and head with the prior pathway off (10 epochs, Adam 1e-3, batch 256); Stage B freezes all of it plus the backbone and fits only the adapter (3 epochs, AdamW 1e-3, weight decay 1e-2). Checkpoints are chosen by factual validation MAE alone. Data are generated from the simulators with multiple behavior policies per domain (smooth control, randomized control, structured perturbation blocks, near-zero control) plus short "snippets" that pair a history prefix with one assigned future action plan; splits are disjoint calendar ranges, simulator seeds, or weather years depending on the domain. Every alternative-action outcome, candidate comparison and closed-loop episode exists only for evaluation. Baselines are five forecasters adapted to condition on future actions (Ridge-VARX, DLinear, PatchTST, iTransformer, TFT) and two latent world models (RSSM, MTS3), all given the same histories, action banks and CEM budget.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **replacement model plus an evaluation decomposition** — no mechanistic reading of the network is offered or attempted. The "understanding" claimed is that a single shared transition, applied under history correction and then under open-loop rollout, is sufficient to reproduce action-induced change and to order candidate plans; the explanation that action effects propagate through the latent dynamics (rather than being injected at the decode stage) is supported only by an ablation showing that removing structured action conditioning is much worse than feeding actions by simple concatenation. The paper's conceptual contribution is the three-level ladder — factual accuracy, intervention fidelity, decision quality — treated as three measurements that are related but not interchangeable, with the ladder itself made quantitative through rank reversal rates. Predictiveness is claimed at the level of *model orderings across the ladder*, not at the level of individual transition predictions.

## How they judged success
Four metrics, all lower-is-better. **Factual MAE/MSE** in target-wise standardized space. **Effect NMAE** = mean absolute error of the predicted alternative-minus-reference effect divided by the mean absolute true effect (so it is a ratio to the size of the effect being predicted, and equals 1.0 for any model whose predictions do not move with the action). **Macro Regret** = equal-weight average of normalized regret over three predefined per-domain objectives, with the simulator oracle as both the numerator's offset and the denominator's reference and uniform candidate selection setting the scale. **Closed-loop objective** under receding-horizon MPC with cross-entropy-method candidate optimization, all learned methods sharing the same episodes, candidate-generation rules and CEM budget, replanning from fresh simulator observations. Cross-level fidelity is measured two ways: for each held-out history and each unordered pair of the eight methods, a *reversal* is counted when the two methods' ordering flips between adjacent levels (ties dropped), giving a reversal rate; and per-history Spearman and Kendall τ_b across the eight methods are macro-averaged, with 2,000 bootstrap resamples of histories for intervals. A diagnostic (Figure 4) selects the best upstream method per history and reports the downstream penalty relative to the method that is actually best there — explicitly labelled an oracle diagnostic using realised test outcomes, not a selection procedure.

## What they found
ActTime ranks first in 12 of the 16 domain-by-setting cells (best average rank 1.50 against 2.25 for the strongest baseline) and takes the lowest factual MAE in all four domains (reducing it 12.2% on SUMO, 25.2% on GreenLight, 20.4% on Inventory against the strongest baseline) — so the alternative-action gains do not cost factual accuracy. But the ordering is not preserved across levels, and the paper's central numbers are the reversals: **17.8% of method pairs flip between factual prediction and intervention fidelity (macro Spearman 0.763, Kendall τ_b 0.643), and 28.4% flip between intervention fidelity and decision ranking (Spearman 0.514, τ_b 0.412)**; the increase appears in all four domains, with the effect-to-decision reversal rate ranging from 17.3% on SUMO to 41.3% on CityLearn. Concrete instances from Table 2: TFT wins intervention fidelity on CityLearn (Effect NMAE 0.0498, well below every latent world model) but is third-best on offline regret there (0.2880 against ActTime's 0.2798 and MTS3's 0.3105); ActTime trails TFT on SUMO effect (0.4825 against 0.4184) yet leads both offline regret (0.0221 against 0.0296) and closed-loop congestion (0.3036 against 0.3147); and on Inventory ActTime has the best effect NMAE (0.2244) but a *higher* offline regret than MTS3 (0.1563 against 0.1531). The diagnostic puts a size on the cost of choosing by the upstream metric: picking the prediction-best method instead of the effect-best raises Effect NMAE by 51.8% on CityLearn, 28.4% on SUMO, 5.1% on GreenLight and 26.6% on Inventory, and picking the effect-best method instead of the regret-best raises normalized regret by 0.251, 0.016, 0.031 and 0.101. Candidate ranking fidelity (per-history Spearman between predicted and simulator candidate costs) is best for ActTime in all four domains, macro-average 0.8661 against 0.8154 for TFT, and the advantage survives the metric change — best macro Spearman 0.8270, τ_b 0.7272, pairwise accuracy 0.8624 over the 12 domain-objective combinations, first on 11 of 12 under Spearman and 10 of 12 under each of the others. The structured ablation is reported as ratios to each domain's own full model (>1 = worse), so the column mixes very different metrics, and the underlying absolute table (Table 14) is what makes the pattern legible: dropping action conditioning is catastrophic exactly where it should be (Effect NMAE 6.393, offline regret 16.349, online objective 2.999; Effect NMAE is exactly 1.000 in all four domains there, which is the arithmetic definition of a model that ignores the candidate action), collapsing per-variable states into one shared state is the largest representational lesion (5.787 factual, 5.367 effect, 4.360 regret), removing observation correction roughly doubles the three offline metrics (2.137, 2.132, 2.215), and simply concatenating actions instead of the structured pathway costs less but degrades every column (1.059 factual, 1.035 effect, 1.137 regret). Two contained abnormalities sit in that table and should be read as such rather than smoothed: the simple-concatenation variant beats the full model on the offline regret of three of four domains individually (CityLearn 0.2808 against 0.2931, SUMO 0.0246 against 0.0247, Inventory 0.1466 against 0.1518) and on SUMO Effect NMAE (0.3526 against 0.5154), so a handful of cells is enough to make the macro-average read as a uniform loss when the per-cell record disagrees — and note the two tables are not on the same checkpoints (the harness's own full model has CityLearn offline regret 0.2931, the final checkpoint 0.2798), so the ablation is a within-harness comparison and not a reading of the headline model; the paired no-prior analysis shows the temporal prior hurts effect fidelity and offline regret on CityLearn (0.0703 against 0.0725; 0.2784 against 0.2798) and SUMO (0.4355 against 0.4825; 0.0207 against 0.0221) while helping factual MAE on three domains and the closed-loop objective in all four — a prior that improves the prediction metric and the online metric while nudging the intermediate level the wrong way is precisely the three-scores-three-answers pattern the paper is otherwise measuring in other people's models. Online gains are narrow (SUMO 0.3036 vs 0.3147 for TFT; GreenLight 0.2944 vs TFT's 0.2946 and RSSM's 0.3009) and ActTime is *not* best on CityLearn online, where RSSM leads at 0.8196 against ActTime's 0.8317 — the one domain where ActTime's cell-rank profile is worse than the other three.

## Limits and open problems they name
Everything is simulator-grounded, deliberately so, because same-history alternative-action outcomes do not exist in observational data; performance in these four simulators does not establish transfer to deployed systems, and the paper distinguishes predictive fidelity under controlled interventions from causal identification under unobserved confounding. Training on factual trajectories only means action effects are learned from whatever variation the behavior policies happened to cover; the authors state that extrapolation to action regimes unsupported by training data is untested and that uncertainty under such shift plus fast adaptation from limited interaction are open. Planning is finite-horizon MPC with CEM and longer-horizon or hierarchical control is untested; and although the head emits quantiles, the planner compares plans using point predictions, so the predicted uncertainty is not used for risk-sensitive planning — a natural extension they name.

## What we take from it
1. **A measure: divided-by-the-effect error.** Effect NMAE is not an error but an error *relative to the magnitude of the true effect*, so a model whose predictions ignore the action scores exactly 1.0 by construction — the ablation confirms it, since removing action conditioning returns exactly 1.0 in all four domains. That gives a prediction-under-intervention score a null that is set by the quantity being predicted rather than by a shuffling procedure. Our white-box model's intervention predictions should carry the same normalisation: error divided by the real effect magnitude, with 1.0 printed as the no-effect line. It sits beside the folder's oracle-deficit floor (run 941) as the cheaper, readout-free reference.
2. **A statistic: the rank-reversal rate between adjacent levels.** The paper's method is to take each *pair* of methods, check on each held-out item whether their ordering flips between two criteria, and report the fraction of pairs that flip (with Spearman as a companion). Reversals are scale-free across metrics that live on different units — which is why this works across four domains with incomparable objectives — and they are the right statistic for our recurring claim that "these are different properties": the reversal rate around 28% says that in more than a quarter of method pairs the better model on one measurement is the worse on the next. Any time our white-box model reports two metrics as "distinct", the pair-flip statistic is the number to print beside the correlation.
3. **A warning that lands on our own reporting habits: a model that wins every column can still be wrong at the level a consumer uses.** Here ActTime leads factual prediction, loses effect on two of four domains, and leads decision ranking; TFT leads effect on two domains and trails on ranking; no method is best on all three and the ladder changes winners between rungs. The reusable framing is that a metric can stay *informative* (positive aggregate correlation, here 0.51) while being a *bad selection criterion* (28.4% of pairs reversed). Those are different sentences and the folder has tended to write the first and use it as the second.
4. **A cheap diagnostic for our own tables: pick the best-by-upstream-metric per item and price what the downstream consumer loses.** Their Figure 4 reports the per-history penalty of choosing by the neighbouring metric — expressed as a relative error increase for the effect level and as an absolute regret increase for the decision level, because those units do not divide. This is the closest thing to a decision-relevant number in the paper and it needs only the two metrics plus realised outcomes, which our entries usually already have.
5. **A methodological precedent to keep in mind when reading structure ablations:** their own ablation reports show the simple-concatenation arm beating the full model on two of four domains for offline regret and on SUMO for effect fidelity, while the macro-average says it is worse. The paper reports the per-domain absolute table in the appendix, which is what makes the pattern visible. Aggregate-ratio ablations should ship with the per-domain absolute table, and a variant that beats the full model on a subset is a result to state, not a rounding to absorb.
