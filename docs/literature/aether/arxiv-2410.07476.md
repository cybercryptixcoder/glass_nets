## What they wanted, and why
Two published accounts of the same model on the same task disagree. One (Chughtai et al.) says a one-hidden-layer network trained on finite-group multiplication computes the operation through representation matrices, neuron by neuron; the next (Stander et al.) examines the same architecture and task and says the model does it with cosets of subgroups. The authors' starting point is that this divergence is itself evidence of how weakly the field evaluates explanations: if two careful groups can both claim a mechanistic account of a small model, then "we understood it" is not yet a measurable claim. Their aim is to find the structure both accounts missed, unify them, and — more importantly for us — to *evaluate* the resulting explanation with something that can pass or fail rather than with human plausibility. The larger goal is a science of interpretability in which an explanation earns its keep by producing guarantees.

## What they studied
One-hidden-layer, two-input ReLU networks trained on the binary operation of a finite group: inputs are a left and a right group element, passed through left and right linear maps, summed, rectified, and unembedded to a logit per element, with an unembedding bias. The main-text group is the symmetric group on five points (S5), with an alternating-group (A5) and a cyclic-group control discussed in appendices; the main experiment is 100 models trained from random initialisation on S5. The unit of analysis is the **neuron** as a function on the input pair, decomposed over the group's irreducible representations, and the object they end up naming is a **circuit** — a partition of neurons into a small number of independently interpretable blocks.

## How they did it
Training follows the earlier work closely: one hidden layer, a fixed iid 40% training split, cross-entropy, 25,000 epochs, Adam with weight decay 2e-4, and a single A6000. The analysis is a sequence of measurements on the weights, described step by step in an appendix as the actual path of discovery: compute each irrep's representation matrices with a group-theory package, fit each neuron's three weight slices by least squares onto the span of each irrep (calling a neuron "supported" on the irrep when one span explains essentially all of its variance), then check that each slice is approximately rank one, that the unembedding slice is a fixed scaling of the left and right slices, that the projection vector inside the rectifier is constant across the neurons of an irrep, and that the cluster means of the left and right coefficient vectors form a finite set closed under permutation by the group. Those checks produce a *description*: each circuit is a pair (an irrep, a finite set of vectors the irrep permutes), and the model's logits are approximately a sum of bi-equivariant terms, one per irrep-and-set. They then build a **verifier**: a program that takes the model weights and an encoding of the description, checks the description's own consistency conditions, constructs an "idealized" model that satisfies the description exactly, computes the idealized model's margin with one forward pass (valid because the idealized model is bi-equivariant, so its margin is constant on group orbits), and finally upper-bounds the difference between the original and idealized logits with a Lipschitz-type lemma. The accuracy bound is the fraction of inputs whose idealized margin exceeds that error bound. The source of soundness matters: the verifier is required to output a valid lower bound *whatever* description it is handed, so a misleading description can only make the bound looser, never wrong. Two competing verifiers are built the same way from the earlier explanations — one from coset concentration, one from irrep sparsity alone as a bare fact about neurons — plus the trivial verifiers: the vacuous one that returns zero and the brute-force one that runs every input and returns the exact accuracy.

## What counts as an explanation here
A formal description of the circuit (each neuron labelled by an irrep and a finite set it permutes, plus the constant projection vector), which is meant to be *partial by construction* and is graded by a number rather than by assent: translating the description into a program that certifies a lower bound on the model's accuracy. The claim "we understand this model" is thus re-expressed as "we can compress an exact brute-force computation of accuracy into a cheaper sound one that is not vacuous". The description is not complete in the sense of a full replacement — noise, float imprecision and the parts of the model the authors do not understand all stay in — but the certificate is a hard property of the actual parameters, and its tightness prices how much of the model the description actually captures. Notably, this is an explanation that *predicts* something checkable about the model's behaviour on the whole input space, which is exactly the shape we want.

## How they judged success
Two axes, printed together: computation time of the verifier (as a proxy for flops, i.e. compactness) and tightness of the accuracy lower bound. Baselines are the vacuous verifier (bound zero), the brute-force verifier (exact bound, worst time), and one verifier per earlier explanation. The evaluation is over 100 models, with a classification of each model by inspection into those the description fits and those where one of two named consistency conditions fails; the mean of the bound over the explained subset and over all models are both reported. Appendix experiments repeat the whole procedure on A5 and on a cyclic group, and attempt the same machinery on cross-entropy loss, where it fails.

## What they found
The description holds and produces non-vacuous certificates for a bit more than half the models: the bound averages to about 100% for brute force, 0% for the coset-concentration verifier, 50.4% for their own verifier over all 100 models, and 91.7% when restricted to the 55% of models whose weights satisfy the description's consistency checks. Mean elapsed time is 0.75 s against 2.20 s for brute force, roughly a factor of three, with better asymptotics. The bound is strongly bimodal — near 100% for roughly half the models and exactly 0% for the rest — and the failures are diagnosable: models either have a projection vector that varies across neurons instead of being constant (losing the bi-equivariance change of variables) or miss pairs in the double sum, the latter more common on groups whose minimal sets are large relative to group order (48% of models unexplained on the alternating group, 78% on the cyclic case reported in the same appendix figure). They also show the cyclic-group model, which cannot be using the coset algorithm because that group has no nontrivial subgroups, reproduces every directional result of the causal interventions the earlier paper used as verification: swapping embeddings destroys accuracy (99.55% → 3.85%), flipping one side's sign destroys it while flipping both preserves it, replacing the rectifier with an absolute value improves it, and perturbing activations by one particular distribution hurts more than another. On the reconciliation: coset concentration follows immediately from their description (the map is constant on cosets of a stabiliser), and projected sets are equivalent to the *conjunction* of irrep sparsity and coset concentration, but neither earlier account gives the relation between a neuron's embeddings and its unembedding, or between neurons — which is where the missing computation lives. They also explain an unexplained frequency ordering in the earlier work: which irreps models learn most often tracks the size of the smallest set the irrep permutes, not the irrep's dimension, since the standard four-dimensional irrep of S5 needs five neurons while the other four-dimensional irrep needs ten.

## Limits and open problems they name
Scope is the biggest one and they state it plainly: one architecture, one group in the main text, and only about half the models. The translation from an informal explanation to a formal bound is itself informal — their pessimisations in constructing the verifier may be unnecessarily strong, so a vacuous bound is not strong *negative* evidence against an explanation, only a failed positive test. Loss bounds do not work with either of their two adaptations. The interpretation-string construction is the fragile part of the pipeline and is deliberately excluded from the measured runtime; the clustering step has to be run several times per model and the best-scoring description kept, which is a selection effect over descriptions that the reported numbers do not price. Complex and quaternionic irreps are only sketched empirically. Causal interventions are argued to give strong negative evidence but weak positive evidence, and to carry no notion of an explanation's simplicity independent of its faithfulness.

## What we take from it
Three things, in order of value to us. (1) **A measure of an explanation that cannot be talked around.** Turning an account into a verifier that must output a valid bound regardless of the account, then reporting the tightness of that bound and the time it took against brute force, gives a two-number grade — and the soundness requirement is what makes it immune to the failure mode of a friendly evaluator, because a description that overclaims is refuted by arithmetic rather than by opinion. This is the strongest available answer to the folder's repeated finding that interpretability scores flatter the method that produced them. (2) **An intervention suite that is not diagnostic, demonstrated on a case where the answer is known.** The same four causal interventions the earlier paper treated as evidence fire in the same directions on a model that provably cannot contain the hypothesised mechanism; the operational rule is that any intervention battery must be run on at least one model where the hypothesis is known false, and a battery that fires there is a behavioural fact about the architecture and not evidence for the mechanism. (3) **A model of how to reconcile two accounts.** The pattern — take each account's stated property, formalise it, prove the *conjunction* of the two properties is equivalent to the richer object, and identify what each account left underdetermined (here: which particular weights realise the property, the bias term, and any statement relating neurons to one another) — is the cheapest way to turn disagreement into progress, and it is directly applicable to us, since our own accounts will be graded against older ones. Warning to carry: the authors' own selection over candidate descriptions, choosing the one that scores best, is precisely the kind of degree of freedom that the compact-proof framework is otherwise good at eliminating, and they do not count it in the runtime.
