# SAEs Are Good for Steering -- If You Select the Right Features

- **Key:** arxiv-2505.20063
- **Authors / organisation:** Dana Arad (Technion; work partly done during an Amazon internship), Aaron Mueller (Boston University), Yonatan Belinkov (Technion)
- **Date:** 2025-05-26 (v1), 2025-12-22 (v2)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.20063
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (abstract, SS1-SS7, limitations, acknowledgments, references, Appendices A-G with Tables 1-9; license CC BY 4.0)
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

A sparse autoencoder's features can be amplified at inference time to push a model toward a concept, and the standard way to pick which feature to amplify is to look at the text that makes it fire. The authors' complaint is that this reads a feature's job off its inputs only, while what a steering intervention needs is a feature whose amplification actually changes what the model is about to say. Their remedy is a two-part taxonomy with a cheap score on each side: features that mostly track input tokens, and features that mostly drive output tokens. The larger target is a practical selection rule, since a benchmark had just concluded that SAE steering loses to ordinary baselines and the authors want to know whether that verdict is about SAEs or about how features were chosen.

## What they studied

Gemma-2 2B and 9B with Gemma-Scope 16K SAEs, Llama-3.1-8B with Llama-Scope SAEs, and Pythia-70m. The unit of analysis is a single residual-stream SAE feature at a single layer, plus its decoder direction projected through the final layer norm and the unembedding matrix into vocabulary space (the logit lens, top 20 tokens). One hundred features are sampled per layer. The downstream experiment uses instruction-tuned Gemma-2-9B at layers 20 and 31 on AxBench's Concept500 pairs of concept and feature.

## How they did it

Two scores. The **input score** takes the sentences where a feature fires hardest (from Neuronpedia's pre-computed activations, at least 20 and at most 100 sentences), collects the maximally activated token in each, and reports the fraction of those tokens that appear in the feature's top-20 logit-lens tokens. The **output score** intervenes once on a fixed neutral prompt ("In my experience,"), adds a large steering factor of 10 to the feature, and compares the rank-weighted probability of the highest-ranked logit-lens token before and after, where the weight is one minus the token's rank over vocabulary size, times its probability; with a neutral prompt this needs a single forward pass. They then measure steering itself: 50 prompt prefixes from a fixed list of 50 neutral openers, 20 generated tokens at temperature 0.7, a swath of steering factors from 0.2 up to 20, generation success counted as occurrences of any of the feature's 20 logit-lens tokens in the produced text, and coherence as perplexity under Gemma-2-9B; the steering factor is chosen per feature as the one maximising success divided by normalised perplexity, discarding factors that push success above 3. Filtering experiments sweep an output-score threshold and compare against randomly drawn subsets of the same size, averaged over 10 draws. Finally they re-run AxBench's protocol on Gemma-2-9B-it, using five instructions per concept to pick the factor and five held out to score it, with an external LLM rating concept presence, fluency and instruction adherence on a 0-2 scale and the three combined by harmonic mean. Robustness checks: the neutral pocket prompt choice is validated on 240 features against all 50 candidate prompts, and a pointwise mutual information test (NLTK's webtext corpus) separates features whose generations contain the exact top token from those whose generations contain only collocations of it.

## What counts as an explanation here

Not a description and not a complete account. A feature's explanation is a short list of vocabulary tokens obtained by projecting its decoder direction, plus one bit of functional classification - input-tracker or output-driver - from which a prediction follows: an output feature amplified by the right factor will make those tokens appear in generated text, and an input feature will not. The claim of completeness is narrow and local: the score says whether this feature is a useful handle, not what the model is computing with it. Prediction is explicitly on the table, though, and cheap - one forward pass per feature on a neutral prompt predicts steering efficacy, and the paper's results are a test of that prediction.

## How they judged success

Generation success@20 (count of logit-lens tokens produced) against perplexity under a large reference model, with the steering factor picked on the same prefixes and reported as a ratio of the two; a randomised subset baseline at matched subset size (10 draws) to show the gain is not a size effect; a layer-conditioned version of the same sweep inside the layers that actually scored high, so that "filter by score" is not a restatement of "use later layers"; the AxBench concept/fluency/instruct scores against its published prompt, LoRA, LoReFT, ReFT-r1, difference-in-means and AUROC-selected SAE numbers; perturbation of the instrument's own free parameter (the neutral prompt); and a PMI check against a webtext corpus for the hybrid features.

## What they found

Both scores are strongly layer-dependent and largely anti-correlated. Median input scores are high and output scores near zero in roughly the first half of model depth; the pattern reverses in the later third, with input scores falling to near zero; middle layers are low on both. Llama-3.1 shows no early-layer trend at all, which the authors attribute to the logit lens being unreliable that early, and Pythia shows the output-score rise but input scores mostly at zero. Filtering by output score moves mean generation success from about 0.5-0.6 unfiltered to 1.1-1.4 at a threshold of 0.9, and a threshold of 0.01 alone drops roughly 60% of the features while adding about 0.4 points; matched random subsets give no significant improvement. Inside late layers only, the unfiltered mean is about 0.8 and the filtered mean again about 1.1-1.4, so the score is doing work beyond layer choice. On AxBench, where the published SAE number at layer 31 is 0.140, the unfiltered replication gets 0.387 and the best-thresholded version 0.470, which takes representation-based methods at that layer; at layer 20 the filtered SAE reaches 0.546 against a best method of 0.602, which the paper reports as 90.7% of the top. Scatter across the two scores shows most features hugging the axes, with a minority of hybrids high on both; hybrid features steer no better than output features and their generations contain tokens that collocate with the top logit-lens tokens rather than the top tokens themselves, which the negative average PMI on the pure output features and the higher PMI on any high-input feature is used to substantiate.

## Limits and open problems they name

Only residual-stream features, so attention and MLP sites are out and the taxonomy may not span the roles a model actually uses. Only one feature at a time, with multi-feature interference named as open. The paper says explicitly that it is not trying to explain features, only to rank them for usefulness. To this reader's additions: the AxBench comparison is not like-for-like, since the filter is itself a form of supervision - it needs the feature's logit lens and a neutral prompt but no concept labels or labelled examples, and the competing LoRA and ReFT rows are trained on positive examples while their scores come from Wu et al.'s published table rather than being re-run here; the unfiltered replication already beats the published SAE number by a factor of nearly three, which the authors put down to different sampled instructions or evaluator instability and cannot resolve because the original instruction sample was not released; success is measured as containment of tokens from the feature's own logit-lens list, which makes the metric partly co-defined with the tool being validated; the endpoint of the scoring sweeps is truncated and reported with perplexity rather than a quality score that is independent of the steered model's own family; the steering factor is selected on the same 50 prefixes that produce the reported success; and every number here is one concept suite at two layers of one instruction-tuned model plus a small-model appendix, so the 2-3x headline is a ratio on one benchmark.

## What we take from it

A cheap, general replacement for the naming step, and a warning that lands squarely on our project. The replacement: any internal unit we intend to use as a handle is scored twice before it is used - once for what activates it, once for what it does to the output distribution under a large amplification on a neutral input - and the amplification response is the score that decides usefulness, because activation overlap routinely survives where effect is absent. That is a predictive instrument for our white-box model at a cost of one forward pass per unit, and it is the shape we should adopt: predict the effect of an intervention from the unit's own weights, then check the prediction against real steered generations. The warning is the earlier one, now measured: the top logit-lens token is not the unit's meaning, it is the token the unembedding is most willing to print, so a feature whose seeds look perfect can be a token-completion reflex that degenerates into repetition when amplified, and our own label hypothesis - a unit enters as a name plus a predicted outcome under amplification at a named position - is exactly the discipline this paper's failure mode calls for. Two mechanical rules follow. First, a filtering gain must be reported beside a size-matched random-subset baseline and a layer-held-constant version, because otherwise "filter" and "use the later half of the model" are the same experiment; here both baselines exist and the gain survives, which is what makes it credible. Second, when a filter needs the same matrix the outcome metric is built from, the metric is partly circular and the honest framing is what the authors use: output score predicts steering efficacy, not correctness - containment of the feature's own tokens cannot fail for mechanical reasons and so measures the intervention's strength rather than its meaning. Worth copying as a control: they perturbed the one free parameter of their own score (which neutral prompt) across 50 alternatives and reported a correlation of 0.9557 between the original and the mean, which is the standard a stability claim should meet before the score is used to discard 60% of a dictionary.
