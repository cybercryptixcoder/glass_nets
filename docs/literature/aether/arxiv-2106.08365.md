## What they wanted, and why
The motivating intuition is the Gaussian-process one: a prediction should be untrustworthy exactly where the surrounding training data is sparse. For a kernel model that is a definition; for a ReLU network the authors want an equally explicit, sample-level quantity that says how much to trust *this* prediction, computed from a *frozen* pre-trained model rather than from an ensemble, a held-out calibration set, or a separately trained outlier detector. They frame the goal as epistemic uncertainty in general - misclassified in-distribution samples as much as out-of-distribution ones - and note they deliberately do not train an auxiliary model on the data for the detector, because what they want is the task model reporting its own gaps. The larger goal is a statement about *why* a network is right on some inputs and wrong on others, phrased as a density argument rather than a certified robustness radius.

## What they studied
Pre-trained VGG16 and ResNet50 on CIFAR10 and CIFAR100, plus a two-hidden-layer 32-unit MLP on a half-moons toy set for the illustration. The unit of analysis is the **linear activation region** of the network: each region is identified with a binary ReLU activation pattern, with the linear function (subfunction) obtained by replacing each ReLU by identity or zero according to the pattern. Inputs are individual test samples; the quantity is per-sample.

## How they did it
They start from the textbook per-region empirical-error bound: a Hoeffding bound on the error of subfunction *i* whose width scales as the inverse square root of the number of training samples in that region (Eq. 4). That bound has two failures the authors state outright - subfunctions are not independent because they share parameters with their neighbours, and in a deep network most test samples land in regions with *no* training samples, where the bound is infinite and useless for ranking. So they smooth: define a proximity weight k between subfunctions (Gaussian in Hamming distance between activation patterns), turn it into a per-subfunction probability mass ℙ(h_i) by summing neighbour counts weighted by k and normalising (Eq. 5-6), rewrite the network's empirical risk as an expectation over weighted subfunction risks (Eq. 7-11), and re-bound (Theorem 2.1, Eq. 12) to get a per-sample score of smooth weighted error plus (1/ℙ(h_i))·sqrt(log(2/δ)/2N). Since the bound holds for any k, k is chosen on data: they derive a proxy objective (Eq. 13-16) showing that k must decay with representation distance but not decay to zero, because the 1/ℙ term explodes, then in practice pick δ and ρ on a validation set by the same metric the paper reports. Their operational shortcut for deep nets is to binarise **only the last ReLU layer's** activations and use the **full bit space** {0,1}^M rather than the feasible set, which keeps regions disjoint and the bound valid for piecewise-linear subfunctions; this makes the sum over all subfunctions exponential, which Appendix B reduces from O(N·2^M) to O(M) and O(N²·2^M) to O(N²+M³) using a completeness argument on Hamming shells and a binomial look-up table. Evaluation is post-hoc ranking of test samples: the score, log-transformed to tame its scale, is thresholded at 1,000 uniformly spaced values and scored as AUROC and AUCEA (area under coverage-vs-effective-accuracy) against ground truth of OOD-vs-in (CIFAR10→CIFAR100, CIFAR10→SVHN) or correct-vs-incorrect on in-distribution test.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **per-sample predictive bound derived from the network's own partition**: one number per input, equal to a smoothed, density-weighted error bound for the linear subfunction that input induces, with a closed-form decomposition into a weighted error term and a 1/ℙ(h_i) density term. It is an attribution-free explanation - nothing is said about which neurons or features cause the error - and it is partial by construction: it is a bound, it is acknowledged loose because the bounding procedure assumes a worst case in order to free k for search, and smoothing introduces a bias that the authors concede does not vanish in the sample limit and argue is reducible by searching k. It predicts something concrete: a ranking of samples by expected error, which is exactly what the experiments test.

## How they judged success
AUROC and AUCEA of the score as an accept/reject classifier over 1,000 thresholds, averaged over 5 seeds, reported per architecture × dataset and as a *difference from the best competing method* in each setting (Table 1). Eight baselines: residual-flow density, a GP with fixed features, nearest-class distance (Lee et al.), softmax margin, entropy, max response, MC dropout, and feature-cluster distance. Crucially, **all hyperparameters including δ and ρ are selected on an in-distribution misclassification validation split**, so the OOD test sets are genuinely unseen (grids in Table 7: δ ∈ 11 values, ρ ∈ 9 values rescaled per architecture). Two supplementary checks: a per-class ranking of CIFAR100 classes by median unreliability, compared against a hand-coloured superclass familiarity label by Spearman correlation (Table 8), and a qualitative confusion matrix (Fig. 4).

## What they found
The subfunction bound is the best method on average and within noise of the best per category: its average deficit from the top method in each setting is −0.007 AUROC (Table 1), against −0.014 for entropy, −0.021 for max response, −0.022 for margin and −0.181 for the GP with fixed features. In absolute AUROC (Table 2) it ranges 0.858-0.862 on CIFAR10→CIFAR100 and 0.886-0.915 on CIFAR10→SVHN, beating cluster distance (0.862/0.867 and 0.870/0.892) and entropy (0.853/0.822 and 0.869/0.858): on the harder, semantically closer OOD split it is the top method by a visible margin, on the easy SVHN split it is within 0.002-0.005 of a cluster-distance baseline. For in-distribution misclassification (Table 3) it is 0.988 AUCEA on VGG16-CIFAR10 against 0.989 for entropy and 0.907 AUROC against 0.914; on CIFAR100 models (Table 6) the picture is mixed - best AUCEA on VGG16 (0.904) but only 0.827 AUROC on ResNet50 against 0.859 for entropy, so the ranking advantage is architecture-dependent and the paper's per-category summary hides a cell where it loses by 0.03. Filtering by it would raise effective accuracy from 70-73% to 90-92% on CIFAR100 and from 91-92% to 98-99% on CIFAR10, averaged over thresholds. On the CIFAR100 *models* (Table 5) the absolute AUROCs are lower - 0.738-0.750 vs CIFAR10 and 0.797-0.807 vs SVHN - with the margin over entropy narrowing to about 0.01 and cluster distance beating it on one cell (0.754 vs 0.750 across CIFAR10, ResNet50). Its one qualitative uniqueness claim - that only its ranking tracks semantic familiarity, Spearman 0.511 against 0.433 for MC dropout and 0.390 for the GP (Table 8) - is also the weakest quantitative claim in the paper. The abstract's headline "up to 20% in absolute terms" is the CIFAR100 effective-accuracy gain; the CIFAR10 gain it rests beside is roughly 7 points.

## Limits and open problems they name
Three are named in §4 as reasons the reported numbers are a **lower bound**: last-layer-only binarisation means the subfunctions are piecewise linear rather than linear; the infeasible bit patterns are included, so density mass is assigned to regions that no input reaches; and the partition itself is infeasible to enumerate exactly (they cite the open problem). Smoothing bias is admitted and argued away rather than measured. The Conclusion lists what they want next: a density of *continuous* input space rather than of discrete partitions (which they say needs a different bound - the method is specialised to piecewise-linear nets), tighter bounds via stronger assumptions on k, and the model-selection question of how to train nets so that test inputs land in high-density regions. A pass through the baselines also documents a failure they could not fix: the transformation-cluster baseline suffered feature collapse without explicit disentanglement training.

## What we take from it
Four concrete things. (1) **The cleanest partition statistic in the folder, and it is a predictive bound rather than a count**: binarise one layer, weight subfunctions by Gaussian decay in Hamming distance of their patterns, normalise to a probability mass, and the reciprocal of that mass is the term that makes a per-sample error bound finite for regions holding zero training points. That reciprocal is the "how far is this input from populated territory" number the arc's failing-boundary and random-probe controls have each been approximating by hand. (2) **A cheap complexity trick worth reusing**: because the full bit space is complete, the sum of the kernel over all patterns at a fixed Hamming distance is the same for every pattern, so two exponential sums become O(M) with binomial lookups - the same Hamming-shell counting the walk-based folding literature needs. (3) **A methodological warning about the paper's own headline**: the score beats the best baseline by 0.007 AUROC averaged across four settings and its win over cluster distance on the easy SVHN split is within the seed spread, so "competitive" is the accurate word, and the 20% abstract figure is a single-dataset best case that is not stated as such. (4) **A design commitment for us**: the success criterion is a *ranking of held-out samples* scored against a binary behaviour, selected-on-validation and untuned on test - which is precisely the shape of test run 723-729 kept naming as missing, and it is applied here to a geometric quantity, not to a probe. The caveat to carry: the binding quantity is density in *activation-pattern* space at one layer, i.e. a discrete partition, and the authors themselves say extending it to continuous representation density requires a different bound - so any transfer we make to a continuous feature space is a new derivation, not a reuse.
