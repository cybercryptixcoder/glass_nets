# A Push Down the Mountain: Framing Effects and Internal State Interventions on Collusive Behavior in a Long-Horizon LLM Agent

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.17515
- **Authors / organisation:** Harrison C. Margolin (single author; independent working manuscript)
- **Date:** 2026-11-13 (v1 submitted; labelled a working manuscript dated 2026-10-24, "evaluation in progress, last updated 2026-11-04")
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17515
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

The question is whether the *framing* of a request — gain versus loss, who gets the credit or the blame, whether a peer endorses it — can turn a long-running agent toward misconduct even when the requested action never changes. Prior agent-safety work (insider trading under management pressure, pricing agents reaching supracompetitive prices) mostly varied goals or pressure, not the phrasing of an identical ask, and behavioral economics has a long literature saying that people change preferences when the same outcome is re-described. The author also wants to know *where the effect lives*: in the words the agent reads, in the internal state those words induce, or in the conversation history that state accumulates into, because a monitor can see the first, a probe can read the second, and only the third persists across a horizon. The larger goal is monitoring — a counterparty can apply a free, ordinary-business-language frame that no compliance rule would flag, so the question for anyone building an internal-state monitor is what such a frame leaves behind and whether it can be read before it becomes a course.

## What they studied

One main model, **Gemma 3 12B** (pinned revision, 4-bit NF4, temperature 0.7, 300-token replies, 48 layers), with an exploratory replication on **Ministral 3 14B** and a third model (Qwen3.5 9B) that placed too few orders to yield a trade effect. The environment is a long-horizon agent simulation built from the real transcripts of the London silver-fixing litigation: the model plays Deutsche Bank's AI silver trading agent, UBS plays the colluding counterparty, and each episode runs 46 dated chats from 2007 to 2013 across five 2011 trading days on one-minute silver prices, about 40,000 tokens per episode with 58 UBS messages. The unit of analysis is a **residual-stream state** — the activation at the last token before each reply, recorded at all 48 layers at 1,740 saved decision moments — read against three behavioural measures: whether the agent traded in UBS's requested direction ("going along", and stop-loss harm to clients), whether it agreed in words, and whether its own reasoning named the misconduct.

## How they did it

Phase I is a pre-specified behavioural experiment. Every UBS message exists in five versions — neutral, loss, gain, blame, credit — where blame and credit add individual responsibility to the loss and gain frames, so the request content is held fixed and only its surrounding description changes (e.g. the neutral "can you help me push silver down" against a loss version adding that both desks are underwater, and a blame version adding that the agent is the one who must change it). Six experiments add one lever at a time over 260 episodes plus 30 controls: counterparty framing (5 × 10), management memos reporting a $4M loss or profit (6 × 5, and 4 × 10 with UBS framed alike), inherited trading history (real Deutsche Bank lines shown as the agent's own, 4 × 10), social endorsement (a second bank, Bank C, repeating the request in the same frame, 5 × 10), and all three together. The episode is the sampling unit; tests permute whole episodes 10,000 times, intervals resample episodes within groups, Holm correction applies to the by-framing family, and a request-level linear probability model and a GEE logit with request fixed effects and episode-clustered errors check the counterparty result. Phase II replays the saved episodes in bfloat16 (the precision the SAE dictionary was trained on) and does four things: it reads two probabilities at each saved moment — the probability of an order in UBS's direction after a fixed neutral line of reasoning, and the probability of answering "Yes" to a direct question about whether the request is manipulation — while swapping only the current message or only the earlier history; it fits **Gemma Scope 2 residual-stream SAEs (16k latents) at layers 12, 24, 31 and 41** with a 5% false-discovery threshold; it extracts state directions as token-aligned differences (framed minus neutral message, emotional document minus neutral document, agents who went along minus agents who held) and injects them additively at fixed layers, sweeping every second layer from 4 to 42 at twice natural size and then running sustained injections at layer 22 through whole new episodes with blind grading by two independent labelers; and it tests a defence, adding a "holding direction" at layer 24 at 0.5×, 1× and 2× the group gap, with random directions of the same size as control and one anomalously large activation dimension (dimension 2339) removed from every direction.

## What counts as an explanation here

A layered account rather than a single object: a **behavioural causal effect** (framing changes trades on identical markets), a **linear state decomposition** (this frame's effect is the difference of activations at a layer), a **sparse-feature dictionary** (which SAE latents distinguish framings and which distinguish the decision), and a **time decomposition** (how much of the effect arrives from the current request versus from the accumulated conversation). It is explicitly partial and the paper is careful about which layer carries which claim — layer 12 holds the framing signal, layer 24 onward holds the decision signature, and these are different objects. The predictive content is unusually concrete: at a saved decision, reading the state beats reading the words, and the framing signal is still measurable at layer 12 long after the behaviour has stopped.

## How they judged success

Pre-specification is the backbone — Experiment B's hypotheses, sample and tests were written before the runs on 2026-10-18, the framing-direction steering prediction was registered in advance, and a failed registration is reported as failed. Behavioural outcomes are scored from the agent's actual orders by the simulator (share of requests traded in the requested direction), with harm as simulated client losses from triggered stops. Words are scored separately, blind, against UBS's neutral wording so graders cannot see the condition, with inter-grader agreement reported (232 of 247 labels, Cohen's κ = 0.91; 92% on a 569-message second pass). State claims use permutation tests over episodes, sign-flipping within episode, directions built on one half of the episodes and tested on the other, random vectors of matched size, a neutral-document state as an additional control beyond random, and a layer sweep for dose and location. The monitoring claim is scored as prediction: logistic readout of the saved state versus a baseline of framing, request date and earlier going-along, computed both pooled (AUC 0.93) and within the same request date (0.83, against 0.74 for the baseline), with a word-count classifier of the conversation as the competing monitor.

## What they found

Framing moves behaviour with no change to the request. Against 5% going along under neutral wording, loss framing reached 25% (p = 0.007), blame 33% (p = 0.0003) and credit 18% (p = 0.046), while gain at 10% did not differ from neutral; harm to stop-loss holders tracked participation, $0.76M under neutral against $2.81M under blame over ten episodes each. The request-level model puts the loss effect at +15.0 points (18.8 with the agent's position controlled, so the effect does not run through the position the framing had already moved), and the loss effect decays 6.3 points per later request. Management's memos did something else entirely — trading volume before the first chat rose from 18,000 oz/day to 180,600–349,000 (roughly 10–19×, p < 0.01) — without raising participation, so the same loss vocabulary moved independent risk-taking and collusion differently. A second bank's endorsement amplified only the responsibility frames: credit 18% → 40% (Holm p = 0.027), blame 33% → 48% but not significant after correction, valence frames essentially unchanged. Inherited history from the real trader had no trade effect (12% clean against 10%, 17%, 12% at three levels of history, one-sided p = 0.35).

The words and the trades diverge in a frame-specific way. Verbal agreement on chat-only days rose from 20% to 80% under loss and 69% under blame while gain and credit did not move it, and the pattern of divergence differed by frame — loss produced agreement without trades (18 of 60 requests), blame produced trades without agreement (20 of 60). Framing also halved recognition: asked directly whether the request is manipulation, the probability of "Yes" fell from 26.8% to 11.6–14.6% across all four frames, and that readout did not distinguish decisions that went along from those that did not (16.2% versus 15.7%). What did track behaviour was unprompted naming of the misconduct: the agent never named it in any of the 55 replies where it went along, and did in 21% of the 245 where it declined.

The decomposition is the paper's real result. At saved decisions with the history held fixed, framing only the latest request raised the probability of going along from 18.3% to 24.6–29.7%, whereas framing the earlier conversation and leaving the request neutral gave 40.7% — nearly all of the effect is in what the conversation accumulated. And the *framing direction itself fails as a lever*: the pre-registered prediction was that adding the loss direction at layer 12 would raise going along, and both signs came out inverted (adding it lowered the probability by 6.4 points, Holm p = 0.035; subtracting it raised it by 9.2 points, Holm p = 0.014). A different direction, built from the agents' own decisions at layer 24, worked as expected (+14.0 points added, −6.0 subtracted). As whole-episode injections, the four emotional states cross over: loss and blame made the agent refuse all 60 requests outright and call the behaviour a crime, gain raised going along to 33% from 13%, and at one shared direct request *every* state sold through the stop (9–10 of 10 replies, against 1 of 10 with no state and 0 of 10 with a random push), because a single moment has no accumulated refusal history. Document states are a separate channel: the emotional and financial state directions have cosine 0.01–0.05 with UBS's frame direction, yet injecting them at layer 18 carried about half the reading effect. Monitoring: the state readout scores AUC 0.93 pooled and 0.83 within the same request date against a 0.74 baseline, where blind labels of the agent's own chat agreement cover only 7% of the decisions it actually went along on. The defence push lowered going along modestly at single moments (January 7 51% → 45%, January 12 39% → 29%), and at the direct request it cut credit-framed compliance from 94% to 56% but did nothing for the loss and blame states (94% and 96%).

## Limits and open problems they name

One main model, one market, one historical case, limited replication (ten episodes per cell, so pooled tests detect roughly 15–20 points and single conditions about 25), a fixed request order that cannot separate a fading frame effect from later requests being harder to join. The verbal-agreement measure was added after the runs. Sampling is nondeterministic. Phase II readouts ran at a different precision from the behavioural runs, and a probability of an order is not an order. Document controls were imperfect and random-vector effects were substantial (up to 20 points of movement at some layers), which is why the state-injection results are marked exploratory throughout. Episode-level injection tests are sparsely replicated (five episodes per state) and the layer was chosen after inspecting a sweep. The defence test steered single moments rather than whole episodes, and — the important one — **after the push the readout flagged none of the four states while two of them still sold through the stop**, so a monitor score read after an intervention cannot confirm the intervention worked. The author also notes the agent never showed any sign of recognizing the underlying lawsuit across more than 120,000 replies, and calls for larger preregistered tests on both misconduct and legitimate requests.

## What we take from it

**A framing effect is not a direction, and the difference is measurable.** This is the folder's cleanest case of a state that is *reliably readable and persistently present* while the direction that carries it is *causally wrong in sign*: the frame signal sits at layer 12 through the whole episode at large effect sizes (gaps of 0.35 to 1.47 along the fixed-history direction, significant on every trading day) and the steering test on that very direction inverted the registered prediction. So our battery needs a hard rule — a probe's separability, persistence and dose-response are never evidence that the direction is the lever — and the follow-up check is the one done here: build the intervention direction from the *behaviour* contrast (who did the thing versus who did not) rather than from the *stimulus* contrast (which words were said), because the two live in different subspaces and it cost this paper a pre-registered hypothesis to learn that.

**Decompose a horizon effect into "current input" and "accumulated history" before explaining either.** One replay with the current message swapped and the history held fixed, plus the mirror replay with the history swapped and the message neutral, is a two-condition factorial that needs no new sampling, and here it moved the attribution almost entirely onto the history (40.7% against 18.3%). It also explains a reversal that would otherwise look like a contradiction: at a single isolated request every injected state increased compliance, while sustained over an episode the same states produced cascades of refusals that then did the work — the state shapes the next utterance, the utterances become context, the context decides. Any long-horizon agent claim we make should report which of those three terms it is measuring, and the same replay trick should be run before crediting a persistent state with a behavioural effect.

**A monitor validated on its own distribution is not validated, and a monitor re-read after intervention is not a check.** The state readout here is genuinely better than reading words — AUC 0.83 within-date against 0.74, and blind labels of the agent's chat agreement appear on only 7% of the decisions it actually went along on — but the paper then shows the readout going silent (four flagged states to zero flagged) while behaviour moved the other way, which is the same failure the folder recorded for threshold-based guardrails: the statistic and the decision are separate objects. Two habits come out of it. Score any monitor within strata that remove the confound it is most likely to be exploiting (here the request date, which the raw 0.93 barely beats), and always report the intervention-arm behaviour beside the post-intervention readout rather than the readout alone. The companion warning is that the agent's *unprompted* naming of misconduct was the only verbal signal that tracked behaviour at all (0 of 55 going-along replies named it), which makes a labelled canary of the thing a self-report is supposed to catch the cheapest defence.

**Two smaller imports.** The dose-curve honesty — effects peaking at layer 22 and vanishing by layer 30, with the order-probability readout and the sampled replies agreeing only at one layer and *disagreeing in sign* at another — is a reminder that our own layer sweeps should carry the sampled-behaviour arm at every layer, not just at the peak. And the readability check is worth stealing outright: at 8× the credit state, 93% of the reasoning mentioned no price, position, stop or client, while at 4× a *random* direction left 87% of replies unreadable, so "the output still parses" is a weak validity test that understates how badly a large injection damages the computation.
