# Distilled Circuits: A Mechanistic Study of Internal Restructuring in Knowledge Distillation

- **Key:** arxiv-2505.10822
- **Authors / organisation:** Reilly Haskins, Benjamin Adams; Department of Computer Science and Software Engineering, University of Canterbury
- **Date:** 2026-03-09 (arXiv v2; v1 2025-05)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.10822
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

Distillation is chosen by deployment cost, and the usual evidence that it worked is output behaviour: a student matches the teacher's validation perplexity, so the compression is called safe. The authors' premise is that matching outputs says nothing about whether the student computes the same thing, and that the difference matters wherever the teacher's mechanism is the reason we trust the behaviour — their framing is that a student can inherit the answer and lose the algorithm. Their larger aim is a *quantitative* handle on internal agreement between two models, because the honest comparison (recover both circuits and diff them by hand) is a case study that will not scale. So the paper has two halves that we care about for different reasons: a circuit-level anatomy of what a distilled student keeps, fuses, and throws away; and a cheap score for "does the student's task-relevant computation resemble the teacher's" that could be computed for many candidate students.

## What they studied

Primary pair: GPT-2 (12 layers, 124M) as teacher and DistilGPT-2 (6 layers, 82M) as student; replications with BERT (109M) versus DistilBERT (66M) and with Llama-3.1-8B versus Llama-3.1-Minitron-4B-Depth-Base (32 layers cut to 16). Unit of analysis is the **component** — attention heads and MLPs — grouped into a task circuit by ablation, plus per-head query–key and output–value subcircuits, plus MLP activation geometry via PCA. Tasks: numeral and word sequence completion (Lan et al.), indirect object identification, and SimpleQA question answering. Inputs are small task sets (100 examples in the case study, 384 for the wider comparisons, 500 IOI, 200 SimpleQA), run in TransformerLens on a single A100/L4-class GPU.

## How they did it

Circuits are found by iterative pruning with a stated threshold: ablate each head and MLP (backwards then forwards), keep components whose removal drops logit difference by at least 20%, then keep edges by path patching under the same rule. All three textbook properties are then reported for the extracted subgraph rather than assumed — keeping only the circuit costs 18.99% of the teacher's logit difference and 19.88% of the student's (completeness), ablating the circuit inside an otherwise intact model drives the logit difference from 6.12 to −0.28 and 3.75 to −0.31 (faithfulness), and minimality is inherited from the pruning rule. Roles are assigned by reading query–key matrices, and then *validated twice*: activation patching at component × token granularity to show causal sufficiency, and layer-wise linear probes on the residual stream to show where the relevant variable becomes linearly decodable. Ablation drops are reported as a percentage of the unablated logit difference and, in the cross-pair table, as mean drops with bootstrap intervals over 384 examples. The alignment metric is then built in three steps: normalise each component's ablation effect by the largest effect in its own model to get an influence in [0,1]; match teacher to student components by representational similarity (mean-activation cosine for heads, mean absolute cosine over the top three covariance eigenvectors for MLPs), allowing several teachers to map to one student; and average, over matched pairs, similarity multiplied by one minus the influence disagreement. It is validated by injecting Gaussian noise into the student's activations across a standard-deviation ladder and checking that the score falls monotonically (they report an inverse-logarithmic relationship, with a plateau near 0.2), and by sweeping normalisation and matching choices (max, L1, L2; greedy, Hungarian, soft top-k) over the three pairs.

## What counts as an explanation here

A mechanism at component granularity: a named subgraph (circuit) with roles attached to heads and MLPs — sequence member detection, numeral detection, numeral movement, successor computation, duplicate-token and name-mover and induction heads — plus a map of how those roles changed between teacher and student. It is deliberately partial and text-and-diagram level: heads get a functional label from their attention pattern, backed by patching and probing, not a formal program. The second artifact is a scalar per task: an alignment score between two models' task-relevant computations, meant to predict which student preserves the teacher's algorithm. Neither claims to be complete; the paper says explicitly that the specific role assignments are task- and model-specific case studies.

## How they judged success

For the anatomy: circuit completeness, faithfulness, and minimality; ablation-induced performance change; QK-matrix inspection; PCA/cosine of MLP activations; head-specific successor and copy scores obtained by projecting output–value contributions into vocabulary space; linear-probe layer curves; permutation controls and balanced sampling in the probes. For the metric: monotone response to injected noise, ranking consistency across eight design variants (all preserve Llama > GPT > BERT), and a cross-task table of alignment against student–teacher logit-difference gaps, with an unrelated architectural pair (GPT-2 versus DistilBERT) as a low-anchor. All six models are the same two families, and there is no shuffled-matching null, no random-init student, and no comparison against the global similarity baselines (CKA, SVCCA) they argue against — that contrast is asserted from the numbers, not measured.

## What they found

The student's circuit is not a small copy of the teacher's; it is a smaller circuit with different bookkeeping. Several role heads survive at a lower layer with the same function but far greater load: ablating the numeral-detection head costs the teacher 33.18% and the student 87.73%; the numeral mover 41.64% versus 72.83%; the successor head 34.94% versus 77.57%. Two of the teacher's MLPs (layers 9 and 10) appear merged into one student MLP (layer 4). Teacher functionality that was absent in the student includes a repeated-token detector in layer 1, and an MLP in the teacher that actively pushed the output toward the last given element rather than the next one (ablating it improved the teacher), which has no counterpart in the student. One teacher–student MLP pair diverges sharply *on the first token only* (cosine −0.302) while agreeing elsewhere (0.721). Loss of redundancy is the recurring theme and is measured directly: mean ablation-induced drop is 3.06pp for GPT-2 against 12.24pp for DistilGPT-2, 6.26pp against 16.89pp for the BERT pair, and 0.84pp against 2.20pp for the Llama pair — a gap that shrinks as the student grows larger relative to its teacher (compression 33.9%, 39.4%, 50%). Alignment is high and task-ordered: 0.945 numeral / 0.940 word / 0.918 IOI for the GPT pair, 0.887 / 0.841 / 0.880 for the BERT pair, 0.978 numeral and 0.981 SimpleQA for the Llama pair, and 0.765 for the mismatched GPT-2 → DistilBERT pairing. Performance gaps and alignment scores dissociate: the BERT pair has the smaller logit-difference gap (0.88) and the lower alignment (0.89), while the GPT pair has the larger gap (2.20) and higher alignment (0.95). The second case study is just as blunt about outputs versus mechanisms: on word-sequence completion the two GPT models' mean logit differences are 3.81 and −1.64, wildly apart, while their alignment score stays at 0.940; on IOI the same pair sits at 3.26 and −0.29 (a student that cannot do the task at all) yet still scores 0.918 on alignment. That last cell is the sharpest caution in the paper: a high alignment number can coexist with a student that has the wrong answer, because alignment compares influence *profiles*, not competence.

## Limits and open problems they name

Six models, three families, four tasks, mostly under 4B parameters, and the deep case study is a single pair on a synthetic numeral task; the authors say the full circuit diff is out of scope for the 8B/4B pair, so the larger-model result is only the metric plus ablation robustness. IOI is the weakest arm: a student that cannot do the task cannot show a preserved circuit, so "collapse" here is not measured against a competent student. The metric's own components are admitted to be coarse — mean activations and three eigenvectors — and the alignment score embeds influence agreement, so by construction it is high when the students' ablation profiles are similar, which is why the Llama pair scores highest and also has the smallest robustness gap; the two observations are not independent. They also flag that component roles were assigned by hand, and that automating that step is the obvious next move.

## What we take from it

Three things, one of them a warning about our own kind of instrument. (1) *A robustness measure that is really a redundancy measure.* Their headline observable — mean ablation drop per component — is a direct read-out of how many components share each role, and it separates teacher from student more cleanly than any output metric here. For our predictive white-box model this is the quantity to carry: an intervention-based estimate of role redundancy per mechanism, because a mechanism implemented once is exactly the mechanism whose removal our predictions will get wrong under a perturbation nobody trained for. (2) *Match before you compare.* The alignment metric's matching step is the part that could be wrong, and the paper shows the failure mode: a student head with high representational similarity to a teacher name-mover (cosine up to 0.52) that does not reproduce the function. Any cross-model similarity we compute — crosscoders, probes, feature dictionaries — needs its own function check at matched pairs, not just a similarity score. (3) *The dissociations are the reportable result.* Alignment and performance gap disagree in both directions across their pairs, and the paper's own data says a student can be output-equivalent and mechanism-different; for us that is the argument for never accepting an aggregate agreement number as evidence about internal structure, and the concrete form of the claim to imitate is the table with both columns and the mismatched pair at the bottom.
