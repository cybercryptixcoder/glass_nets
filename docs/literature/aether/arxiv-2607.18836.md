# Partial pooling predicts cross-validation reliability: a closed-form triage and Rao–Blackwellised cure for hierarchical LOO

- **Key:** arxiv-2607.18836
- **Authors / organisation:** Aidan D. Bindoff — Wicking Dementia Research & Education Centre, University of Tasmania (Hobart, Australia); ORCID 0000-0002-0943-2702
- **Date:** 2026-07-21 (v1)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.18836
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Cross-validation is the standard way to ask whether a fitted model predicts held-out behaviour, and the cheap version of it — importance-sampling leave-one-out, where a single fit is reweighted once per held-out unit — comes with a per-unit reliability diagnostic. In hierarchical models that diagnostic fires constantly, and not at random: it fires on the units whose group is small, which is exactly the population a health or behavioural study cares about. The cheap repair on offer is either an exact refit per flagged unit (what the shortcut was built to avoid) or a proposal-repair trick applied per unit. The authors want three things: to predict *which* units will fail before or alongside running the estimator, to replace the reweighting with a construction that cannot fail that way, and to explain geometrically why one part of the influence causes the failure while another part limits the cure. The larger goal — explicitly stated in the limitations — is to know when a cross-validation number can be trusted at all, since a silently over-optimistic number does not announce itself.

## What they studied
A single grouping factor with a scalar random-effect block: Gaussian linear mixed models (where the per-observation information is constant, so the predictor collapses to group size), and Bernoulli, binomial and Poisson generalised linear mixed models with a random intercept. Evidence is three replicated simulation grids plus one public real dataset of overdispersed seizure counts (counts per patient per visit, and a second specification in which each observation is its own group), plus a model-comparison experiment between a latent-per-observation Poisson model and a negative-binomial model. The unit of analysis is the individual held-out observation (a case-deletion fold), and the failure object is the shape parameter fitted to that fold's importance weights, thresholded at the conventional value.

## How they did it
They begin from the fraction of a group's posterior precision that the prior supplies — the classic pooling factor — and from a per-observation leverage that shares any group's leverage budget additively with its group-mates. They then partition the score of each observation under the joint Fisher information of base parameters (population effects, variance components) and group coordinates, take the Schur complement of the group block, and show algebraically that the observation's total influence splits into a group-only vertical term and a lifted horizontal term. The vertical sum over a group equals one minus the pooling factor, which makes the pooling factor itself a predictor of the reweighting failure. For the cure they marginalise the group coordinate out of the leave-one-out predictive and importance-sample only over the base parameters: an analytic Gaussian downdate for linear models, a one-dimensional deterministic quadrature over the group prior for the three scalar response families, all inside the base-only reweighting. They validate against exact refits (brute-force conjugate refits for the linear case, refits-with-resampling for the generalised case) and against the recommended proposal-repair comparator (moment matching) on exactly the flagged folds, and they stress their own estimator with a few-groups design in which the base is weakly identified so that the residual reweighting must break. Every simulation is replicated and reported with Monte Carlo standard error; per-replicate rank statistics are given as primary because folds within one fit are not independent.

## What counts as an explanation here
A two-level decomposition plus a closed-form predictor: a design-time (linear case) or post-fit-but-weight-free (generalised case) map from model structure and group sizes to which folds will be unreliable, then a second predictor for where the cure itself is strained. The explanation is geometric rather than descriptive — the two halves of a single influence identity are claimed to govern, respectively, where the reweighting fails and where the marginalised cure fails — and it is predictive in the measurable sense: the flags are graded against observed failure and against observed estimator error. It is deliberately partial: the paper says outright that the vertical term is prior art and that it claims only the cross-validation application, the packaged closed forms, the head-to-head comparison and the geometry.

## How they judged success
Pooled AUC over folds (with an explicit admission that pooled AUCs are optimistic about effective sample size) and per-replicate Spearman with Monte Carlo standard error; number of flagged folds cured and number left above threshold; elpd root-mean-square error against an exact refit on the high-shape folds, both per-replicate and pooled; one-step influence's correlation against the realised leave-one-out shift of the variance component; a sensitivity sweep over the number of posterior draws; a Kolmogorov–Smirnov calibration check at the boundary of the linearised integrand; and, for the decision experiment, a reported elpd difference with its standard error at three estimators, plus a fold-by-fold adjudication against exact refits stratified across the diagnostic range. Counts of optimisations and wall-clock are reported for the cost comparison.

## What they found
The predictor separates failing folds at AUC 0.96 in the linear simulation and 0.81 in the replicated logistic simulation, with per-replicate Spearman 0.75 and 0.61; on the observation-level count model with 97 failing folds out of 236 it reaches Spearman 0.60 and AUC 0.80. The marginalised estimator cures every flagged fold on the simulation grids (RMSE against exact refit 0.096 versus 0.232, and 0.015 versus 0.060) and, on the count stressor, brings mean shape from 0.68 to −0.01 and maximum from 1.34 to 0.35, with RMSE 0.041 against 0.638 for raw reweighting and 0.658 for moment matching; moment matching leaves 37 of the 97 folds above threshold and here is no better than the incumbent. In the head-to-head, moment matching only moves pooled RMSE from 0.140 to 0.131 while the marginalised estimate reaches 0.044, and it needs one optimisation per fold where theirs needs none (97 optimisations and 8 minutes versus 97 exact refits and 82 minutes for the same answer). The decision experiment is the sharpest result: the reweighted comparison reports an elpd difference of +39.3 (±8.0) for the more complex latent model, refitting at threshold only brings it to +11.0 (±3.2), and the marginalised estimate gives +3.1 (±3.1) — indistinguishable — which 40 exact refits confirm, and which is also why the recommended refit-at-threshold remedy is itself optimistic on its untriggered folds (the larger model's estimate is biased even below the threshold, at +0.092 on the 0.55–0.7 band, while the cheaper model's estimate is already exact). Their own limit is measured rather than asserted: in the few-groups design their estimator is off by 0.532 on flagged folds against 0.068 on unflagged, its residual diagnostic separates the divergences at AUC 0.93, and applying the cure selectively gives RMSE 0.067 while refitting only three percent of folds.

## Limits and open problems they name
Marginalisation is prior art and the paper says so in its own section; the design-time claim holds exactly only in the Gaussian case, and on real data with constant group size the predictor's discrimination is driven by fitted means rather than group size, which brings it closer to the folklore that influential points have high diagnostics. Agreement with an exact refit on singleton folds is estimand-shared — both compute the same integral — so it cannot falsify their estimator there; the independent falsification is the few-groups design. Pooled figures over-count (2.5× per-replicate against 3.0× pooled). Crossed or multiple grouping factors are declared out of scope and fall back to plain reweighting rather than degrading silently; non-linear blocks are approximated and the degradation is measured but real (calibration breaks at extreme change-point sharpness). The moment-matching comparator was run at default settings without sweeping its tuning, and the mixture-proposal alternative is discussed but not benchmarked.

## What we take from it
Three reusable things. (1) **A failure predictor built from structure, not from the estimator's own output.** The pooling factor is computable from the ratio of prior precision to total precision with no weights and no deletion, and it predicts which units the reweighting will break on — for us the analogue is predicting which probe/queries a cheap estimand will fail on from the account's own precision structure, before running the cheap estimator. (2) **Cure by marginalising the offending coordinate, not by repairing the proposal.** Their diagnosis of why the proposal-repair comparator plateaus is precise: an affine transformation of the draws cannot represent a data-driven coordinate reverting to its prior under deletion, whereas integrating the coordinate out does. The mechanical rule for us is to identify which coordinate in a cheap estimator is the heavy-tailed direction and integrate it out analytically rather than adapt around it, and to prove the variance reduction against the *same estimand* so the claim is not vacuous. (3) **A two-level triage with a published speed/accuracy curve**: first flag the units the cheap estimator will fail on, cure those, then flag the units where the cure itself is strained and pay for those. Their 0.067-RMSE-at-3%-refit row is the shape of every "near-exact at a fraction of the cost" claim we make, and the honest corollary they state — that an estimator's own residual diagnostic tracks its own error, which is why the falsification has to come from an oracle experiment — is a warning to adopt in every triage we build.
