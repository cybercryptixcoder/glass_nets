- **Relevance to us:** 3 (directly useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Every practical steering method faces the same trade: push the model toward a target behaviour and watch unrelated benchmarks sag. The authors argue that the usual fixes attack the symptom — monitor downstream accuracy, back off the coefficient — while the cause is geometric. Benchmarks move because the edit is not orthogonal to anything else that matters, and every standard operator treats all non-target directions as equally expensive to disturb. Their aim is to make that cost explicit, then minimise it subject to a fixed amount of steering. The framing is explicitly ours-to-borrow: they want steering described as a constrained optimisation with a stated objective and a stated budget, so an intervention's side effects are something you chose rather than something you discover afterwards.

## What they studied
Residual-stream activations in four instruction-tuned models spanning two families and 3B–14B: Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct and Gemma-2-9B-IT. Two intervention sites per transformer layer (after attention-norm and after MLP-norm), so 2L points per model, and a separate steering direction fitted at each site by a difference of unit-normalised class means — 416 harmful AdvBench prompts against 512 sampled Alpaca prompts. The behaviour being steered is refusal, measured by HarmBench attack success, and the cost being protected is accuracy on six one-hundred-example tinyBenchmarks tasks (ARC, MMLU, Winogrande, GSM8K, TruthfulQA, HellaSwag).

## How they did it
The quantity of interest is *collateral damage*: the expected squared change in alignment along non-target semantic directions, which is a quadratic form in the residual shift with a weighting matrix given by the second moment of those directions. Two choices of that matrix are offered — a sparse-autoencoder dictionary with importance weights, or, their preferred and much cheaper option, the plain empirical second moment of reference activations from 100,000 C4 tokens, which silently folds in co-activation terms the dictionary version assumes away. Steering is then posed as: land on the latitude of the unit sphere where the cosine to the target direction equals a chosen budget α, at the point of least weighted disturbance. Because that feasible set is a sphere of dimension p−2, they solve it with Riemannian gradient descent — an explicit tangent-space projector and a closed-form geodesic step — and prove global convergence of the continuous flow from a projection initialiser, plus a step-size bound from geodesic smoothness. In practice: one iteration, step size 0.3, the weighting matrix normalised by its top eigenvalue, per-token budget scaled by the token's existing alignment with the direction. There is also an exact KKT route that reduces to a one-dimensional root find, which they derive in an appendix and then decline to use, saying one approximate step is enough.

## What counts as an explanation here
A measured metric with a causal claim attached, not a mechanism. Collateral damage is defined as an expectation over semantic directions, so it is only as meaningful as the weighting matrix, and the authors are candid that the matrix *is* the modelling choice: uniform feature importance, an SAE dictionary with frequency weights, or the raw activation second moment are three different statements about what the user cares to protect. The theory earns its keep by showing the three isotropic cases — features orthogonal to the target, nearly orthogonal, or uniform on the sphere — where the optimum collapses to a single closed-form Slerp, which makes Slerp a special case of their method rather than a rival to it.

## How they judged success
Two axes plotted against each other, attack success against task accuracy, so that a method is judged by how far right the curve sits rather than by either endpoint. Baselines are ActAdd (coefficient swept from −10 to 10), Angular Steering, Slerp, and no steering. Steering strength is matched across methods by a single angle budget swept over 0–180°, converted to a cosine target, which is a genuinely careful piece of experimental design — it lets three operators with different knobs be compared at equivalent displacement. The damage metric is validated separately by correlating it with accuracy across methods and coefficients within Qwen2.5-14B, and throughput is measured in tokens per second on official implementations.

## What they found
The trade is real on both ends of the existing menu: reaching a 30–40% attack success rate with ActAdd costs 10–20% accuracy in Gemma-2-9B, while Angular Steering keeps accuracy and never gets past roughly 10% success. Collateral damage tracks accuracy at r below −0.9 across six benchmarks on Qwen, which is the paper's licence for treating the metric as a proxy. Against ActAdd at equal attack success they report up to 20% higher accuracy, and against Angular Steering up to 30% higher attack success. Against Slerp — the harder baseline — the win is small and not uniform: averaged over the full angle sweep, attack success rises in three of four models (Llama-3.2-3B 52.68→54.76, Llama-3.1-8B 51.67→52.13, Gemma 19.28→19.48) and falls slightly on Qwen (7.64→6.93), with accuracy essentially flat apart from a Gemma GSM8K gain (83.39→85.21) and a Llama-3.1 GSM8K gain (67.65→68.80). On Qwen the entire sweep only reaches about 7% attack success, so that arm is close to a floor effect. The theory's own claim — that COAST should never do worse than Slerp, since Slerp is its special case — is contradicted in that one cell, and the authors attribute it to gradient-descent approximation in near-isotropic regions where Slerp is exact. Overhead is 0.9–4.0% throughput against unsteered.

## Limits and open problems they name
The honesty here is unusual and the limits are stated plainly: one behaviour (refusal, itself routed through a single difference-in-means direction at each site), four models, and one attack-success judge. The theoretical guarantee is for the continuous flow; the implementation is one discrete step, so the "global optimum" language describes a method they did not run. The Slerp cell where the ordering reverses is explained by optimizer noise rather than investigated. Appendices A and B carry the proofs; the KKT appendix shows an exact solver exists and is characterised only as slower, with no comparison of solution quality. Nothing is said about negative budgets, so the framework is one-sided in the same way most refusal work is.

## What we take from it
- **A metric that can be checked against behaviour, and the manner of checking.** Collateral damage is scored as the correlation between a geometric quantity and downstream accuracy across methods and coefficients, which turns an abstract squared-displacement into a falsifiable proxy. Any side-effect metric our white-box model reports should be validated the same way — plotted against the benchmark it is supposed to predict, with the correlation and the cell count published — rather than asserted as a cost.
- **Anisotropy as the load-bearing assumption, and the cheap version of it.** The paper's central observation is that uniform-cost steering is a special case, and the interesting one is where the ambient geometry is skewed by co-activation. The second moment of reference activations is the cheapest object that carries that skew, and it requires no dictionary, no SAE training, and one pass over a reference corpus — a default the folder should adopt whenever a steering cost needs defending.
- **The matched-strength protocol is worth stealing outright.** Comparing three operators through a single angle budget, then sweeping it, is what makes the accuracy-versus-success curves comparable at all; the folder has repeatedly read method comparisons whose dose was method-specific. Any intervention comparison we report should name one scalar that all arms can be scored at and sweep it.
- **A caution about guarantees versus implementations.** The theory is about a flow; the experiment is about a single geodesic step with a step size chosen from a bound. When a claim of optimality rests on a limit, the deliverable we ship should state which regime the reported numbers came from — here the strongest theoretical statement and the weakest practical one sit in the same abstract.
