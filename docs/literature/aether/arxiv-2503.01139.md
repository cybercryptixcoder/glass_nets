## What they wanted, and why

The setting is *online* causal discovery: a fixed budget of intervention rounds, each round choosing one variable to intervene on, each round returning a small batch of samples, with a structural discovery algorithm refit after every round. The classical answer to "which variable next" is a numerical utility — an information-theoretic or uncertainty score, or more recently a gradient signal from the differentiable discovery model — and the authors' complaint is that these utilities are *estimated from very little interventional data* precisely in the rounds where the choice matters most, so the first few rounds are chosen almost at random and the search commits early to a bad direction. Their question is whether a language model, given only a table of natural-language variable descriptions and no numbers at all, can supply the missing prior knowledge about which variables are upstream and influential. The larger goal is the one every online-discovery paper states: buy identifiability with the fewest, cheapest interventions, especially when interventional data are expensive to gather.

## What they studied

Four discrete Bayesian-network benchmarks with known ground-truth DAGs from the bnlearn repository — Asia (8 nodes, 8 edges; lung-diagnosis), Child (20 nodes, 25 edges; congenital heart disease), Insurance (27 nodes, 52 edges; car-insurance risk), Alarm (37 nodes, 46 edges; anaesthesia patient monitoring). The discovery backbone is ENCO, a differentiable score-based method, and the numerical targeting method is GIT, the gradient-based rule that the framework is layered on top of. The unit of analysis is the **choice of intervention target per round** — a single variable index — scored by how good the final graph is after a fixed number of rounds. Interventions are single-node hard interventions; the authors state explicitly that soft interventions are out of scope.

## How they did it

The experiment is a controlled substitution inside one loop, which is what makes it readable. First, they show the failure mode empirically: they run the standard numerical targeting over the first epochs on each benchmark and plot which nodes get chosen, finding that on Asia and Alarm the picks land on influential nodes but on Insurance they land on peripheral or leaf nodes — a budget being spent on variables that tell the discovery model little. Second, they build a prompt per benchmark containing a role line ("expert in car insurance risk research"), three generic cautions about confounding, correlation versus causation, and temporal order, then a list of each variable's name and one-sentence plain-English description, and a request to pick the best few root-cause targets with step-by-step reasoning. The framework (called LeGIT) has four stages: a *warmup* in which the language model's chosen targets are used for the first rounds, with self-consistency sampling over shuffled variable orderings to stabilise the answer; a *bootstrapped* stage in which the intermediate graph is inspected for variables that never appeared, and the model is re-prompted on that leftover set alone; a *double selection* stage that replays the warmup and the missing-node lists; and then a *continual* stage where the ordinary numerical method takes over for the remaining budget, on the argument that once the graph is roughly right the numerical signal is trustworthy and the language model is not needed. Everything else is held fixed: same ENCO hyperparameters, same 5000 observational samples, same 33 rounds of 32 interventional samples (1056 total) in the main table and 33 rounds of 16 (528 total) in the low-data table, five seeds, and the same three graph metrics. Baselines are three targeting strategies (CBED, AIT, GIT), three heuristics (uniform random, round-robin over unvisited nodes, and a sample proportional to each node's out-degree), and — the most informative one — a human baseline in which five masters/PhD-level people are given exactly the same information and process as the language model. The out-degree-proportional sampler is deliberately positioned as an oracle, since it reads the true graph's out-degrees, and the authors are careful to note that it is a reference point rather than a competitor that has to be beaten by the same means.

## What counts as an explanation here

Not an explanation at all — the paper's object is a **decision rule** for spending a budget, and its "understanding" is entirely instrumental: a language model's prior over which variable is upstream, expressed as an ordered list of intervention targets, which is then consumed by a numerical loop. There is no claim that the model represents the causal graph, no intermediate object that a reader can inspect (the prompt response is free text and only the extracted variable list is used), and no attribution of the resulting improvement to any particular piece of the prompt. The paper is careful to frame the model's knowledge as world knowledge about *the domain the variables are named from*, and to describe its value as compensating for missing data rather than as knowing the mechanism.

## How they judged success

Three graph-comparison metrics, all computed against the ground-truth DAG and all averaged over five seeds with a standard deviation: Structural Hamming Distance (edge insertions, deletions and reversals needed; lower is better), Structural Intervention Distance (the number of pairs whose interventional distribution the learned graph gets wrong; lower is better), and the Balanced Scoring Function (an edge/independence-weighted accuracy that corrects for the fact that sparse graphs make raw precision look good; higher is better). Two budget regimes are reported as separate tables rather than pooled. Additional qualitative evidence is a curve of mean SHD against the number of interventional samples, and figures showing which nodes each strategy picked in the first five epochs. There is no statistical test beyond the reported seed standard deviations, no paired test against GIT, and no ablation of the prompt components, the self-consistency step, or the warmup length — the framework is evaluated only as a whole.

## What they found

Under the main budget, LeGIT has the lowest SHD of every method on all four benchmarks: 17.40 against 19.60 for GIT and 22.60 for the human baseline on Alarm, 12.60 against 16.40 and 14.20 on Insurance, 2.20 against 2.80 and 2.00 on Child, 0.80 against 1.00 and 1.40 on Asia. The same ordering holds on SID and BSF, with one visible exception worth recording: on Child the human baseline's BSF (0.9944) is above LeGIT's (0.9858) even though LeGIT wins on SHD and SID, and on Asia the out-degree oracle is at 1.00 SHD while every method including LeGIT is above it. The low-data table is where the claim is strongest and also where the odd result appears: at 16 samples per round LeGIT improves on GIT by about six SHD points on Alarm (21.00 against 27.20) and roughly four on Insurance, but on Asia LeGIT's SHD (1.40) sits between GIT (1.80) and the two heuristics (1.20 each), so the smallest network does not separate the methods at all. The paper's own reading of the low-data regime is the one to keep: the numerical method no longer beats round-robin on the three smaller datasets, so the advantage of the prior is not that it is better than a good heuristic, it is that a numerical signal fitted on almost no interventional data is worthless, and something other than that signal has to make the early choices.

## Limits and open problems they name

Two things are off the table by declaration: soft interventions, and interventions on sets rather than single nodes. The convergence argument in the appendix is weak and the authors present it as a sketch — it assumes faithfulness, no hidden confounders, that the base method converges given enough informative data, and then takes as a lemma that the language model's warmup targets are "sufficiently informative" with nonzero probability, which makes the whole proposition essentially "the base method eventually converges because it eventually dominates the choices". There is no analysis of what happens when the model's domain knowledge is wrong: the prompt's failure direction (choosing a plausible-sounding but causally irrelevant upstream variable) is not measured, and no benchmark in which the variable names are uninformative or deliberately misleading is run. The ablations they do report are confined to the appendix hyperparameter table and the ENCO settings, so the number of rounds the language model is used for, and whether self-consistency matters, are unexamined; the two warmup lengths are stated as a fixed choice scaled to graph size rather than derived or swept. Reproducibility is limited by the use of a specific hosted model version, with no report of prompt-level variance beyond the self-consistency sampling.

## What we take from it

Three things, ordered by how much they transfer.

**A heuristic prior is worth exactly as much as the first few decisions, and that is a design rule, not a demotion.** The framework is not an alternative to the numerical method — it is a *scheduler* that says which component makes the decision at which point in the budget, and it hands control back once the numerical estimate becomes informative. Mechanical rule for us: **when our white-box account-selection pipeline uses a cheap prior (a language model, a metadata heuristic, a degree proxy) to make the first k choices, the write-up states k, states the criterion under which the expensive numerical method takes over, and reports the score as a function of k rather than at a single k** — otherwise the reader cannot tell a prior that helped from a prior that was simply never contradicted. This is entry 549's stopping rule and entry 545's selection/commitment split applied to the *ordering* of a budget rather than to a threshold.

**Report the heuristic baselines that a learned method must lose to on some subset, or you cannot tell where the learning is.** Their own tables contain the interesting negative: at the low budget, round-robin beats the gradient method on three of four benchmarks and is within noise of everything on Asia; and the out-degree oracle (which reads the true graph) is only reachable on the smallest network. Mechanical rule for us: **every "our method beats the numerical baseline" table carries a trivial heuristic (round-robin or uniform) and, where available, an oracle that uses information no real method has, and any cell where the oracle is not strictly best is discussed in the text** — an oracle that loses is a bug in the metric or the setup, and it is the cheapest available check.

**A domain prior bought from named variables does not transfer to unnamed ones — and no experiment here tests that.** The whole result rests on variable descriptions like "whether or not the patient is a smoker", which encode the answer. The paper notices the adjacent risk (it cites the causal-parrot critique and describes the model's own hallucinations as the thing to mitigate) but never runs a control where descriptions are stripped, anonymised, or shuffled between graphs, which is the single experiment that would separate "the model knows the domain" from "the model is a convenient rewrite of the out-degree oracle". Mechanical rule for us: **any time a prior enters a selection loop from a text or metadata channel, we run the anonymised-channel control (names replaced, descriptions permuted across structurally different cases) before claiming the channel carries knowledge, and the result is reported whichever way it falls.** This is the practical form of entry 554's "the prior *is* the training distribution": here the training distribution is the public literature about these four benchmark domains, and a fifth domain would show it.
