# Unknown Unknowns: Model Misspecification in Machine Learning for Physics

- **Key:** arxiv-2608.13633
- **Authors / organisation:** Juan Cruz-Martinez, Carolina Cuesta-Lazaro, Alexander Held, Michael Kagan; Universidad de Sevilla; Center for Computational Astrophysics, Flatiron Institute / Institute for Advanced Study; University of Wisconsin–Madison; SLAC National Accelerator Laboratory

- **Kind:** survey/agenda (position and review article, part of the VERaiPHY validation series for ML in physics)
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

Physics has always advanced by building a model, finding where it breaks, and iterating; the authors' point of departure is that machine learning has not created a new problem but inherited and scaled an old one. They separate misspecification into two uses that are routinely confused. Sometimes the failure *is* the discovery — an anomaly that the current model cannot absorb is the signal being hunted — and sometimes the failure is contamination from a component nobody expects to resolve (detector response, non-perturbative effects, foregrounds) that must be absorbed so it does not bias a measurement. The goal of the article is a framework in which an analysis can tell those two apart, and in which a learned model that passes every available check is still not treated as established. Their larger claim is that the honest target is distributional adequacy and never mechanistic correctness, so the write-up of any model-based result has to carry its own suspicion rather than a certificate.

## What they studied

No single model or dataset: the object of study is the *modelling chain* itself, split into links (the physical theory governing the true state, the observation model mapping state to what instruments record, the numerical implementation of each, and — when a learned model is added for inference — a further link). Failures are typed by which link and which kind: structural (wrong functional relationship, missing degrees of freedom, wrong causal structure), numerical (discretisation, finite resolution, sub-grid prescriptions), distributional (wrong noise family or prior), and observational (instrument effects and selection). The unit of analysis is an assumption, and the evidence is drawn from astronomy, cosmology and collider physics: supernova cosmology, CMB-versus-late-universe distances, parton distribution function fits, photometric redshift calibration, perturbative truncation, unfolding, and a set of ML components now standard in those pipelines (emulators, classifiers, normalising flows, simulation-based inference, reweighting).

## How they did it

The article is a structured argument rather than an experiment, and its method is a taxonomy plus a set of worked failure stories from the literature. Part one draws the distinctions: a formal definition of misspecification as the data-generating process lying outside the family the model can represent, a separate treatment of rarity (an event occurring at its predicted rate is a success, not a warning), and a long argument about the instability of the known/unknown boundary — a known unknown whose *characterisation* is wrong becomes an unknown unknown in its own right, illustrated by PDF uncertainties derived under a functional-form choice and by photometric calibrations fitted on an unrepresentative subsample. Part two organises detection into three complementary families and asks what each can and cannot reach: goodness-of-fit tests between data and model samples (binary classifier two-sample test, optimal transport and its sliced version, kernel two-sample discrepancy, chi-square and its covariance-corrected form, compared in a table on power, cost and interpretability); splitting the data along physically meaningful boundaries rather than at random (energy regimes, independent experiments, early-versus-late-universe probes, the CMB/late-universe disagreement as the standing example); and closure testing, where pseudodata are generated from a fully known distribution so that a method that recovers the known truth can be said to close, with the caveat that closure is only as good as the assumption that the generator is right. Part three sorts mitigation into four families — cover with nuisance parameters, calibrate the model to data, avoid the features that are poorly modelled, or work empirically from data — with the mapping from failure type to family, and emphasises an iterative loop of detect, attribute, address, re-validate, plus blind analysis as the safeguard against the analyst selecting and interpreting with the same data. Part four is speculative: what LLM-driven discovery systems would have to get right, and why fitness scores and Bayesian model weighting both relocate rather than remove the assumption.

## What counts as an explanation here

The explanatory object is a **typed account of a failure plus a stated reach**: which link of the chain is wrong, which of the four kinds of failure it is, which diagnostic can see it, which mitigations apply, and what the mitigation buys and costs. No model of the system is produced. The article's sharpest object is a negative one — the explicit statement that no diagnostic confirms correctness, so the best available explanation of a model is a battery of tests whose *design* (which subsets, which pseudodata variations, which summary statistics) narrows the space of candidate failures. That is deliberately partial and predictive only in the weak sense: it predicts which failure classes will be caught and which will pass, not what the data-generating process is.

## How they judged success

No benchmark, no metric, no ablation — the standards are argued rather than measured, and the evidence is the historical record of anomalies that were later attributed to systematics or to new physics. The pieces of machinery that function as success criteria are: whether a diagnostic has power against a named failure class (the metrics table compares this qualitatively); whether an independent probe agrees, with early/late-universe agreement treated as the strongest available form of generalisation test; whether a method closes on pseudodata whose truth is controlled; and for mitigation, whether the correction is validated on control data and whether it can be shown not to absorb the effect of interest. Two recurring cautions stand in for statistics: testing many aspects of misspecification produces false positives by construction, and overly inflated uncertainties both lose sensitivity and make the result harder to interpret.

## What they found

The load-bearing claims are conceptual and the authors state them as such. First, the definition of misspecification cannot be operationalised: a model can reproduce the observed data through the wrong mechanism, so every diagnostic in the review, including the most powerful, is testing adequacy and not truth. Second, detection and attribution are different problems, and attribution is bought by *design* of the comparisons rather than by any test's output — a flagged discrepancy rarely says which link produced it. Third, the known/unknown boundary moves under its own weight: the examples given (PDF uncertainties, photometric redshift calibration) are cases where the uncertainty was characterised, the characterisation was wrong, and the resulting error was invisible to the uncertainty budget it should have enlarged. Fourth, ML cuts both ways — it amplifies the impact of simulator defects because it can exploit high-dimensional correlations that hand-built summaries cannot, and it surfaces failures that lower-dimensional diagnostics miss, while enabling more powerful corrections (classifier-based reweighting, flows, transport-based calibration). Fifth, data-driven methods do not remove assumptions, they relocate them: the background fitted from sidebands rests on the assumption that the sideband and signal region are related, and the collision data that motivated the caution is the excess that vanished with more data. Sixth, on automation, the authors name two gaps they consider unsolved — there is no accepted metric for discovery (predictive accuracy on held-out data is what current systems optimise and is not what physicists mean by understanding, since two theories can predict identically while only one generalises) and current systems have no taste, meaning no preference among empirically adequate explanations for the one that will generalise.

## Limits and open problems they name

Stated by the authors: no diagnostic can confirm correct specification, and this is a theorem-like fact about observational checks rather than a gap to be filled; attribution from a diagnostic alone is not possible in general; closure testing fails exactly where the underlying physics is unknown, which is the regime new-physics searches live in; robustness against unknown unknowns cannot be guaranteed by any technique and is described as a disposition rather than a property; and automated discovery lacks both a metric and taste, with the burden of distinguishing interesting from plausible-looking explanations left with the human. Implicit limits of the article itself, not of its subject: it is a review without new experiments, all its quantitative content is second-hand, and its evidence is drawn from three neighbouring fields, so its transfer to a setting where the "instrument" is a neural network and the "chain" is a single forward pass is an argument by analogy that the authors make but do not test.

## What we take from it

Five things, three of them framing, one a typing scheme the fields share, and one design rule. **A vocabulary**: physics calls it systematics, statistics calls it misspecification, ML calls it distribution shift, and the three communities have separately built overlapping instruments — reading this article is the cheapest way to import the physicists' version, which is the most mature of the three. **A typing scheme for our own failures**: the four-way split (structural, numerical, distributional, observational) is directly usable on a white-box account of a network, where "structural" is the wrong causal story the account tells, "numerical" is the account's own approximation (a dictionary with too few features, a probe fitted on finite data), "distributional" is an assumed noise or prior family, and "observational" is the activation-recording pipeline itself. Naming which type a known limitation belongs to decides which of the four mitigations has any chance, which is a decision our write-ups currently make implicitly. **The relocation principle**: any diagnostic or mitigation we adopt moves the assumption rather than deleting it, and the write-up has to say where it moved — this is the general form of several of this folder's specific warnings (a probe's training prior, a benchmark's generator). **And the design rule that is worth copying outright**: attribution comes from designing comparisons, not from reading a test's verdict. Their version is closure testing plus deliberately varied pseudodata — generate the network's behaviour from several structurally different generators consistent with what we have observed, and ask which deviations the account absorbs and which it reports. That is a battery we can build from the weights side (we can synthesise a model whose mechanism we chose) at a cost no external benchmark can match, and it converts "our account is faithful" into "our account catches this class of failure and does not catch that class", which is the only form of the claim that is checkable. **Warning**: this article is the strongest statement in the folder that a passing suite is not a licence — read it as the multi-field version of run 558's known-truth table, and as the reason to print, for every account, the failure classes it is blind to.
