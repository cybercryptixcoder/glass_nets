# When Feature Representation Changes Local Explanations: A Controlled Comparison of SHAP and LIME under Numerical Discretization

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.21159
- **Authors / organisation:** Parv Patel (single author) — Indian Institute of Technology Palakkad
- **Date:** 2026-11-16
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21159
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Local feature attributions are computed over whatever representation an earlier data-engineering step chose — an age passed as an integer, bucketed into decades, or standardised — yet readers consume the output as a statement about the underlying quantity. Prior work had shown that such routine choices shift SHAP-derived importance even with the classifier frozen, and that result can be abused to make a biased model read as neutral. The open question the author takes up is whether that sensitivity belongs to Shapley-value estimation specifically or to post-hoc local explanation in general, which decides whether switching explainer is a defence. Answering it requires the two explainers to see the same instances and the same prediction target with each one's own settings held fixed. The paper is explicitly framed as a controlled empirical comparison with named controls, not a new method and not a new phenomenon.

## What they studied
A single frozen classifier — a 200-tree random forest on UCI Adult (32,561 train / 16,281 test rows, held-out ROC-AUC 0.9138) — with the `age` column collapsed onto equidistant or quantile bin representatives at three bin counts, and the same 120 stratified held-out instances explained in every condition. The unit of analysis is a per-instance feature attribution from two model-agnostic explainers, KernelSHAP and LIME, operating on a deliberately chosen 12-column interpretable feature space (one-hot expansion to 106 columns happens inside the frozen pipeline and is never shown to the explainers). Not a neural network and not an internal component: the object under study is the explanation itself, and what a preprocessing choice does to it.

## How they did it
Six pre-specified representation conditions (equi-width and quantile, three bin counts each) against an unchanged baseline, with bin edges fitted on training age only and the representative taken as the bin midpoint. Every setting, metric, threshold and seed was fixed in a configuration file before results were examined; the forest's parameters are hashed and re-checked after all explanations to prove the model never moved. Crucially, the seed for each explanation depends on the repeat and the instance but not on the condition, so a discretized condition and the baseline share the explainer's random draws and differ only in the function being explained. Each explainer is first run against *itself* at different seeds to build a seed-noise floor, whose 95th percentile defines "large"; measured changes are then expressed as ratios to that floor. Prediction-side coupling is recorded per instance (class flip, change in predicted probability, held-out AUC under quantized input) and joined onto every attribution comparison. LIME's own internal discretisation is switched off for the main run — because leaving the library default on would apply the manipulation under study to the control — and re-enabled only as a sensitivity condition. Statistics are cluster bootstraps over instances and paired Wilcoxon tests with rank-biserial effect sizes, Holm-corrected within families.

## What counts as an explanation here
An attribution vector over the input features, plus the derived feature-importance ranking. The paper does not propose a new explanatory object; it audits the ones practitioners already consume. Its positive contribution is a *scope statement* about what such an object can mean: for KernelSHAP, a frozen model behind a quantizing wrapper is shown analytically to be exactly equivalent to explaining the unchanged model at the quantized instance against a quantized background, so an attribution change is a statement about a changed effective input and cannot be read as a property of the explainer alone. For LIME the equivalence fails, so the two explainers are not even solving the same transformed problem. Nothing here is predictive of model behaviour; it is a statement about the reliability of a readout under a change that leaves the model intact.

## How they judged success
Pre-registered directional hypotheses tested per (condition, explainer) with Holm correction; every attribution change scored against the explainer's own seed-noise floor rather than against zero; rank metrics (Spearman, top-k overlap, the feature's own rank shift) reported separately from magnitude metrics; prediction agreement reported beside every comparison so an attribution move is never attributed to the explainer when the model itself changed its output; reconstruction error and weighted local R² reported as *diagnostics only*, explicitly not as validity checks. Effect sizes accompany every p-value, and the paper separates statistical from practical significance by naming which observations would actually change a reader's behaviour.

## What they found
Explainer noise is not negligible and differs sharply between methods: median movement when re-running unchanged input is 0.000327 for KernelSHAP against 0.002233 for LIME on the raw scale (a factor of 6.8), and 5.1× apart after normalising to shares of attribution mass. Against that floor, KernelSHAP's age attribution moves in all six conditions (median ratios 2.4–59, all Holm-significant), while LIME exceeds its own floor in only two of six, both at the coarsest binning; at one quantile setting its response is statistically indistinguishable from its re-run variation and in three others significantly smaller than it. SHAP responds more than LIME in every condition (3.0–14.1× on the normalised scale, rank-biserial 0.81–1.00) and is the more consistent method (2.5–4.6× smaller within-instance spread). Prediction is nearly unaffected — class flips in 0.8–5.8% of instances, AUC never below 0.9097 — yet 45–94% of instances carry a large SHAP attribution shift with no class flip, and the correlation between the two quantities is 0.42–0.76 for SHAP against about 0.10 for LIME. Rankings barely move at all: median Spearman never below 0.979, top-3 overlap 1.00 everywhere. For LIME, re-running the explainer disturbs its ordering more (0.944) than discretising the feature does (≥0.986) — a null result reported as one. LIME's median weighted local R² sits between 0.543 and 0.545 across all conditions, including the one where its attribution moved beyond noise, so the quality number LIME reports by default is blind to the change.

## Limits and open problems they name
The central limit is structural: the representation change and the change in effective input cannot be separated within a frozen-model design, so no attribution result here is a "pure explainer effect" and all of them are conditional on the reported prediction changes. One dataset, one continuous feature, one model family, 120 instances. Both explainers evaluate off the data manifold under the libraries' default marginal formulations. Finite budgets (1,500 coalitions over 40 background rows; 5,000 perturbations) mean the floor itself is budget-dependent. The SHAP–LIME magnitude comparison is between normalised quantities, not equivalent ones, and part of SHAP's larger response may be definitional because Shapley values must sum to the output. Three explanations for the divergence — definitional, structural (LIME's linear surrogate in un-quantised coordinates averages across the steps), and noise — are stated and left unseparated. Stability is never correctness: a constant vector would be perfectly stable, and nothing here says which explanation is right.

## What we take from it
A measurement discipline to copy wholesale, and one warning that lands directly on our battery. The **seed-noise floor** is the cheapest control in the explainability literature and we have no reason not to adopt it: any stochastic readout we compute — a sampling-based probe, a Shapley-style attribution over our own features, an SHAP/LIME-style summary of a black-box model — should be re-run on unchanged input and its effect expressed as a ratio to that re-run spread, because an effect smaller than the explainer's own variance is not a finding and would otherwise be published as one. The paper's second reusable idea is that **magnitude and ranking are different kinds of instability**: rank correlation is the number most stability claims are made in, and here it reads 0.98–1.00 while the magnitudes shift by up to 59 noise floors. For us the consequence is that a battery reporting "the top-k features are unchanged" or "the ordering is preserved" is reporting almost nothing; the magnitude is where the effect lives, and where a reader's interpretation would change. Third, the *scope statement* is the model for how we should describe any readout fitted through a wrapper we did not design: state precisely which function was evaluated and at which input, because for KernelSHAP the frozen model plus a quantizing wrapper is provably a different effective input, and a design that freezes the network's parameters still has not frozen its behaviour. Fourth, a warning about defaults that applies to our own tooling — LIME ships with internal discretisation on by default, so a study of representation that used the library as-is would have applied the treatment to its own control; when we instrument a model with a third-party explainer, the default we did not choose is part of the method. What we do *not* take: anything about internal state. This is a post-hoc, output-level audit and it says nothing about whether a component carries a computation — but its honesty about what its design cannot separate is exactly the form we should use for our own conditional claims.
