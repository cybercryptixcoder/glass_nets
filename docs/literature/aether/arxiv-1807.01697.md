# Benchmarking Neural Network Robustness to Common Corruptions and Surface Variations

- **Key:** arxiv-1807.01697
- **Authors / organisation:** Dan Hendrycks (University of California, Berkeley); Thomas G. Dietterich (Oregon State University)
- **Date:** 2019-04-27 (v5; v1 2018-07-04)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1807.01697
- **Kind:** benchmark
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

Robustness research had collapsed into adversarial robustness: the paper's related-work section notes that the adversarial line had made the word roughly a synonym for worst-case perturbation, while the corruptions that actually occur in deployment — noise, blur, weather, compression — went unmeasured and were scored on bespoke, incomparable test sets. The authors wanted to make robustness to *ordinary* input degradation a standardised, hard-to-cherry-pick quantity, on the theory that any progress reported against a fixed public benchmark is progress a later paper cannot move the goal posts on. They also wanted a second axis that adversarial work does not touch at all: inputs whose *content* is intact and whose *appearance* changes — a new illustration style for a known object, an unfamiliar member of a known category — which they call surface variation robustness. Both goals are in service of a broader ambition stated in the conclusion: classifiers that track class structure rather than spurious cues, "as models are unleashed into safety-critical real-world settings". The paper is explicitly a measuring instrument, not a mechanism.

## What they studied

Image classifiers trained only on clean data, evaluated on two new datasets. **ImageNet-C** applies fifteen algorithmic corruption families to the ImageNet validation set — four noise types, four blurs, three weather effects, four digital artefacts — each at five severities, so 75 distinct test conditions; networks never train on any of them. **Icons-50** is 10,000 icon images over 50 icon-semantic classes harvested from the design styles of several platform vendors, each class carrying roughly 200 images and many styles, with two protocols: hold out one vendor's style entirely (style robustness) or hold out 50 named subtypes while keeping the broad classes (subtype robustness). Models span the standard architecture ladder of the period — AlexNet, SqueezeNet, several VGG variants with and without batch norm, ResNet-18/50, DenseNets, ResNeXts, CondenseNets, multigrid nets, MSDNets — plus five interventions on top of ResNet-50. The unit of analysis is the whole model: one number per model per condition.

## How they did it

Each corruption family is generated procedurally at five severities, with per-image randomisation so that instances are not pixel-identical at fixed severity, and the dataset plus the generation code is published so it can be recreated rather than merely downloaded. Error is normalised twice: a **Corruption Error** divides a network's summed top-1 error across the five severities by AlexNet's summed error on the same corruption, which handles the fact that fog is intrinsically harder than brightness; averaging the fifteen values gives **mCE**. Because that first metric rewards raw accuracy, a second is computed by subtracting clean error from both numerator and denominator first, giving **Relative mCE** — the degradation attributable to corruption rather than to the model's overall quality. The protocol forbids training on the 75 test corruptions and ships four *extra* families (speckle, Gaussian blur, spatter, saturation) for training or validation, precisely so that robustness can be obtained without fitting the test set. Interventions are then applied to ResNet-50 — stability training with a tuned noise range and stability weight, non-local-means denoising driven by a noise estimator, CLAHE preprocessing, ten-crop averaging — and each is scored by both metrics. For Icons-50 the same models are trained with cosine learning-rate decay and Nesterov momentum at 32x32 resolution, once withholding an entire vendor's style and once withholding a set of named subtypes, and scored by held-out accuracy; a control experiment holds out *one version* of icons available in many versions, to show the training set is not simply too small. Two supplementary probes reuse CIFAR-100's hierarchy and ImageNet-22K's superset relation to repeat the subtype experiment at other scales, and a style-transfer corruption is added as a synthetic stand-in for real style variation.

## What counts as an explanation here

Nothing about internals. The object produced is a **benchmark plus a pair of normalised metrics**, and the explanatory work it does is comparative: which architectures are more robust *than their accuracy would predict*, which interventions help, which hurt. It is deliberately partial — the paper's claim is never "here is why the network fails", only "here is a fixed instrument under which these failures are comparable across models and years". The one near-mechanistic statement is architectural: multiscale processing, which carries fine detail alongside coarse representation through the stack, appears to suppress pixel-level distraction, and the authors treat that as a hypothesis the benchmark supports rather than a finding they isolated. Whether that architectural account is sufficient is untestable from this paper's data: multiscale nets also changed clean accuracy and parameter count at the same time as the mechanism.

## How they judged success

Construct validity rather than a single score: the benchmark is successful if (a) it separates models that the clean top-1 metric conflates, and (b) the normalised metric changes sign relative to the raw one for some pairs of models, which is what proves the normalisation is not redundant. Baselines are AlexNet for every corruption (the normaliser) and clean top-1 error for every architecture. Sensitivity checks are the paired metrics themselves, human-level comparison stated qualitatively, and the Icons-50 multi-version control. There is no held-out test of the benchmark's own predictive validity, no error bars or seeds, and no human baseline measured on ImageNet-C — the claims about subhuman robustness rest on the qualitative literature rather than a matched experiment.

## What they found

On raw **mCE**, the architecture ladder looks like steady progress: AlexNet 100 by construction (43.5% clean error), SqueezeNet 104.4 (41.8%), VGG-11 93.5 (31.0%), VGG-19 88.9 (27.6%), VGG-19+BN 81.6 (25.8%), ResNet-18 84.7 (30.2%), ResNet-50 76.7 (23.9%). On **Relative mCE**, the same ladder is flat-to-worse: SqueezeNet 117.9, VGG-11 123.3, VGG-19 122.9, VGG-19+BN 111.1, ResNet-18 103.9, ResNet-50 105.0 — every one of them *above* AlexNet's 100, meaning the accuracy gains were not accompanied by any gain in resilience to corruption, and the paper's own summary is that robustness "in itself" barely moved across roughly six years of architecture work. The normalisation is therefore the finding: the same table read two ways gives opposite headlines. Among interventions, three helped — CLAHE reduced ResNet-50 mCE from 76.7 to 74.5 while nudging clean error down from 23.87% to 23.55%; multigrid and MSDNet reached 73.3 and 73.6 at clean errors of 24.6; and feature-aggregating or larger models helped substantially, DenseNet-121 73.4 at 25.6% clean, ResNeXt-50 68.2 at 22.9%, DenseNet-161 66.4 at 22.9%, ResNeXt-101 62.2 at 21.0%. Three other plausible remedies failed or backfired: stability training across every tuned noise and weight setting produced worse mCE than the baseline even on held-out noise types; non-local-means denoising *raised* mCE from 76.7 to 82.1 because slight smoothing leaked into the non-noise corruptions; smaller CondenseNets were less robust than larger models of comparable accuracy (80.8 at 26.3% clean, 84.6 at 28.9%); and ten-crop averaging gained accuracy without gaining robustness. On Icons-50 the deficits are large and not attributable to data scarcity: holding out one vendor's style, ResNet reaches 58.3% and DenseNet and ResNeXt do worse at 48.5% and 51.8%, while the same models score 97.2-98.0% on the held-out-version control. Holding out 50 subtypes, the ordering inverts — ResNet 57.5%, DenseNet 58.7%, ResNeXt 60.0% — which the authors read as evidence that style and subtype robustness are distinct capacities. Shake-Shake regularisation improved both, style dramatically (51.8 to 76.0) and subtype modestly (60.0 to 63.6), and multiscale MSDNets improved style (65.1 versus DenseNet's 48.5) while barely moving subtype. The supplementary probes agree: a Wide ResNet on CIFAR-100 averages 59.7% broad-class error on held-out subtypes against 13.2% on seen ones, and style transfer as a synthetic corruption is far harder than any ImageNet-C family — ResNet-50 only reaches 92 versus 76.7 for the algorithmic corruptions.

## Limits and open problems they name

The paper is candid about scope and about the instrument's blind spots. Corruptions applied to 32x32 inputs are explicitly not predictive of robustness at larger sizes, so the benchmark does not transfer to small-image datasets. Shake-Shake could not be evaluated on corruption robustness at all because GPU memory would not accommodate it at ImageNet scale, leaving the strongest surface-variation intervention untested on the main benchmark. There is no mechanism offered for any result, no seeds or confidence intervals, no matched human experiment, and the subtype robustness gap has no account given beyond the observation that it exists. The dataset is also explicitly presented as already superseded by the authors' own successor benchmark, which the v5 record names as arXiv:1903.12261 — so the paper is a snapshot of a benchmark lineage rather than its final form. Finally, the "robustness in itself barely changed" conclusion depends entirely on AlexNet being a reasonable normaliser; the authors do not test the metric's sensitivity to that choice.

## What we take from it

Four things, in descending order of transferability.

- **The normalised-versus-raw reporting pair, as a reusable template.** Any battery we publish that scores models under perturbation should report the raw aggregate *and* the degradation relative to a fixed reference model, because here the two give opposite answers about whether several years of progress helped. Same discipline as the folder's two-sided robustness reporting in `arxiv-2007.04440`, applied to the *normaliser* axis: name your baseline, and check whether the ranking survives it.
- **A method for buying blind-spot coverage without fitting the test set.** Publishing four extra corruption families for training or validation, while forbidding training on the 75 scored ones, is a cheap way to keep a benchmark from being gamed by fitting it. For us the analogue is holding back a set of probe directions or intervention settings that we never optimise against, and saying so in the report.
- **Six interventions, three of which fail, all reported with the same two numbers.** Stability training, denoising, small models, ten-crop — each an intuitively reasonable fix — are reported as failures with their exact mCE deltas rather than omitted. Denoising is the instructive one: it fixed the corruption it targeted and damaged the others, which is the same one-sided-metric trap `arxiv-2007.04440` diagnosed. Our rule: any intervention claimed to improve a read-out gets scored on the full battery, and the failures get published.
- **"Robust in itself" is a construct that has to be measured separately from accuracy**, and the Icons-50 result adds a sharper version: two perturbations that both leave class structure intact (a new style; an unseen subtype) are *not* served by the same capacity — the architecture ranking inverts between them — so a single "robustness" score over a bundle of perturbations can average away the structure we care about. This connects directly to `arxiv-1611.03530`: as with the randomised-label ladder, sweeping one intervention axis and reporting the endpoints is weaker than reporting the whole curve with the per-condition breakdown intact.
