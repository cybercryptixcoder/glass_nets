## What they wanted, and why

Neurons in real networks are sometimes clean single features and sometimes respond to unrelated mixtures, and the paper's opening question is why the same layer can contain both. Their answer is a mechanism, superposition: when features are sparse, a model can store more of them than it has directions by letting them share directions and tolerating the interference, then filtering the noise with a nonlinearity. The goal was not to measure a real network but to build the smallest system in which the hypothesised phenomenon is the *ground truth* of the model rather than a post-hoc reading of it. From there they wanted the condition under which superposition is chosen, the form it takes geometrically, and what it implies for interpretability programmes that need to enumerate a model's features. The strategic frame at the end is explicit: "solving superposition" — being able to enumerate every feature — is treated as a prerequisite for the kind of universal claim mechanistic interpretability would like to make about safety.

## What they studied

Synthetic data only: an input vector whose entries are treated as features, each zero with probability S and otherwise drawn uniformly, weighted by a per-feature importance multiplier, compressed into a much smaller hidden space by a learned matrix and decompressed by its transpose (occasionally an independently learned second matrix). The unit of analysis is the *direction* — one column of the weight matrix per input feature — plus, in the last two model families, the *neuron* in a basis the architecture makes privileged. Two core models differ only in a final activation: a linear output model and a ReLU output model. A third adds a ReLU on the hidden layer so that features can align with neurons, and a fourth replaces identity recovery with computing the absolute value of each feature, which is the only one of the four that genuinely needs its hidden nonlinearity.

## How they did it

Train the autoencoder on synthetic sparse vectors and inspect the weights directly instead of the loss in isolation. Three statistics carry the analysis: the Gram matrix of the embedding (a features-by-features map of which features interfere), the norm of each feature's embedding (approximately one if the feature is represented, zero if not), and a per-feature *dimensionality* defined as the squared embedding norm divided by the sum of squared projections of all other embeddings onto that direction — a fraction of a dimension. Sweep the sparsity parameter S and watch represented features rise above the number of available dimensions; in the larger study they report dimensions-per-feature as hidden width divided by total squared norm, because that ratio is basis-independent. To isolate the transition from "not learned" to "in superposition" to "dedicated dimension" they run a two-feature/one-dimension model over a grid of second-feature importance against sparsity, average over ten trained models after discarding the highest-loss run, and compare the empirical diagram with a closed-form loss for each of the three possible weight configurations. Geometry is studied in the uniform case (all features identical) by reading off the fractions the dimensionalities cluster at and matching them to the vertices of specific uniform polytopes; non-uniform cases are studied by perturbing one feature's density, then by linking the on/off events of bundles of features to make them correlated, and by allowing only one member of a set to be active at a time to make them anticorrelated. The privileged-basis model is trained with a ReLU on the hidden layer (a thousand runs per sparsity setting, keeping the lowest-loss one), and adversarial vulnerability is tested with analytically constructed L2 attacks rather than gradient descent because the ReLU zero region causes gradient masking.

## What counts as an explanation here

A *mechanism in the weights*, stated as a claim about the learned matrix and readable at a glance: this feature is not represented (near-zero norm), this feature has its own dimension (norm near one, orthogonal to all others), these two are antipodal (equal norms, cosine near minus one), these five form a pentagon. The explanation is meant to be complete for these models — the authors can say exactly what every weight entry is doing — and it predicts the model's loss, its response to inputs with two features active at once, and its vulnerability to a small crafted perturbation. It is avowedly local: they say plainly that the geometry and learning-dynamics findings may be idiosyncratic to the toy, while treating superposition's existence, the monosemantic/polysemantic mixture, and perhaps the adversarial link as the parts likely to generalise. There is also a second, more interesting sense in which the explanation is predictive — the compressed-sensing bound they derive says how many features *cannot* be packed for a given sparsity, which is a ceiling on any account of this kind, not a fit to the data.

## How they judged success

Success is the existence of the phenomenon under conditions where the linear baseline provably cannot produce it, plus agreement between the mechanism and quantities not used to fit it. Concretely: the linear baseline always learns the top second-dimension-count of important features and never more, so any extra feature represented by the ReLU model is superposition by construction; the phase diagram is validated by matching an empirical grid against a closed-form "toy model of the toy model" whose crossover is analytically first-order; the geometric account is validated by predicting which *fractional* dimensionalities appear and by showing those fractions are exactly those of the corresponding polytope factors; the adversarial claim is validated by measuring attack harm as a function of where the model sits in the sparsity sweep, with the authors noting the increase and that it tracks features-per-dimension. There is no held-out benchmark, no real model scored, and no random-direction baseline — the controls are analytical (the linear model, the closed-form losses) rather than empirical.

## What they found

- Dense features give the linear solution (top-k of the important features, orthogonal); as sparsity rises the ReLU-output model represents more features than it has dimensions, beginning with the least important, and originally pairs them antipodally before moving to richer arrangements.
- The transition between "not learned", "in superposition" and "dedicated dimension" is sharp enough to call a phase change — a discontinuity in the derivative of the optimal loss in the closed-form two-feature/one-dimension case, observed on the empirical grid too.
- Under uniform features the per-feature dimensionality clusters at specific fractions, which they read as the vertices of uniform polytopes: tetrahedron, triangle, antipodal pair, pentagon, square antiprism. Dimensions-per-feature is sticky near one and near one half, and the half-sticky point is the antipodal-pair strategy. They connect the packing problem to the Thomson problem and observe that non-uniform solutions appear as combinations of lower-dimensional uniform pieces.
- Non-uniform features deform the polytope smoothly until a critical imbalance snaps it to a different one (making one feature sparse enough collapses a pentagon into two digons with the sparse point at zero).
- Correlated features prefer to be orthogonal and, in larger models, form what they call a local almost-orthogonal basis inside an otherwise superimposed model; when capacity is short they collapse into the pair's principal component, which makes PCA and superposition read as competing strategies that trade off against correlation and sparsity. Anticorrelated features prefer to share a dimension and to interfere negatively.
- In a privileged basis the model reproduces the empirical mixture: some neurons cleanly single-feature, a subset strongly polysemantic, and the fraction drifting with sparsity. Many polysemantic neurons carry one high-weight primary feature plus smaller secondary ones, which they note matches the "looks interpretable at top activations, mixed at lower magnitude" pattern reported in real language models.
- Computation survives superposition: with a hundred features and forty neurons the model still computes absolute value, and the mechanism is legible — most neurons implement the obvious positive/negative pair, and a few implement an asymmetric motif where one feature interferes with the other but not vice versa, repaired by a second neuron that inhibits it.
- Adversarial vulnerability rises sharply (by more than a factor of three on their measure) as superposition forms, and tracks features-per-dimension; adversarial training reduces superposition only at attack sizes around eighty percent of the input norm, which they judge unsatisfying.
- Learning dynamics show discrete jumps between dimensionalities coinciding with small drops in the loss curve, and multi-step geometric rearrangements; they suggest smooth-looking loss curves in larger models may be many such jumps in aggregate.

## Limits and open problems they name

The final activation is doing all the work and the model is deliberately tiny; they enumerate what they cannot say: whether superposition exists in real models to the extent the toy suggests, how to build a statistical test for it, how to estimate the feature-importance and sparsity curves of a real model (the two quantities the toy says matter most), whether scaling removes superposition or leaves it a constant or growing fraction, and what the most principled definition of superposition even is. The privileged-basis toy is explicitly criticised by its own authors — the model routes around its hidden ReLU whenever it can, by pushing biases positive or simulating a bias by averaging features, so it only uses the nonlinearity when forced. The absolute-value experiment cannot be used to compare losses across sparsity levels, because changing sparsity changes the task itself. The compressed-sensing lower bound they prove allows superposition to grow dramatically as sparsity rises, which they flag as probably an artefact of the proof technique. They also note the derived bound requires the projection to satisfy a restricted isometry property, which a learned, importance-aware matrix does not generally respect.

## What we take from it

- **A framing.** "Solving superposition" is defined as the ability to enumerate features, and the paper is careful to say that enumeration buys universal statements ("no circuit of this class exists") rather than merely naming features. For our predictive white-box goal, that is the right target to borrow: an account should support quantification over features, not just description of the ones we happened to probe.
- **A bound to cite.** Their Theorem 1 makes the toy model a compressed-sensing algorithm and inherits the inner-dimension floor proportional to sparsity-count times the log of the feature-to-sparsity ratio. Any overcomplete-basis account of a layer should be reported against a bound of this shape, because it is the only number in this folder so far that says how much compression is *impossible* rather than how much was achieved.
- **A per-feature quantity that is basis-independent.** Feature dimensionality — squared norm over summed squared projections — is the cleanest available statement of "how much of a dimension does this feature get", it sums to the width when the packing is efficient (a commenter in the same document shows this is the matrix leverage score under an isotropic-packing assumption), and it can be computed on any dictionary, including one we fit ourselves, with no ground truth.
- **A warning about the null.** Their controls are analytical because the linear model provably cannot superimpose. That is stronger than a randomised baseline and is worth imitating where a closed form exists; where it does not, a fitted account without a provable floor is an account whose success cannot be attributed to the mechanism.
- **A precedent on correlated features.** Correlated features forming a local orthogonal basis in isolation is exactly the structure that would fool a dictionary fit into reporting a clean, small feature set while the model as a whole is superimposed.
