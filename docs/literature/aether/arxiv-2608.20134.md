## What they wanted, and why

Most interpretability work reads a finished model. If a white-box account is supposed to *predict* a network, the larger question is whether the units it names are stable objects over the network's own life — features that appear, persist, and stay put can be a coordinate system; features that are invented and discarded by the optimiser cannot. The authors want a feature-level readout of training itself, along two axes at once: depth (which layer) and time (which checkpoint), so that "this feature lives in layer 8" becomes a claim with a trajectory rather than a snapshot. Their stated complaint against existing instruments is precise: representation-similarity scalars such as CKA say whether two activation spaces are similar, not which features they share, and linear probes only track attributes someone already annotated. So their goal is a middle level — per-feature identity, tracked from raw activations through a sparse dictionary, without a human vocabulary. The payoff they are aiming at is a description of how visual features come into being and how the network reorganises where they are represented as it learns.

## What they studied

One model: ViT-Tiny (12 blocks, patch 16, embedding 192, 12 attention heads), supervised training from scratch on two ImageNet-derived datasets — ImageNet-1k (300 epochs, 72.55% accuracy; SAEs fitted on a 100k-image class-balanced subsample, evaluated on the 50k validation set) and Mixed 10, a balanced superclass variant (same recipe, batch 512, 86.37% accuracy; 77,060 train / 2,700 test). SAEs are trained on the **CLS token only** at **every layer × every checkpoint** (checkpoints sampled densely in [0,100], then every 10 epochs to 280, then [281,300)). Dictionary: BatchTopK, K = 32, expansion 5 (192 → 960 latents), 150 epochs, l2-normalised inputs, decoder renormalised to unit norm each step, no dead-feature resampling. Unit of analysis: one SAE latent's **activation profile over a dataset** — an n-length vector of per-image activations — compared across (layer, epoch) points. Feature kinds were partly automated: class-specific if the runner-up ImageNet class has under half the top class's normalised activation, superclass-specific if a group of 2–10 top classes is close together, drops sharply after, and shares an immediate WordNet hypernym, else manually labelled (inter-class / not understandable).

## How they did it

Step by step. (1) Extract CLS representations for the whole analysis set at every sampled checkpoint and layer, forming a matrix X per (t, ℓ). (2) Fit an independent SAE at each of those points — this is the expensive part, ≈192 H200-hours for the ImageNet-1k run. (3) Score similarity between a reference feature and any other (t, ℓ) two ways and take the **maximum of the two**: a *fixed/transferred* score, meaning the reference SAE's encoder row is applied unchanged to the other checkpoint's activations and the resulting profile is Spearman-correlated with the reference profile (tests whether one dictionary stays a valid readout), and an *independent* score, meaning the cross-SAE matrix of profile correlations over all latent pairs is taken row-wise max (tests whether a fresh SAE rediscovers the feature). The authors are explicit about why the max: the fixed score is indifferent to a reparameterised feature and the independent score is at the mercy of both SAEs succeeding, so neither alone is identity. (4) Threshold each feature at half its own second-highest similarity (feature-relative, so weak-scale features are still trackable), turn the surviving layer scores into a softmax-over-layers distribution per epoch, and from that distribution compute the paper's whole vocabulary: layer centre of mass, layer spread, active lifetime between a sustained emergence (3 consecutive sampled checkpoints above threshold) and a sustained, final disappearance (5 consecutive below), localisation as one minus normalised entropy, stability as the time-weighted share of its active life spent within ±1 layer of its anchor layer, and signed drift as the change in centre of mass between the first and last 5% of its active epochs. Features are then labelled spread (localisation < 0.5), migratory (|drift| > 1 layer), or stable (|drift| < 0.5 and ≥ 70% occupancy within one anchor layer), with the in-between band deliberately unclassified. Controls are thin but honest: an A.6 comparison against linear CKA on the same grid to show that CKA reproduces the trend without the per-feature resolution, and a per-feature sanity check in A.3 where a tracked feature from epoch 1, layer 8 is shown next to its best-matching later features and their top-activating images, which do look like the same tube-like object.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **feature-level trajectory on a checkpoint × layer grid** — the object is a heatmap per feature (or averaged over features), and its summary statistics: when it emerges, how long it lives, where it sits, whether it moves. The claim form is descriptive-dynamic rather than mechanistic: no circuit, no causal test, no intervention anywhere in the paper. It is explicitly partial and pinned to one dictionary class — the authors call linearly decodable SAE latents *candidate* features and note features need not be linearly decodable throughout the network — and it can predict only in the weak sense that a feature observed in an early checkpoint is expected to be findable in a specific layer band later. What it does add that a static entry lacks is a **null**: it quantifies how much of the "feature" you report at one checkpoint is a property of the optimiser's current coordinates rather than of the task, by measuring what fraction of features move by more than a layer.

## How they judged success

No accuracy-style headline metric — the deliverables are rate maps over the grid: percent of features classified deeper-migratory, earlier-migratory, stable, widely spread; mean signed drift in layer units; mean active lifetime in epochs; layer centre-of-mass trajectories with standard deviations; and per-checkpoint feature-type counts (class / superclass / other, Table 1). Thresholds are all pre-declared constants in Table 2 (k_on = 3, k_off = 5, σ_max = 4 layers, f_se = 0.05, δ = 1, migration > 1 layer, spread λ < 0.5, stability ≥ 0.7, stable drift < 0.5). The comparison baseline is linear CKA over the same grid (A.6), used qualitatively. There is a live sanity check in A.5 — SAE test loss rises in deeper layers and later training — and a feature-count-per-checkpoint diagnostic (A.1) so "dead latents" are visible.

## What they found

Migration is mostly **towards earlier layers** and mostly **early in training**: deeper-layer migration peaks at about 4% of features, while earlier-layer migration dominates the first part of training; mean signed drift is largest in the earliest layers and earliest checkpoints, reaching roughly −2.5 layers in the strongest region, and deeper layers / later training are on average least affected. Stabilisation is graded and depth-dependent: deeper layers stabilise earliest and most strongly, earlier layers stabilise later and less completely; by the end of training, combined with the spread map, there is little migration left anywhere. Active lifetime is not the same quantity as stability — some early layers and early checkpoints hold features with very long lifetimes that are nonetheless *not* stable, while deeper and intermediate layers in early training hold features with surprisingly short lifetimes. Widely spread features occur throughout training, concentrated at the beginning and in early layers. Feature type drifts towards specificity: class-specific counts rise monotonically across reference epochs 1 → 100 → 200 → 300 (70, 259, 401, 461 of 825, 959, 1308, 1406 active features, with superclass 0, 22, 35, 42), but the authors report **no correlation between feature type and migration behaviour**. Label entropy differs sharply by dataset — ImageNet-1k high-entropy (many-label) features sit in higher layers, Mixed 10 high-entropy features in lower layers — which they attribute to the expansion coefficient being too small (960 latents for 1,000 classes) rather than to a property of training. A single feature tracked from epoch 1, layer 8 shows the fine-grained behaviour the aggregate hides: successive best matches drift in layer while staying recognisably the same pattern.

## Limits and open problems they name

One model (ViT-Tiny), one architecture family, supervised training only; no self-supervised (MAE) or fine-tuning regime. **One SAE per checkpoint ("a combination of two SAEs per checkpoint" is the most A.4 allows), no multi-seed experiments** — the authors say stronger claims would need them. Feature classification is partly subjective and the evolution-metric definitions "may need further grounding". Restricted to linearly decodable features. Spearman over activation profiles is sensitive to ties among zero activations, so Top-K overlap should be tried instead. The SAE pipeline gives no guarantee it finds the same feature at two points, which is exactly the thing being measured. CLS-only: patch-stream evolution is left open, with reasons to expect it differs. Two datasets that differ in label granularity and test size as well as scale, so their comparison cannot isolate dataset size. Encoder's exact SAE configuration (K = 32, expansion 5, 150 epochs, no resampling) is fixed across all 300+ fittings to keep the cost tractable.

Limits I am adding, marked as mine: (i) **the max-over-two-scores rule makes the headline migration rates optimistic in an unquantified direction** — a feature counts as present whenever *either* readout finds it, so a reparameterised or split descendant keeps the parent alive and suppresses the "migration" and "disappearance" it should count; the paper reports the max and never the two components' disagreement rate, which is the single most informative number it could publish. (ii) **"Migration" is measured on a feature defined by its own profile, so it is partly circular**: the profile is computed at a fixed layer's activations, and moving to another layer changes the activation geometry, so a drop in correlation at distance is expected even for a perfectly copied feature; the fixed-score half of the max is exactly the test that would show this, and it is not reported separately. (iii) **No untrained or shuffled-label control grid** — the natural null (a network trained on permuted labels, or a randomly initialised checkpoint series) would say how much of the observed early-layer drift is learning versus the residual stream's normal maturation. (iv) **The class-specificity label is driver-of-the-heatmap, not a measured cause**: features are labelled by the class distribution of images where they fire, so "class-specific" means "selective for these images", which is compatible with a low-level cue that happens to correlate with the class. (v) The trajectory exists for one seed, so whether "deeper layers stabilise earlier" is a law or an artefact of this run is unknown — and given the folder's own record on probe stability across seeds, that is the first replication anyone should run.

## What we take from it

**A fourth stability axis, and the cheapest one to bolt onto existing work.** The folder already separates a feature's firing from its routing and a probe's verdicts from the model's argmaxes; this paper adds *position over training time*. Concretely: if we fit dictionaries at two checkpoints we can already ask the two questions the paper's pair of scores separates — does the old encoder row still rank images the same way (transportability), and does a fresh dictionary rediscover it (identity) — and reporting both, plus their disagreement, is a protocol we can adopt for the cost of one extra correlation matrix.

**The trajectory vocabulary is reusable verbatim** as a scoring scheme for any "feature X at layer L" claim in our own write-ups: emergence, lifetime, localisation, stability, signed drift, with pre-declared cutoffs in units that mean something (layers, epochs). It converts "this direction lives in the middle layers" into a distribution with a spread, which is what a predictor needs before it can be surprised.

**The empirical shape to test first in language models, not to assume:** features migrate *earlier*, deeper layers stabilise *first*, and early-layer features are long-lived but not localised. If that holds in LLMs it argues for reading behaviour off deep, late-stabilising layers; if it inverts, the folder's habit of preferring middle layers in post-trained models is an artefact of reading one checkpoint. The paper's own caveat is the guard on this: their residual-connection explanation means the "earlier-layer" drift may be the representation becoming *less* layer-localised, which they observe directly (average layer width from layer 0 increases over training).

**Two warnings we should carry into our own probing.** Lifetime ≠ stability, so "this feature was present at every checkpoint" says nothing about where it lived. And dictionary capacity sets the semantic granularity you are allowed to find — their class-specific features appear only as the fitting budget (expansion 5 over 192 dims) is spent on 1,000 classes, which is the same denominator problem this folder has met whenever it asked how much of behaviour a dictionary could possibly cover.

**A framing line worth stealing for the survey's own thesis:** the paper positions itself as a feature-space counterpart to weight-space training dynamics — same trajectory, different observable — which is the cleanest statement in the folder of what "reading weights" gains over "reading activations at one checkpoint", and of what it does not: a trajectory is still not a mechanism.
