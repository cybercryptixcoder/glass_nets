- **Key:** arxiv-2606.08365
- **Authors / organisation:** Evan Duan (University of Michigan — inferred from the `umich.edu` correspondence address; no separate affiliation line is printed in the byline)

- **Kind:** paper

- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Dictionary features are routinely used to steer a model by adding a latent's decoder direction to the residual stream, and the appeal of the method is that one feature should give one interpretable effect. In practice the same intervention drifts across contexts and drags along unrelated features, and both failures are only discovered by running the steering sweep per feature across many inputs. The author asks a different question from the rest of this folder: not what a latent means, but whether a latent's *behaviour under intervention* can be forecast from numbers computable before any intervention at all. The larger goal is a cheap screening rule that would let a practitioner pick well-behaved features without paying for a per-feature sweep, and a calibrated statement of how far such a rule transfers between models and dictionary sizes. The framing is honest about ambition: the paper is explicitly a method paper about prediction, not a discovery about mechanism.

## What they studied
Four pretrained model/dictionary settings, each with 300 sampled features: GPT-2-small with a ReLU residual SAE (primary site block 8, collateral measured at block 10), Pythia-70M-deduped with a ReLU SAE (blocks 4 → 5), Gemma-2-2B with a JumpReLU Gemma Scope SAE (layer 12 → 16, width 16k), and Llama-3.1-8B with a TopK Llama Scope residual SAE (layer 16 → 20 at 8×, plus a controlled 32× replacement). No SAE is trained here. The unit of analysis is the single SAE feature, and the outcome is a pair of intervention-behaviour labels rather than an interpretability score: *stability*, the average cosine between a feature's per-context logit-difference vector and that feature's own mean logit-difference across a mixed context set, and *collateral spread*, how many downstream-panel features move by more than a threshold, normalised by the effect's size. Data is Wikitext-103, 2,048 contexts of 48 tokens, with the mixed context set deliberately containing top-activating, random and low-firing contexts.

## How they did it
Every score is computed before steering and then regressed onto a measured label, so the whole paper is a held-out prediction exercise rather than a causal one. Activation-free predictors are grouped into four families that follow from a first-order expansion of the downstream feature activation around the intervention: decoder geometry (pairwise decoder cosine norms and a top-k "crowding" statistic), activation statistics of the feature over the corpus, co-activation structure, and the feature's direct-logit footprint — the logits reached by multiplying the decoder direction through the unembedding, summarised by L2 and L∞. Steering is additive at the final token position only, `h + α·d_f` with α = 1.0 fixed, chosen over clamp-style edits because clamping flips sign between high- and low-activation contexts. Stability and collateral labels are then measured across the context set, and the downstream effect is read through a second, later-layer SAE rather than through logits alone. Seven predictor sets are compared by ridge regression with standardised inputs and five-fold cross-validated Spearman correlation, including two deliberately weak baselines (frequency only, activation magnitude only) and a "no-magnitude" set that excludes activation magnitude so that any gain cannot be a restatement of effect size. A residualisation arm refits the stability label on effect L2, intervention value and natural activation and repeats the regression on the residual. The screening evaluation is the one that matters for practice: a model is trained on part of the measured features, ranks an unseen pool by a composite cleanliness score, and the predicted-clean versus predicted-messy groups are steered on fresh Wikitext validation contexts and compared with Mann–Whitney tests, with groups matched on predicted effect size first.

## What counts as an explanation here
A regression that ranks features, and nothing more. There is no claim that any predictor is the mechanism — the paper is careful to say the local-linear expansion is "motivational only" and that the experiments decide which signals actually forecast. The intended use is compensatory rather than interpretive: a screening score that says which latents are likely to steer cleanly and which to avoid, usable where no labelled probing benchmark exists. What the paper offers in place of mechanism is a distinction it names explicitly: *method-level transfer*, meaning the procedure of fitting cheap predictors and screening on them, versus *mechanism-level transfer*, meaning one predictor signature holding across settings. It argues, with evidence, for the first and against the second.

## How they judged success
Two separate tests, both quantitative. Prediction: cross-validated Spearman between predicted and measured label over 300 features, reported as the gain of the no-magnitude set over the frequency and activation-magnitude baselines across eight primary targets, plus per-target scores and per-predictor univariate correlations with p-values. Robustness: the same regression on labels residualised against effect magnitude, intervention value and natural activation. Practice: held-out screening, where the contrast between predicted-clean and predicted-messy groups is graded by a rule fixed in advance — strong if significant on both axes, partial if on exactly one, weak if neither — with multiple-comparison correction (BH and stricter Holm) and an effect-size balance check that verifies the two groups do not differ in realised effect L2. A controlled Llama width swap (32× to 128× SAEs, everything else held fixed) prices dictionary dependence.

## What they found
Cross-context side effects are partly foreseeable from pre-intervention statistics, and the foreseeability is uneven. The no-magnitude set beat both baselines in all four settings, with mean gains over the frequency baseline of +0.390 (GPT-2), +0.421 (Pythia-70M), +0.451 (Llama-3.1-8B) and only +0.193 (Gemma-2-2B), and gains over the activation-magnitude baseline of +0.249, +0.299, +0.386 and +0.182. On individual targets the no-magnitude set reached cross-validated Spearman of 0.554 / 0.590 / 0.396 / 0.447 for signed stability and 0.556 / 0.613 / 0.381 / 0.631 for downstream collateral count, in the same model order; in Gemma the absolute-cosine stability target was not predictable at all (0.028, with the geometry and crowding sets at or below zero). The leading univariate predictor is different in every setting — decoder crowding in GPT-2 (ρ = 0.466 on collateral), direct-logit L2 in Pythia and Llama for collateral (0.391 and 0.464) with decoder crowding or decoder norm leading stability (−0.360 and 0.366), encoder norm leading signed stability in Gemma (0.350). The residualisation arm retained signal in GPT-2, Pythia and Llama (Llama 0.366 on residualised signed stability and 0.290 on residualised absolute stability) but not in Gemma. Held-out screening worked but on a different axis per model: strong on both axes for GPT-2 (stability Δ = +0.071, p ≈ 4e-6; collateral per effect Δ = −0.523, p = 0.020, with no significant effect-size difference), stability-only for Pythia (Δ = +0.076, p = 1e-8 under the main selector), collateral-only for Llama (0.209 vs 0.281 collateral per effect, p = 1.3e-4, effect L2 balanced), and partial-for-collateral-only for Gemma (p = 0.0018, with a small effect-size imbalance flagged by the paper's own balance check). Under a 4× wider Llama dictionary the predictive gain persisted (+0.420 over frequency, +0.266 over magnitude) but screening degraded to a stability-only effect under a different selector, and the paper reports that as a weakening rather than a transfer.

## Limits and open problems they name
Held-out screening groups are small (25-32 features per group, 256-512 fresh contexts). The primary cross-model experiments use a single intervention strength (α = 1.0) with a sensitivity sweep only for GPT-2, where the collateral advantage attenuates at α = 2.0. Collateral is measured through a downstream SAE proxy, so dictionary error is inside the label. Model and dictionary family are not fully crossed, so the paper explicitly declines to attribute differences to architecture versus SAE family. Four settings is not enough to make its dictionary-level diagnostics (crowding, coactivation count) a causal explanation, and the author says so. Future work named: vary width and family within a fixed base model, enlarge screening pools, and connect the screen to an end-to-end steering pipeline.

## What we take from it
A method for our predictive white-box goal, and a warning about how to state it. The method: a behaviour can be predicted from frozen statistics of a component without any intervention, and the honest way to price that prediction is a same-shaped baseline (frequency, magnitude) plus a residualisation against the confound the prediction could be secretly reproducing. The warning, which is the sharper of the two: the paper separates *method-level* from *mechanism-level* transfer and shows that its own procedure transfers while every one of its predictor signatures fails to — the dominant predictor and even the axis on which screening pays off move between four settings. For us this is a template for how to report a predictor whose fitting works: report the per-setting ranking of predictors, not one aggregated claim, and never promote the winning predictor in one model into the account of the phenomenon. Two smaller transfers: the composite cleanliness score (z-scored stability minus z-scored log collateral) is the sort of scalar an account needs when it must rank candidates rather than judge one; and steering at the final token with additive edits is a deliberately narrow intervention, which is exactly why the collateral label needs a second, downstream SAE rather than the same site.
