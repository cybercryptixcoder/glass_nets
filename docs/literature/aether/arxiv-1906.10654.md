# ReachNN: Reachability Analysis of Neural-Network Controlled Systems

- **Key:** arxiv-1906.10654
- **Authors / organisation:** Chao Huang, Jiameng Fan, Wenchao Li, Xin Chen, Qi Zhu; Northwestern University, Boston University, University of Dayton
- **Date:** 2019-06-25 (ACM conference paper, October 2019)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.10654
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Verifying a system whose controller is a neural network requires reasoning about the controller inside the loop, not as a standalone function. The obstacle the authors name is that a reachability engine needs a *tractable model* of the controller's input-output mapping, not merely an interval bound on its outputs: an interval loses the dependence between state variables, and that dependence is exactly what makes each control step's set grow. They want a replacement model of the controller that is cheap to compose with an ODE solver, comes with a certified error box, and — the paper's distinguishing aim — works for networks whose activation functions are not fixed in advance. The larger goal is safety verification of learned controllers, and the narrower, sharper goal is generator-agnosticism: accept any controller that is Lipschitz continuous, including heterogeneous networks that mix activation types in one network.

## What they studied
Six benchmark plants of two to four state variables (a polynomial-ish nonlinear pair, a classic third-order example, a four-dimensional system with a sine coupling, and similar), each with a control step of 0.1–0.5 and a goal set to be entered after a fixed number of steps. Controllers are three- or four-layer feed-forward networks, twenty to a hundred units wide, with ReLU, sigmoid, tanh, and mixed ReLU+tanh / ReLU+sigmoid activations. An illustrating example uses a two-hidden-layer, twenty-unit heterogeneous controller. One extra experiment on an inverted pendulum on a cart compares two controllers of very different estimated Lipschitz constant. The unit of analysis is the controller as a whole, represented as a box-valued polynomial plus an error interval.

## How they did it
They keep the standard flowpipe loop and replace only its controller step. At each control step the state set is enclosed in a box; a Bernstein polynomial of chosen multi-degree is fitted to the controller on that box, in normalised coordinates so the basis lives on the unit cube, and the fitted polynomial is lifted into a Taylor model so that its coefficients carry the state-set's own dependencies forward into the ODE computation. The plant is then integrated with off-the-shelf Flow* over the control interval with the polynomial plus its error box as the input. The work is in the error box, and they give two ways to obtain one. The a-priori route multiplies a per-layer Lipschitz estimate, itself obtained layer by layer from interval propagation of the activation's derivative bound over that layer's input interval (a bound that tightens with the actual interval, not just the weight norm), by the published Bernstein approximation rate for Lipschitz functions, scaled to the box's side lengths. The a-posteriori route partitions the box into a grid, evaluates the network and the polynomial at each cell centre, and takes the largest centre discrepancy plus a Lipschitz term proportional to the cell diagonal; a worst-cell maximum then gives a bound valid on the whole box. The grid is shrunk adaptively to hit a user-set sampling-precision target. A separate experiment retrains the controller with a product-of-spectral-norms penalty — a Lipschitz regulariser — to see whether the same machinery then holds for more steps.

## What counts as an explanation here
A replacement model with a certificate: a polynomial surrogate for the controller plus a certified error box, valid on a stated input box. It is partial by construction and it does predict — the error box is what licenses the claim that every behaviour of the true closed loop is contained in what the surrogate produces. Notably it is a *function* surrogate, not just a set: the dependence between state variables survives into the plant integration, which is exactly the distinction the authors draw against interval methods ("lower-order" versus higher-order sets).

## How they judged success
Two verdicts per benchmark instance: proven to enter the goal set within the step count, or unknown because a flowpipe left the goal region or blew up after a stated number of steps. Wall-clock time is reported beside the verdict. Baselines are an interval-overapproximation variant (in the illustrating example, the only other method extendable to heterogeneous networks), Sherlock (piecewise-polynomial controller model, ReLU only), and Verisig (exact transformation to a hybrid system, sigmoid and tanh only). The Lipschitz experiment compares the same polynomial degree across two controllers. No randomness across seeds is reported for the main table, and the sampling grid is a hyperparameter given per row.

## What they found
On the heterogeneous illustrating example the interval variant's flowpipes grow past the tolerance within the step budget while this method proves the property. Across the table, the method verifies most instances — every activation type including the mixed ones, which the two baselines cannot touch at all — but at a large time cost: on one ReLU instance verification takes 3,184 s against 41 s for Sherlock, and the four-layer hundred-unit instances take thousands of seconds each. It does not always succeed: tanh controllers are unknown on two of the six plants at the chosen degree, and on the largest plants the sigmoid and tanh runs are refuted or unknown while Verisig proves them. The sampling study finds Bernstein fits far better for ReLU than for the smooth activations on the same box (error about a factor of a hundred-million apart in the reported orders of magnitude), which the authors flag rather than explain. The Lipschitz experiment is the most instructive: a controller with an estimated constant in the hundreds loses the bound at the tenth step, while a retrained controller with a constant an order of magnitude or two smaller stays tight, with comparable closed-loop behaviour.

## Limits and open problems they name
The error bound is conservative precisely because the method only requires Lipschitz continuity, and the sampling that compensates for it dominates runtime. The polynomial's number of monomials grows exponentially in input dimension, so high-dimensional state spaces are out of reach as implemented. Smooth activations fit worse than ReLU for reasons they leave to future work. Six plants is a small benchmark, no unsafe instance is reported as correctly refuted in the main table, and retraining the controller to make it verifiable is presented as an observation, not a method.

## What we take from it
Three things. (1) *A surrogate is only as good as its error certificate, and the certificate should be cheap and repeatable everywhere the surrogate is used* — the two-route structure (analytic rate + sampled residual) is directly reusable as a template for any place we propose to replace part of a model with a fitted object: one route from theory that holds by construction, one from measurement that is tighter, both meeting the same interface. (2) *Sampling with a Lipschitz term is a legitimate proof, not a heuristic* — the bound is worst-cell residual plus a term fixed by the cell size, so the grid spacing is what pays for the claim; this is the cleanest example in the folder of measurement that stays sound. (3) *An account of a loop inherits its tightness from the single-step model's sharpness, and it must say which* — and the paper also shows, without making a point of it, that the verifiability of a loop is largely a property of the controller's Lipschitz constant, so an account can be made true-or-false by retraining the thing it describes.
