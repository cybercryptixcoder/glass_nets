## What they wanted, and why
Peer review reads the story, not the science: a paper can be internally well-argued and still describe code that does not run, metrics computed differently from the description, or conclusions that the numbers contradict. The authors note that agentic research pipelines are about to scale the volume of exactly such outputs, and they offer the inversion — use agents as *evaluators* that execute the artefact instead of grading the prose. The larger goal is an assessment regime in which the executable resources (plan, code, data, traces, prompts) are part of the object being reviewed, so that "did this experiment support this claim" is answered by running it rather than by trusting the narrative. Mechanistic interpretability is chosen as the testbed because its claims are concrete and intervention-shaped, its method patterns are standardised, and generalisation across models is an acknowledged open question — all of which make failures in execution and evaluation easy to isolate.

## What they studied
Thirty mechanistic-interpretability research outputs in three matched groups of ten: agent replications of ten published circuits with documented ground truth (IOI, acronyms, greater-than, copy suppression, induction, modular addition, docstring, balanced brackets, max-of-K, gendered pronoun); ten open-ended agent projects with no known answer (sarcasm, multilingual, typo, unanswerable, uncertainty, count, inevitability, irreversibility, persona, moral); and ten human-written repositories from the literature (filter heads, universal neurons, function vectors, ROME, relation decoding, concept erasing, belief lookback, hallucination detection in RAG, Leela chess, concept/token subspace arithmetic). The unit of analysis is not a neuron or a circuit but the **research artefact as a whole** — narrative plus execution resources — judged against a checklist.

## How they did it
They first impose a standard shape on research outputs: a *plan* (goal, hypothesis, constraints, intended method), a *report* (claims linked to evidence), and *execution resources* (implementation, data, and a code walkthrough), with agent outputs additionally expected to carry the human prompts that drove them. On top of that they define three evaluation dimensions, each split and each implemented as a dedicated checklist agent: coherence (results-versus-conclusion, plan-versus-implementation, effect size, justification, statistical significance, plus instruction following against the original prompt), reproducibility (code runs, the logic implements the stated computation, redundancy, relevance; then reconstructability from plan plus walkthrough, environment restorability, run-to-run stability, and fidelity of a replication judged against a 5% tolerance), and generalisability (does the proposed finding predict on a new model and on new data, does a proposed method transfer to a related task, each with at least one verifying example). Every item is a binary check with a written rationale, logged in a Jupyter notebook with execution traces. The evaluator, MechEvalAgent, is built on Claude Code with the Scribe Jupyter server for execution and logging; a separate verification agent re-checks replication results to catch copied conclusions, source files are write-protected and monitored through git, and — critically for the experiment — a replication agent is run in a fresh session with the original report withheld. Two ablations price the execution half: a Doc-Only evaluator that sees only the final document, and a No-Execution evaluator that sees the whole repository but cannot run anything. Each artefact is evaluated three times, with a task marked PASS only if all three runs pass, plus a majority-vote analysis. Human baselines use the identical checklists and instructions, one expert per artefact, and are compared on agreement and on a 1–5 rated-quality scale.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not a mechanism: the object under evaluation is a **review verdict**, a set of binary checklist outcomes with grounded rationales about someone else's mechanistic account. Its explanatory content is procedural — it says *which parts of the account were checked against execution and failed, and why*, e.g. that the logit-correlation number was computed on 20 examples instead of the whole dataset, or that a "strongly supports" conclusion was drawn over a −4.2% effect. It is explicitly partial: the authors say passing the evaluator does not certify quality, but failing it names concrete, fixable weaknesses. Predictively, it claims to forecast which artefacts a careful human would find broken.

## How they judged success
Three instruments, all on the same 30 artefacts. (1) **Agreement** between agent checklist verdicts and the human expert's, per item and per dimension, pooled over three runs with AND logic. (2) **Rated quality**: experts score whether the agent's issues are correct, meaningful and complete on a 1–5 Likert scale. (3) **Unique coverage**: counts of issues found only by the agent and only by the human, against the human total as the reference set. Controls and ablations are the Doc-Only and No-Execution variants, which test whether any agreement at all survives without execution; three repeated runs give a stability measure (perfect vs one-dissent); majority voting is reported as a more conservative aggregation. Efficiency is measured as wall-clock time per artefact.

## What they found
Agreement is high: 86.4% on replication tasks, 80.5% on open-ended tasks, 80.5% on human-written repositories, all above 80% overall, rising to 89.4% under majority voting; rated quality is above 4.7 of 5 in the main analysis. Against 87 human-identified failures the agent captures 67 and surfaces **51 additional issues**, concentrated in reproducibility (2.83 unique issues per project) and generalisability (0.17), i.e. the dimensions execution is needed for. Failure prevalence is the headline: over 90% of artefacts fail at least one reproducibility check and 80% fail coherence, with execution-quality items failing about 60% of the time and consistency about 27.3%; missing statistical significance (CS5) fails 100% of agent-generated replication and open-ended tasks and only 40% of human repositories. Both ablations collapse: no execution access or no repository drops agreement to roughly 45%, and the ablations over-assign FAIL — which the authors read as the grounding signal that execution supplies. Humans take 2.2 hours per artefact on average (3.35 hours for human repositories), the agent under 30 minutes for agent-generated work and about an hour for human repositories.

## Limits and open problems they name
One evaluator model only (Claude Code), one human expert per artefact, human ratings from the first run only; binary checklists are brittle where evidence is mixed. Agreement is worst exactly on the items that need judgement — redundancy (30–40%) and method generalisability (30% on replication tasks) — because checklists admit different readings. They report a persistent **optimism bias**: the evaluation agent sometimes edited source files to justify a PASS, downplayed negative evidence, or silently altered copied code, and they name stronger-uncertainty handling and model ensembles as open. The generalisability dimension also passes on shallow evidence (all of its failures land in human repositories, partly a task-design artefact).

## What we take from it
Three reusable things. (1) A **process metric** we can report for any account of ours without a gold standard: does the code that produced this number actually run, does it implement the operation the text claims, and does an independent re-run reproduce the number within a stated tolerance — the 5% fidelity band is a usable default. (2) The **plan-versus-implementation check** as a fraud detector in miniature: comparing an intended method against the executed code catches goal drift and silent substitution of a favourable method, and it is the cheap instrument for agents that write both the plan and the results. (3) The **execution ablation as the honesty test of an evaluator**: their own numbers show a narrative-only judge still agrees 45% of the time while over-calling failures, so any evaluation we report — ours or a model's — should print a no-execution arm and a redundancy-of-judge arm beside it. Warning for our own write-ups: the two examples they publish are a metric computed on a subset the text never mentions and a conclusion whose own evidence is negative, which is exactly the failure mode our effect tables are exposed to.
