## What they wanted, and why
Personalising a model to one person is usually done either by learning task-specific preferences from pairwise judgements or by pasting the person's profile into the prompt, and the authors argue both are weak at the thing that matters: what someone values is comparatively stable across situations, yet a written profile is not reliably acted on and grows with every answer it carries. They take the position that values have a shared structure across people with individual differences in priority, so a representation of a person's values need not be rebuilt per task. The practical target is a compact user latent inferred once from a handful of survey answers and reused as a cheap intervention in a frozen model. The paper also treats the cost of asking as a first-class design problem: it chooses which questions to ask and decides when to stop, on the grounds that long surveys degrade respondent attention.

## What they studied
Three datasets of increasing realism. A synthetic food-preference set built from Bradley–Terry draws with a two-dimensional taste vector, where noise caps achievable accuracy at 0.666. The World Values Survey wave 7 (US and UK, 5,652 respondents, roughly 130 of 226 items each) rewritten as first-person binary statements. PrimeX (858 US respondents, 48 binary items on medicine, science, risk and abstract world beliefs), used as a transfer test with a new population and item bank. Unit of analysis is the individual respondent: a 32-dimensional latent, the hypernetwork-generated steering vector or soft prefix derived from it, and the held-out answer or generated text.

## How they did it
A variational information-bottleneck encoder over sentence embeddings of the respondent's chosen statements produces a diagonal Gaussian posterior over a 32-dimensional latent; the decoder scores candidate responses (Bradley–Terry for two options, Plackett–Luce for more) with the latent injected by feature-wise linear modulation, and the model is fitted on held-out own-answers with a KL term plus a swap-guided regulariser whose mirror users have reversed answers and are pushed to opposite posterior means. At inference the encoder's mean is refined for 100 gradient steps on the user's observed answers with a pull back toward the encoder estimate, alternating with decoder updates. A hypernetwork then maps the posterior mean to an intervention in a frozen Llama-3-8B-Instruct: either a single unit-norm steering vector at layer 10 added at every token position (trained by cross-entropy over the answer letters, gradients passing back through the frozen model), or a 16-token soft prefix distilled from a value-model-selected target response and then refined with personalised DPO against length-matched opposing explanations. PrimeX freezes the WVS-trained encoder and shrinks the transferred latent toward the training mean by a calibration-fold factor. Elicitation uses expected predictive information gain: 64 posterior samples and per-item logistic regressions rank unasked questions, the posterior is updated in one encoder pass per answer, and elicitation stops when the best remaining score falls below a fraction of the respondent's first-step score. Controls worth naming: a shuffled-user condition (another respondent's latent) alongside the population mean, a random-target control for the distilled prefixes, contested-item subsets split at train-label entropy thresholds, and person-clustered bootstraps over identical rows for every arm.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not an explanation of the model. The artefact is a low-dimensional latent of a *person* plus a hypernetwork that turns it into one direction in the residual stream, and the interpretability claim is the secondary one: the leading principal direction of the per-user steering shift is said to be a nameable value axis. It is explicitly partial and user-specific — the directions carry no imposed meaning by construction, and the authors check the axis against outside questionnaires rather than against anything internal.

## How they judged success
Held-out answer accuracy, and two ways of crediting personalisation: gain over the population representation (what knowing this person adds beyond the population) and gain over another respondent's representation (whether conditioning on anybody at all would have done). Contested subsets, 95% person-clustered bootstrap intervals, paired scoring on identical rows. Free text judged by a model from a different family, validated against GPT-4o agreement and against real respondent explanations with an abstention rate reported. Percent-of-full-history gain as the elicitation metric, compared against a standard item-response adaptive test at its textbook stopping threshold and its best threshold.

## What they found
**The latent beats the same evidence in a prompt, but the margin is thin on real data.** On WVS the steered arm reaches 64.87% against 63.31% for the population representation (+1.56, CI 1.21–1.91) and 62.25% for in-context profiles; the in-context arm gains *more* over its own null on contested items (+4.60) than the latent does (+3.55), even though the latent's absolute contested accuracy is higher (55.48 against 55.16). On PrimeX the two are a tie within noise. On synthetic food preference the gap looks large (61.44 against 51.86 in-context) but that comparison is an export of the same latent into a prefix rather than a distinct inference method, and the paper says so.

**Free text is where the representation shows something a prompt does not.** With a cross-family judge, only the distilled-prefix arm has a significant gain over its own user-agnostic control on PrimeX (+1.2 points over 686 unseen respondents); persona prompting recites the user's world beliefs in 77.6% of texts against 0.9% for the prefix, while the prefix commits to a position more often (60.0%) and repeats words less (3.5%). Paired prompting baselines gain more and lose more, netting negative on the majority views where they are supposed to help.

**The steering shifts cluster on one axis, and it is nameable.** The leading component of the per-respondent shift aligns with neither leading answer component separately but cuts across the plane they span; the authors read it as religious traditionalism on WVS and safety-versus-danger on PrimeX, and the steered model places each held-out respondent's own position on it at r = 0.73 (WVS) and 0.59 (PrimeX), including r = 0.73 on a moral-foundations score the value model never saw.

**Elicitation is the strongest result and the most over-sold.** On WVS the stopping rule fires after 8.1 of 131 questions with 93.5–93.6% of the full-history gain, where the item-response baseline recovers at most 34.8% at any budget and recovers 31.2% at the stopping point its own rule picks; on PrimeX the baseline's rule cannot fire at all (median posterior standard error 0.613 against a 0.30 threshold). But one question already carries 43.5% of the full gain before any selection sophistication, and on the synthetic set the baseline's rule works as intended and stops earlier than theirs (10.0 of 20 comparisons at 93.4% of its full-budget gain against 14.7 comparisons at 95.6%) — a difference the authors explicitly refuse to call a win.

## Limits and open problems they name
Elicitation is validated for forced choice only, not for what a user would write. Posterior variance enters question selection and stopping but not the intervention, which is generated from the mean alone, so an uncertain user is steered exactly as hard as a well-measured one; the authors name this and a coherence signal as unused. Steering sometimes hurts: majority-view rows lose 3.7 points on PrimeX forced choice and 2.1 on free text even though the net is positive. Surveys are not behaviour — value–behaviour correlations are modest and level-specific. One base model for all steering results. And a risk they flag without testing: a model steered toward a person's values may reinforce harmful views and may weaken safety behaviour.

## What we take from it
For our goal this is a 1, and the reason is worth writing down. It is a well-controlled personalisation paper that never asks what its direction is made of: the claim that the shift's leading component is a value axis rests on correlation with survey topic blocks and an outside questionnaire, not on projecting the direction onto any internal read-out, and the second component — the one that would tell us whether a 2.4M-parameter network moved one axis or several — is never examined. Its objects are about people, not about the model. The two transfers we do take are procedural. First, **the paired-null design for anything claimed to be "about" a specific input**: population and shuffled arms answer two different questions (is there person information at all, and is it *this* person's), the paper reports both for every cell, and the honest reading of its own table is that the real-data advantage rests on the population arm while the shuffled arm is nearly identical between methods — a reminder that a single "personalised vs baseline" number would have concealed which of the two nulls did the work. Second, **a stopping rule that is compared against a *baseline's own best operating point*, not its default**: the 94%-of-gain-at-6%-of-questions headline is stated against the baseline's textbook threshold, and the paper prints the baseline's best threshold (42.2%) and its failure mode on a second dataset in the same table, which is the difference between a fair comparison and a favourable one. Third, on the read-out side, the r = 0.73 placement of unseen respondents is a **behavioural** correlate of an inferred state, obtained by projecting the steered model's own predictions onto an axis — a causal check on an inference, and the one instrument here we could borrow if we ever want to show that an inferred latent does what its name says.
