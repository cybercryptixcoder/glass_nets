## What they wanted, and why

LLM-as-a-judge evaluation is accurate but expensive and awkward: the judge emits free text that has to be parsed back into a category, and the parsing step is where schema failures and near-miss judgements enter. The authors build on their own earlier metric ParaPLUIE, which reads the model's probability of a one-token "Yes" against a one-token "No" and never generates, and ask a narrower question: does tailoring the prompt to the evaluation task — a paraphrase question, a network-policy-equivalence question, a revision-instruction question — make that read-out better aligned with human ratings, and does it stay cheaper than a generating judge? The larger goal is an evaluation instrument whose scale is intrinsic (zero is the decision boundary) rather than fitted per corpus, so that metrics and judging models can be swapped without recalibration. For us the paper is not about interpretability at all, but it is a clean, fully worked instance of a general claim we rely on constantly: **a two-way preference read off the next-token distribution is a usable dependent variable, and its evidence is agreement with humans plus a cheap stability check.**

## What they studied

Two perplexity models, Phi-4 14B and Llama 3 70B, chosen as the judge; Appendix H adds Llama 3 8B, Mistral 7B and GPT-4o / GPT-4o mini as judges, and Table 10 adds SmolLM2 135M / 360M / 1.7B as perplexity models. Three tasks: French paraphrase classification (1,914 pairs, 33.6% positive, built from the Tytgat et al. expert-annotated paronym/synonym transformations of 355 source sentences); network-intent-language translation acceptability (Mushra scores of 436 translation triplets binarised at "Good"/"Excellent", ~60% positive); and paragraph-level scientific revision quality (ParaReval test split, 258 paragraph pairs × 2 instructions = 516 instances, human preference 44% A / 41% B / 15% tie). The unit of analysis is **one prompt ending in a one-token answer**, scoring a pair of strings by the log-likelihood ratio of Yes against No.

## How they did it

The score is the log odds of the affirmative over the negative completion of a prompt that contains the pair (Appendix A): the authors show with Bayes' rule that this equals a difference of mean log-perplexities over the whole prompt-plus-answer, then that under a log-perplexity loss it reduces to a difference of loss values each multiplied by the prompt-plus-one-token length. This is a log-likelihood-ratio between two hypothesised *strings*, not an extra probe or a trained head — the score comes out of a single forward pass because both candidate tokens are entries of the same vocabulary distribution. That last point is their efficiency argument in one line: one decoder pass suffices however many candidate answers are compared, while a generating judge needs at least the answer token plus end-of-sequence. Four prompt variants are compared: the original Para-PLUIE template, a French translation of it (Fr-PLUIE), a network-policy-equivalence question (Net-PLUIE) and a revision-instruction question with the gold reference as a one-shot example (Rev-PLUIE); few-shot examples are drawn from outside the evaluation data. Classification is scored twice per metric — once at the **natural threshold of zero** and once at the F1-maximising threshold, so the zero point is itself tested rather than assumed — and preference is scored by pairwise accuracy with tie calibration, Cramér's V and Cohen's κ, against BLEU, METEOR, Levenshtein, BERTScore, Modern BERTScore, an output-based yes/no judge, a pairwise-choice judge and a five-point Likert judge. Runtime is measured per task per method with recorded GPU counts.

## What counts as an explanation here

A calibrated scalar: one number per pair, positive meaning "the affirmative answer is more probable", with the claim that zero is the correct cut and that human agreement is above the similarity baselines and comparable to a generating judge. It is deliberately partial — a single global direction of preference on one question — and it predicts only the categorical or pairwise label it was built to emit. The interesting part for us is that "the model's read-out" here is nothing but the logit difference of two tokens, so the paper is a demonstration that a two-token contrast carries enough structure to support an evaluative claim.

## How they judged success

Classification: accuracy, recall, precision and F1 at both the natural and the calibrated threshold, with reported confidence intervals under 1e-3 (which is a statement about the sampling variance of n≈1.9k pairs, not about run-to-run variance). Preference: pairwise accuracy with tie calibration, Cramér's V and Cohen's κ. Efficiency: wall-clock minutes per task, with GPU counts, against the three generating judges. Robustness checks: threshold sweeps for every metric (Appendix G), non-paraphrase score distributions shown to lie *above* paraphrase ones for Modern BERTScore (mean 0.95 across pairs), a French-prompt control for the LLM judges (Table 9), and — the sharpest one — a set of two implications a "same meaning" predicate should satisfy, tested over 430 Nile–natural-language triplets, using the score in both argument orders because the metric is not symmetric.

## What they found

Their own summary is "personalised prompts correlate better with humans at up to 7.9× the speed". Concretely, on French paraphrase at the natural threshold (0) Fr-PLUIE scores 0.74 accuracy with F1 0.58 on Phi-4 and 0.70 / 0.58 on Llama, against 0.74 / 0.56 and 0.71 / 0.52 for the output-based yes/no judge and 0.84 / 0.49 for Modern BERTScore — a baseline whose recall at that threshold is 0.33, i.e. it labels nearly everything positive, and the authors say so. On network intent, everything sensible lands at 0.80–0.81 accuracy and ~0.85 F1, and the best cell is the *original* template at the calibrated threshold, not the personalised one. On revision preference the ordering flips back: Rev-PLUIE reaches 0.61 pairwise accuracy / V 0.61 / κ 0.31 (Phi-4) or 0.62 / 0.34 (Llama, with gold), against 0.53–0.60 for every generating judge and 0.45 or below for the n-gram and embedding metrics; across all six judges in Appendix H, Rev-PLUIE with Llama 3 70B or Phi-4 is the best or second-best cell in every configuration. Runtime (Table 3, Phi-4 and Llama 3 70B as judges): on scientific revision the Phi-4 generating judges take 40 min (41 with the reference) against 9 min (13 with reference) for *-PLUIE, and the Llama judges take 124 min (127) against 33 min (55) — so on the Llama row *-PLUIE is ~3.8× faster, not 7.9×, which is the best cell anywhere in the table and not the median. The implication tests are the part I trust most: of 430 triplets, **56 fail the first condition and 7 fail the second** — i.e. roughly one triplet in eight has two natural-language statements the metric calls equivalent but which are not both aligned to the same formal intent. The authors read this as a bound on the metric's use as an alignment function rather than as a disqualification, and give example rows.

## Limits and open problems they name

Perplexity models were not fine-tuned (fine-tuning "could potentially enhance" accuracy and task sensitivity, untested); 70B is the ceiling of what they tried; most experiments are English with French as the second language, so morphologically rich languages are open; only Yes/No-formulated questions were considered, with sentiment, topic classification and QA named as extensions; and the one-token restriction could be lifted, which they discuss but do not evaluate. Ethical section adds that Phi-4 14B is competitive with Llama 3 70B, so the cheap judge is defensible.

Limits I am adding, marked as mine: (i) **the natural threshold is not shown to be stable across tasks** — it is checked per task by comparing zero against the F1-maximising cut and reporting that "the difference is minimal", but the optimal thresholds in Table 1 span −13.73 to +3.97 across metrics and models, so the claim is that zero is *usable*, not that the scale is calibrated; a scale whose cut moves by seventeen points across tasks is a scale with an unknown offset. (ii) **The multi-token generalisation is flagged by the authors themselves as biased toward the shorter answer** (their equation 4), so the extension they propose is not free. (iii) **There is no random-direction or random-prompt control for the score itself.** A one-token contrast can be informative for reasons unrelated to the question — the token-frequency prior, the position of the answer in the template, the dialogue role marker they explicitly warn must be stripped before scoring. A template with the question replaced by an unrelated one of the same shape would bound that, and it is one forward pass. (iv) **Nothing here separates "the model knows the answer" from "the template elicits a Yes/No bias"**, which is exactly the readability-versus-use distinction this folder keeps finding: the metric is scored against *human* labels, never against a behavioural downstream consequence of the judgement it makes. (v) The 1e-3 confidence intervals are sampling intervals over one dataset per task; no seed or run-to-run replication is reported, so the standing 1/(k+1) bound on what k runs certify applies in full.

## What we take from it

Three transferable things, in descending order of value.

**A template for a cheap dependent variable.** The log-likelihood ratio of two one-token continuations is the cheapest evaluative read-out available — one forward pass, no generation, no parser — and this paper is the worked argument that it is good enough to replace a generating judge at roughly an order of magnitude less compute. Anywhere our own loop needs a label ("is this revision an improvement", "does this prompt express the same policy"), this is the baseline to beat, and the honest comparison is against it rather than against a free-text judge.

**Score the natural cut, then the fitted cut, and report both.** Their Table 1 gives every metric two rows. Two rows is what makes the calibration claim falsifiable: if the fitted cut beats zero by a lot, the intrinsic scale is decorative; if it barely moves, the zero point is doing real work. This folder's steering entries almost never do this — they report effects at a chosen dose with the sign of the threshold implied by the metric — and the paired-row habit costs nothing.

**Implication tests as a consistency check on a similarity predicate.** Appendix I is the cleanest small piece of methodology in the paper: state a transitivity-shaped property that any "same meaning" predicate must satisfy, then count violations over the available triples. Fifty-six of 430 is a published, honest failure rate that a judge model would never report about itself, and the design — a formal target language whose meaning really is unambiguous, so the ruling on transitivity is not itself a judgement call — is the part to copy. The related warning: they exploit the score's asymmetry deliberately (two argument orders, one used as the alignment measure) rather than pretending a directional score is symmetric, which is the same discipline our patching entries need when they say an edge "carries" a signal.

And one shortcut worth having written down for the loop itself: the implementation is a small wrapper with swappable template and swappable model, published on a HuggingFace Space and GitLab, so the metric is reproducible without the paper's code path.
