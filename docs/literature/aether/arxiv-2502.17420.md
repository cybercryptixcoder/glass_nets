# The Geometry of Refusal in Large Language Models: Concept Cones and Representational Independence

- **Key:** arxiv-2502.17420
- **Authors / organisation:** Tom Wollschläger, Jannes Elstner, Simon Geisler (now at Google Research), Stephan Günnemann — School of Computation, Information & Technology and Munich Data Science Institute, Technical University of Munich (equal contribution noted on the title page); Vincent Cohen-Addad, Johannes Gasteiger (now at Anthropic) — Google Research
- **Date:** 2025-02-24 (v1); 2026-02-08 (v2)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.17420
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

The folder's refusal entries all inherit one object without ever testing it: a single direction, obtained by differencing the mean activation on harmful prompts from the mean on harmless ones, is treated as *the* refusal mechanism, and jailbreaks are explained as that one scalar being pushed down. The authors ask whether that is a fact about the network or a fact about the extraction procedure, and they answer it on both sides at once — a better extraction method that produces directions with more effect and fewer side effects, and an argument that refusal occupies a multi-dimensional region rather than a line. The larger goal is predictive: if we know the geometry of the region that controls a behaviour, we know what an attacker has to reach and what a defence has to cover. Their stated motivation is defensive robustness, and the paper is explicit (in both signature and impact statement) that the result is also an offensive recipe, because a direction optimised for surgical ablation is by construction a better jailbreak tool than the baseline it replaces.

## What they studied

Ten chat models across three families, at small-to-mid scale: Qwen2.5-Instruct at 1.5B, 3B, 7B and 14B (residual widths from 1,536 to 5,120 are named in the cone section); Gemma 2 IT at 2B and 9B; Llama-3-Instruct at 8B. Training prompts are 1,184 harmful instructions drawn from SALADBench with up to 256 per source (the Multilingual and ToxicChat sources excluded) plus an equal number of harmless instructions from Alpaca, with 128 held-out harmful and 128 held-out harmless prompts for validation and test. The unit of analysis is a **unit vector in one layer's residual stream**, evaluated through the two interventions the field already uses — directional ablation, which projects the stream onto the complement of the direction at every position and every layer, and activation addition/subtraction, which adds a scaled multiple of the vector at a single layer. The paper's own object beyond the vector is a **basis of such vectors spanning a cone**.

## How they did it

The method replaces extraction with optimisation. Instead of computing a difference of means, they initialise a random unit vector and train it with gradient descent through the frozen model, with three losses on the same step: a cross-entropy term that makes ablating the vector cause the model to produce an affirmative answer to a harmful instruction (the target answer being generated once per prompt by ablating the difference-of-means direction for thirty tokens), a cross-entropy term that makes *adding* the vector to a harmless instruction cause a refusal (the refusal target generated the same way via activation addition), and a KL term that, under ablation, keeps the model's distribution on harmless prompts over a short retained response close to the unablated model's. The vector is renormalised each step. The ablation layer is matched to the layer the baseline direction was extracted from, and the addition coefficient is set to the baseline's norm, so the comparison is like-for-like.

The cone extends this by optimising an orthonormal basis and requiring that *all* non-negative combinations of it satisfy the same two properties. Loss is averaged over Monte Carlo samples from the cone (unit vectors sampled uniformly in the non-negative orthant, then mapped) and over the basis vectors themselves, because the boundaries degrade first; a Gram–Schmidt projection follows each step. Sampling is uniform over the unit sphere in the cone rather than over convex combinations of the basis, because normalisation would otherwise bias samples toward the basis vectors. Independence is then attacked directly: they first find a direction *exactly orthogonal* to the baseline by construction, measure how its cosine with the activations changes when the other is ablated, and — having found that orthogonality is not enough — train a new direction that is orthogonal *and* minimises a loss penalising any change in cosine similarity, layer by layer, between the two directions and the activations when the other is ablated; that loss is applied only over the first 90% of layers, because constraining the output end destabilises training. RepInd directions are searched iteratively, each constrained against the baseline and all previously found ones, with five candidate vectors per direction and the best-on-validation kept. Finally, to ask whether these directions are reachable from the input at all, they train GCG adversarial suffixes whose loss includes a term that pushes the activations away from the first RepInd direction.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **geometric characterisation of a behaviour's control region**: a set of directions, the cone they span, and a definition of when two of them are independent under intervention. It is not a circuit, not a feature dictionary and not a description — it is the claim that the subspace in which refusal lives has a shape, plus a formal criterion (representational independence) for whether two directions in it act on the same mechanism. The paper is careful about scope: it claims a cone exists and that independent directions exist, not that the cone is exhaustive, and it explicitly leaves open whether other regions of the model mediate refusal. In predictive terms the object's value is that it predicts *how many* independent handles exist on a behaviour and therefore what a single-direction defence or a single-direction attack is missing.

## How they judged success

Effect: attack success rate under directional ablation on JailbreakBench, scored by the fine-tuned StrongREJECT judge, with replication on StrongREJECT and SORRY-Bench; refusal scores on harmless prompts after activation addition, using the substring heuristic the baseline paper introduced; greedy decoding, 512-token cap. Selectivity: benchmark performance under ablation — TruthfulQA (via the LM Evaluation Harness with each model's chat template) as the primary column, plus ARC-Challenge, GSM8K and MMLU as a side-effect table — with the difference from the unablated model as the reported quantity. Over-refusal: refusal scores on XSTest harmless prompts swept against refusal scores on SORRY-Bench harmful prompts, i.e. a safety-versus-over-refusal curve. Cone effect: 256 vectors sampled per cone and their ASR distribution read as a box plot, so the reported statistic is the *lower bound* of a sample rather than a mean; best-of-N comparisons against temperature sampling of a single direction. Independence: cosine similarity between each direction and the last-token activations across all layers, on and off ablation, on the held-out set, with further-model replication in Appendix F. Compositionality: ASR under simultaneous ablation of the top-k independent directions, against ablating several difference-of-means directions extracted at different layers. Reachability: GCG with and without an independence-targeting loss, judged by the ASR of the resulting suffix.

## What they found

**The optimisation beats the extraction, and the side-effect column is where it shows.** Under directional ablation the optimised direction is competitive with the difference-of-means baseline and, on average, better under activation subtraction even though subtraction was not trained for. On TruthfulQA, ablation with the baseline direction costs roughly twice as much as ablation with the optimised one in every model reported — the baseline's scores sit 3.0 to 8.0 points below the unablated model across Gemma 2 2B/9B, Llama-3 8B and Qwen2.5 1.5B/3B/7B/14B, the optimised direction's sit 1.1 to 5.0 below, and the improvement is largest on Qwen2.5 14B (+4.6 of the 7.5-point gap closed). The authors describe this as cutting the error by 40% on average. The retain loss is the dial: on Qwen2.5 3B it improves benchmark preservation with little ASR cost up to a weight of 4, after which ASR falls sharply, and several settings Pareto-dominate the baseline. On the safety-versus-over-refusal sweep the optimised direction refuses more harmful prompts at the same or lower benign refusal rate than the baseline at every strength tested.

**A single direction is not the shape.** Cones up to dimension five mediate refusal on every model tested, measured as the distribution of ASR over 256 sampled members. Small models degrade faster with dimension — Gemma 2 2B is the reported case where the lower bound collapses at dimension five — and the paper's explanation is a capacity one: 14B's 5,120-wide residual stream against 1.5B's 1,536. Inducing refusal through a cone is much easier than disabling it that way: with activation addition, nearly all dimensions stay effective. Best-of-N sampling from Gemma 2 2B's four-dimensional cone beats best-of-N temperature sampling of the single direction in the low-sample regime and matches it once sampling volume dominates, and the gain plateaus at dimension four, which the authors read as the model not supporting a higher-dimensional cone rather than as an optimisation artefact.

**Orthogonality is not independence.** A direction constructed to be exactly orthogonal to the baseline still moves it: ablating one reduces how much of the other the later layers represent, measured by cosine similarity at the last token, and the effect is reciprocal. The stricter trained criterion produces directions that barely move each other, and several of those still work — ASR above a random-vector control and declining with each additional constrained direction, for every model tried. Their causal claim is separate from their geometric one and rests on additivity: ablating the top-k independent directions lifts ASR monotonically in k for Gemma 2 2B and passes the baseline from four directions on, with diminishing returns, whereas ablating several baseline directions extracted at different layers does *not* improve on one. That contrast is the evidence they marshal for "different mechanisms" rather than "one mechanism sampled differently".

**The independent directions are reachable from the input, not just latent.** GCG suffixes trained additionally to push activations off the first RepInd direction succeed about as often as that direction's own ablation does (roughly a third of prompts), so an input-only attack can reach the same handle the white-box ablation uses. The paper reads this as showing the direction is used by the model rather than being a feature of the activation space nobody visits.

## Limits and open problems they name

Two are admitted: every direction is optimised against the *same* targets, generated by one ablation, so the mechanisms may be less distinct than the independence test suggests — an RL objective with a judge-based reward is offered as the fix — and the existence of cones does not establish that other refusal-mediating regions have been found. What the paper does not discuss is worth carrying beside that. **No confidence intervals or seeds are reported for the ASR comparisons**; the cone results are lower bounds over 256 samples but the headline "cones up to dimension five on all tested models" is a statement about a sample distribution, and the RepInd ASR decay across iterations is attributed to "increased difficulty of the optimisation problem or a limited number of independent mechanisms" without a test between the two. **Attack success rate under ablation is a dual-use measurement**, so a method that closes 40% of the benchmark gap may be closing it toward a *better jailbreak* rather than a better understanding — the paper's selectivity columns are the counterweight and they are real, but the primary effect metric points the adversarial way. And the *positive* direction (refusal that survives) is studied much more weakly than the negative one: for cones, activation addition stays effective at high dimension by the authors' own admission of it being "significantly easier", so what is measured well is how easily refusal can be switched off.

## What we take from it

**A definition to adopt, and a cheap screen to run before any single-direction claim.** Representational independence is the first operational answer in the folder to a question every direction entry raises and none answers: given two directions, do they act on one mechanism or two? The criterion is not "are they orthogonal" — this paper demonstrates the counterexample inside its own construction, an exactly-orthogonal pair whose representations interfere through the network's non-linearities — but "does ablating one change how much of the other the activations carry, at every layer". That is one ablation pass per direction per prompt, measured with cosines rather than with behaviour, and it should be printed wherever the folder reports two probes as separate (`arxiv-2605.14038`'s near-orthogonal cognition and action probes, `arxiv-2507.11878`'s harmfulness and refusal directions at two token positions). The angle between two probe normals is a *linear* independence estimate; this entry supplies the non-linear counterpart and shows why the cheap one is not sufficient.

**The additivity test is the causal half and it is cheaper than a mechanistic story.** Ablating k directions and reading ASR as a curve in k — monotone and passing a baseline that k same-family directions from different layers does not pass — is a strong argument for distinct mechanisms, and it is a dose-response design of exactly the kind the folder already prefers (`arxiv-2606.25092` masking-shared-experts curve). The same shape would price our own multi-probe accounts: if two probes are believed to capture different causes, ablating both should beat ablating either, and if it does not, one of them is a reparameterisation.

**Extraction is a hyperparameter, and the objective should be the thing we care about.** This paper's core move is to stop *measuring* the direction and start *training* it against the two properties we want, with a third term that bounds collateral damage. That generalises directly to our pipeline: a probe direction is normally fit for classification accuracy, which is a proxy for read-ability and no statement at all about write-ability or selectivity. Fitting the direction on the intervention objective and holding out a KL-retain set is the same experiment we would run to test a claim we are trying to make anyway, and the retain-weight sweep with the Pareto frontier is the right way to report it.

Three warnings travel with it. (1) **A side-effect column is mandatory, and the folder's absence of one has been mispriced as a mild omission** — here it is the *entire* advantage of the method, and the baseline direction is shown to lose 3–8 points of TruthfulQA where the optimised one loses 1–5, i.e. "it ablates refusal" was never the interesting claim. (2) **A cone measured by sampled lower bounds is an existence result, not a dimension estimate** — the reported plateau at four and collapse at five are properties of a sampling and optimisation procedure on a small model, and quoting "refusal is 4-dimensional" from this paper would be overreading it. (3) **The dual-use framing is a genuine constraint on how we write**: the paper's improvement is simultaneously a 40% reduction in collateral damage and a more effective jailbreak, so any future Aether work on refusal directions should state which direction of effect a result cuts before it is published, in the same way this paper's impact statement does.
