# FOCUS: Fixed-Confidence Online Causal Learning Using Sequential Adaptive Interventions

- **Key:** arxiv-2609.32165
- **Authors / organisation:** Haijie Xu, Chen Zhang (corresponding author); Department of Industrial Engineering, Tsinghua University, Beijing
- **Date:** 2026-09-26 (v1 only)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.32165
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

The previous entry in this line (`arxiv-2405.11548`) priced the number of *samples* a probe campaign needs to reach a stated confidence, but it paid for that with an assumption that is easy to miss: it starts from an exact, faithful observational distribution and over discrete variables. Here the authors remove that starting grant entirely. Their learner begins with **no** samples of any kind, must decide at every single step whether to observe passively or to intervene on one variable at one value, and must stop on its own once the evidence is sufficient; the output is the graph *and* the edge weights. The motivation is the practical one — clinical trials where stopping too early distorts an effect estimate and running too long wastes patients — so the deliverable is a stopping rule with a guarantee, not just a design. The larger goal is the same as the folder's: an account of a system whose cost is quoted as a draw count at a stated confidence.

## What they studied

Linear-Gaussian structural equation models over p observed variables: a weighted adjacency matrix, independent noise variances, single-node *perfect* interventions that replace one node's equation with a fixed value while leaving every other equation untouched, plus passive observation as its own action. The unit of analysis is the **action**, drawn from a finite set of size 2p+1 — passive observation plus both endpoints of each node's admissible intervention interval (Section 4 shows interior values add nothing). The objects being learned are the DAG support, the signed edge weights subject to a known minimum magnitude, and the noise variances; nothing here is a neural network.

## How they did it

Five moves. (1) **The floor.** Information complexity is defined as the best mixture of actions maximising the worst-case KL separation between the true parameter and any parameter that is either structurally different or has some edge weight displaced by at least r; a change-of-measure argument over adaptive policies, with an expected occupation measure replacing action counts, turns that into a lower bound on expected stopping time for any (epsilon, delta)-correct algorithm. (2) **Endpoint reduction.** Fixing a target, the Gaussian KL between two parameters is a constant in the intervention value plus a nonnegative quadratic in it, so interior values can have their probability mass split to the two endpoints without losing information against any alternative; the uncountable action space collapses to 2p+1 without weakening the bound. (3) **A game instead of a solver.** Because the optimal mixture depends on the unknown parameter, the algorithm plugs in the current joint maximum-likelihood estimate, asks which alternative parameter is hardest to tell from it under the current mixture (**the alternative best response**), and lets that alternative define a per-action information gain; an exponential-weights learner with an adaptive learning rate updates the mixture from those gains. (4) **Turning weights into draws.** The mixture is fractional, so the next action is chosen to keep realised counts close to the accumulated weights, with a floor that forces any action whose count has fallen below the square root of the elapsed rounds — the exact tracking-plus-forcing scheme of the previous entry, with the proof that every action keeps a count growing at least as the square root of time. (5) **Stopping.** A statistic is the smallest total weighted KL from the current estimate to any admissible epsilon-alternative; the algorithm stops once a tail bound on that statistic falls below delta, where the tail bound comes from a concentration inequality for the accumulated KL divergence of a *joint* estimator of graph, weights and variances shared across actions. Estimation, allocation learning and stopping all run online, one fresh sample per round. The appendices replace both optimisation oracles with finite exponential-time procedures: enumeration of topological orders, parent sets and coefficient-sign patterns with a convex quadratic program per local fit, and a two-state dynamic program that carries whether the candidate graph has already diverged from the current estimate.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **stopping rule plus a floor**, as in the previous entry, but the currency has changed twice over. The account is (policy, stopping time, estimator), and the claim attached to it is quantified over both accuracy and confidence simultaneously: with probability at least 1−delta the returned graph is exactly the true DAG *and* every entry of the returned weight matrix is within epsilon of the truth. Between the floor and their upper bound there remains a single multiplicative gap that depends on the instance and not at all on delta, and the authors say so explicitly rather than claiming optimality. Nothing here predicts an internal state of a network; the predictive content is a *rate* — how many online samples buy a stated confidence at a stated accuracy — together with the direction of the trade between the two.

## How they judged success

Three things, precisely: (epsilon, delta)-correctness as a definition — the pair returned must be exactly right in structure and epsilon-close entrywise in weights, jointly, with probability 1−delta uniformly over the bounded parameter class, and the algorithm must terminate with probability one; the expected stopping time normalised by log(1/delta), sandwiched against the instance-dependent lower bound in the limit of small delta; and, experimentally, structural Hamming distance and maximum entrywise edge-weight error plotted against the number of collected samples under an equal sample budget, plus a plot of empirical stopping time against log(1/delta) at five confidence levels. Baselines are three: **RANDOM**, sharing the estimator and action set but choosing actions uniformly (isolating the value of the adaptive allocation), **AIT-DCDI** (active intervention targeting combined with a differentiable interventional discovery learner), and **CBED** (Bayesian experimental design by expected information gain over both target and value). Graphs are Erdős–Rényi over a random topological order at five (p, density) settings — 5/0.3, 6/0.3, 7/0.3, 6/0.4, 6/0.5 — with weights drawn uniformly on a symmetric range excluding a dead zone, noise variances uniform on a narrow interval, one thousand independent repetitions per setting, mean curves with 95% bands. Edge weights of every method's estimated graph are refitted on that method's own samples so the comparison is about allocation rather than about the estimator.

## What they found

Methodologically the two headline claims are the sandwich: the lower bound is a binary-entropy term divided by the information complexity at twice the accuracy radius, their upper bound is one over a constant times the information complexity at the accuracy radius, and the ratio between the two depends on the instance and not on delta — order-optimal in the confidence level, with an admitted constant gap. The second is the concentration inequality itself; the authors point out that the fixed-confidence literature typically validates stopping through likelihood-ratio concentration, whereas their stopping statistic is built from the estimated model's KL divergence from an alternative, which requires a tail bound for a joint estimator that the standard route does not supply. Empirically, on the representative setting the adaptive method lags slightly in the first rounds and then overtakes all three baselines on both structure and weight error; the gap to the random-allocation arm is attributed to the learned non-uniform mixture, the gap to the design baselines to targeting weights as well as structure. The observation the authors flag hardest is a *sequencing* one: structural Hamming distance frequently reaches zero well before the entrywise weight error settles, so a campaign that stopped on structure alone would report a model whose effect sizes are still wrong — which is the reason the accuracy criterion includes weights at all. Their stopping-time plot against log(1/delta) is approximately linear, consistent with the predicted logarithmic dependence. Remaining settings are relegated to an appendix and described only as consistent.

## Limits and open problems they name

The model class is narrow: linear-Gaussian, causally sufficient, no latent confounding, perfect single-node interventions only, known bounded ranges for edge magnitude, error variance and intervention value. The correctness and rate guarantees are stated for the *oracle* versions of the estimator and the best response; the finite implementations are exponential in the number of nodes, and the authors state plainly that restricted parent sets, order-based dynamic programming or approximate continuous search are the practical route at larger sizes but that such replacements no longer inherit the theorems. Extension to nonlinear mechanisms, latent confounding and more general intervention mechanisms is named as future work. Nothing is said about the cost of an action beyond its information content — an intervention and a passive observation are priced identically — so the paper prices samples and confidence but not experiments.

## What we take from it

A **definition to steal**, a **strictly maximal reading rule**, and one warning. The definition: correctness should be stated over a *pair* of things — the account must be structurally right **and** numerically right within a stated tolerance, simultaneously, at the stated confidence — because the paper's own experiment shows the two are reached at different sample counts and the structural one comes first. Any claim of ours that "the account was recovered after N probes" is ambiguous between these two and should be split. Second: the best mixture of probes is not deployable but its *price* is, and the floor-minus-upper-bound sandwich with an admitted constant gap is the honest way to say "our chooser is near the best possible" — a claim of order-optimality in confidence with the instance-dependent constant left visible, rather than a claim that a number matches a number. Third, and the most portable: when the actions differ in which parameters they carry information about, the quantity that tells you whether to keep drawing is *the smallest total KL from the current estimate to any still-admissible competitor*, i.e. the "can anything still beat me" gap, not an error bar on the estimate itself. That is an audit statistic our batteries can compute directly from candidate accounts, as `arxiv-2410.20089` computes it per target and `arxiv-2405.11548` tracks it globally, but here it is the sole basis on which the campaign is allowed to end.
