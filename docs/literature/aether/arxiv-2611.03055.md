## What they wanted, and why
Iterative improvement of an LLM-generated scientific idea usually takes reviewer-style feedback and rewrites the whole idea. The authors argue this conflates two decisions: where the weakness is visible, and how wide a revision would actually help. They name the gap *diagnosis–intervention mismatch* and want to close it with a decision layer that sits between an existing feedback provider and an existing idea generator, choosing the **scope** of revision without replacing either component. The stated motivation is structural: an idea is a problem, a hypothesis and a solution in a dependency chain, so a weakness observed at one link may be caused upstream, and edits propagate. The larger goal is a general feedback-to-revision controller rather than a rule set keyed to one system's internal state.

## What they studied
A generator plus judge loop over scientific ideas: five open benchmarks' worth of tasks reduced to four used here (AI Idea Bench and IdeaBench under a reconstruction protocol, LiveIdeaBench and MLR-Bench under a paper-review protocol), a Qwen3.5-4B generator (with a Llama substitution for transfer), two "ideators" (direct prompting and SciMON, plus CoI-Agent in a transfer arm), and 20 revision iterations at a 2,048-token generation budget. The unit of analysis is **not** a network component but the intervention scope, drawn from a seven-element set of edit masks over the idea's own structure: problem, hypothesis, solution, and the three composites. Plus 112 human-annotated idea–feedback pairs used as the audit sample.

## How they did it
The idea is first normalised into a triple (problem, hypothesis, solution) with the two dependencies made explicit, and feedback is normalised into goal, evidence, preservation requirements and constraints. stage one, *structural attribution*, reads the feedback through six views (each component, each adjacent dependency, the full chain) and emits one record per view with evidence, observed failure, plausible structural explanation, repair requirement, downstream consequence, severity and confidence — records deliberately stop short of naming an edit. stage two, *counterfactual routing*, builds candidate interventions for every mask consistent with the records, each as an ordered plan of add/remove/revise/replace steps with success conditions and dependencies, then produces a cheap *structured rollout* of the successor (applying the plan's actions and copying untouched fields verbatim) rather than generating a real successor. A routing evaluator scores each rollout for projected utility and for risk (unsupported change, infeasibility, dependency breakage, drift, over-complexity, uncertainty); invalid candidates are filtered and the rest ranked by utility minus a weighted risk. Problem-level edits are conditioned on a persistent "problem pool" of grounded alternatives, and a fixed-reference variant forbids problem switching while an open variant allows it — that single switch is the difference between the refinement and evolution settings. stage three, *execution*, compiles the chosen scope, plan, preservation set and dependency set into one generator-facing specification, and the original generator is called once, instructed not to touch anything outside the scope. The mismatch claim is audited separately and much more carefully: for each of 112 annotated pairs the idea, feedback, context, generator and budget are held fixed and **only** the scope is varied over the six masks, giving 672 scope-conditioned successors; annotators label the diagnostic locus blind to those successors, evaluators score the successors blind to policy, a selection split defines the near-optimal scope set and a separate held-out split measures regret. All seven policies are then replayed against that same bank of outcomes, which is what makes their routing comparison a comparison of decisions rather than of generations.

## What counts as an explanation here
A routing rule, not an account of a mechanism: an attribution record per structural view plus a predicted-utility-and-risk score per candidate scope, used to pick one scope. It is predictive in a narrow sense — the structured rollout stands in for a successor that has not been generated, and the regret metrics test whether that stand-in ordered the candidates correctly. Nothing here explains why a model behaves as it does; the object explained is a text's structure.

## How they judged success
Mismatch rate at a tolerance (fraction of instances where the diagnosed locus is outside the near-optimal scope set, for tolerances 0, 1, 2 and 5, with CIs), a stricter "any admissible locus" variant, replication on the held-out split, and held-out regret of following the diagnosis. Routing is scored by target hit, replication error, benefit and harm at tolerance 2, successor utility, gain over the diagnosis-aligned choice, and route regret — all measured against the outcome bank rather than against a fresh generation. Judge reliability is reported as exact agreement, adjacent agreement and Pearson correlation over three judges on 400 sampled ideas. Ablations remove counterfactual routing, dependency closure, upstream candidates, problem-level candidates, and the risk penalty.

## What they found
The mismatch is large and replicates. At tolerance 2 the human-ground-truth diagnosis misses the near-optimal scope in 65.2% of instances [56.2, 74.1] and still misses in 40.2% when any admissible locus is allowed; the held-out split gives 43.8%. Following the diagnosis costs 3.79 utility points on average [2.74, 4.93], and the mismatch holds for model attribution (61.6%) and individual annotators (68.8%, 71.2%) — while two humans agree with each other far better (62.3%, regret interval spanning zero), so the disagreement is with the *choice*, not measurement noise. Routing against the bank separates the policies sharply: the evolution variant hits the near-optimal scope 43.8% of the time, benefits in 65.2% and harms in 2.7%, with the highest successor utility (64.16) and lowest regret (3.93); the refinement variant matches the diagnosis 45.5% of the time — more often than any baseline — yet is *worse* than evolution on benefit (50.0%) and utility (62.52), i.e. fidelity to the diagnosis is not what buys quality. Benchmarks are mixed: PRIME leads all three AI Idea Bench metrics under direct prompting and the MLR-Bench overall under both ideators (7.260, 7.120), while LiveIdeaBench is not uniformly better. Judges agree at 68.7% exact and 78.9% adjacent with r = 0.72. Ablation: removing counterfactual routing is catastrophic (quality 3.463 to 1.960, regret 0.426 to 1.929), removing upstream or problem-level candidates hurts moderately, and removing the risk penalty barely moves anything (3.459, 0.430).

## Limits and open problems they name
No dedicated limitations section is present in the HTML text. What the paper itself concedes in passing: the conclusion describes reference-frame ambiguity and a dynamic problem tree that this version of the method does not contain and does not implement, so the closing paragraph describes a different system than the one evaluated; the rollouts are approximations of the successor, not the successor; gains under ideator substitution are metric-dependent, with flat feedback still winning one metric; the risk term is near-inert on the evaluated benchmark. The annotated audit is small (112 pairs) and drawn from three of the four benchmarks, and the annotation budget supports at most a handful of distinct diagnostic labels, so the scope space and the human labels are coarse in the same way.

## What we take from it
Four things. (1) **The audit design is the reusable part.** Hold everything fixed — model, input, budget, scorer — vary *only* the intervention scope, and score every policy against the same realised outcomes, so policies are compared as decisions rather than as samples. That is exactly the structure we need to ask whether two accounts chose different components because they saw different evidence or because they scored the same evidence differently. (2) **A measured gap between "where the effect is seen" and "where the intervention should go" is the empirical licence for our whole project** — it is the strongest statement in the folder that a localising probe is not a predictor, and it comes with a number and a confidence interval. (3) **Cheap surrogate rollouts can carry a routing decision, provided they are scored against executed outcomes** — the paper's own ablation says comparison across scopes is what matters, not the fidelity of any single surrogate. (4) **A caution about fidelity metrics:** the variant that matched the diagnosis most often was not the best, so "agrees with the localiser" is a trap metric — the same trap as scoring an explanation by its agreement with an attribution rather than by the effect of acting on it.
