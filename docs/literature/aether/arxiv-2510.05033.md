# Causal Abstractions, Categorically Unified

- **Key:** arxiv-2510.05033
- **Authors / organisation:** Markus Englberger, Devendra Singh Dhami; Eindhoven University of Technology, Department of Mathematics and Computer Science
- **Date:** 2025-10-06
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.05033
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

Causal abstraction has been formalised several times and each formalisation covers a different slice of the problem: exact transformations of structural equations, strong and constructive abstractions over Bayesian networks, cluster-graph abstractions under unobserved confounding, and the intervention-based abstraction used in mechanistic interpretability. The authors' complaint is that these are treated as separate results with separate proofs, when most of them are instances of one structure. Their goal is a single definition — an abstraction as a natural transformation between two functors out of freely generated Markov categories — from which the earlier notions can be *derived* rather than re-argued, and which is not restricted to discrete or to deterministic models. A second goal is more interesting for us: they want a formal home for abstractions in which the concepts one can intervene on do not line up with individual variables or with a product of variable domains, which they name explicitly as the situation created by linear representation hypotheses and superposition.

## What they studied

No networks and no data. The objects are causal models: a directed acyclic graph, a freely generated Markov category over that graph, and a functor from it into a Markov category, with ordinary conditional Bayesian networks as the special case where the target is the category of stochastic kernels. The unit of analysis is again a *transformation between two such models*, expressed as a functor that embeds one graph's restricted morphisms into the other's, together with a deterministic natural transformation between the two models' images. Illustrations are small hand-built graphs and domains (a three-node chain; two- and three-node fan-in graphs with real-valued domains; a voting model with a hundred voters and two binary ad variables; a chain collapsed to a single node).

## How they did it

- Set up the category theory: Markov categories with copying and discarding, deterministic morphisms as the ones respecting copying, freely generated Markov categories over a graph, and causal models as functors out of them. Their key observation is that in the *restricted* category — the one in which each generating box appears at most once — the morphisms correspond exactly to the interventional distributions of the model, one morphism per intervention type and no extras. That identification is what makes the rest of the paper possible: "does the abstraction respect interventions" becomes "is the small diagram a sub-diagram of the big one".
- Define a causal abstraction as an embedding functor plus a deterministic natural transformation, and then define *graphical* abstractions of the graph by two concrete operations: delete a node and connect its parents to its children (allowed only when the node is not a confounder, i.e. has at most one child), or merge two nodes by unioning their incoming and outgoing edges. They prove the graph operation and the categorical embedding are the same relation, in both directions.
- Prove that their definition is exactly equivalent to the standard distributional criterion when the target category is stochastic kernels: mapping the low level up and then running the high-level mechanism gives the same interventional distribution as running the low-level mechanism and then mapping up. This is proved as an if-and-only-if, not a one-way embedding, and the proof reuses the diagrams of the earlier literature.
- Relax strictness in two steps to reach the cases interpretability cares about. A *strong* monoidal functor allows an intervention set whose domain is only isomorphic to a product of per-concept domains; a *lax* monoidal functor allows the product of the concepts one wants to intervene on to be larger or smaller than the domain of the variables actually affected. The two worked examples use real-valued domains and subspaces to show what each relaxation buys.
- Propose a second, *reversed* abstraction. From the commuting square for a three-node chain they derive that coarsening a variable's domain preserves the factorisation only if the coarsening either groups values by their effect on the child, or groups them by how the parent affects them. The first case is the standard definition; the second is captured by placing the natural transformation in the opposite direction, with per-variable stochastic right inverses. They show the resulting regularity: the inverse map from a cluster down to its elements is just the low-level distribution conditioned on the cluster, and the coarsening map must be a sufficient statistic for the mechanism's dependence on its clustered parents. The hundred-voter example is where this pays: voters with different baseline biases can be grouped if the group's behaviour is captured by the sum of votes, which the effect-focused definition permits and the standard one does not.
- Re-derive the existing results as corollaries: constructive and strong abstractions are recovered (the constructive case being the one where the map factorises across variables, and strong abstractions appearing as the lax case, with an example given of a strong abstraction that cannot be made constructive, contradicting a conjecture in that literature); the interpretability framework's constructive abstraction is identified with their deterministic abstraction; the cluster-graph results for graphs with unobserved confounders are subsumed, including do-calculus rule validity on the abstracted graph, generalized from partitions to arbitrary graphical abstractions; and the nearest rival categorical framework is argued to be both narrower — unable to express plain node clustering — and unable to recover the earlier notions.

## What counts as an explanation here

A typed transformation between two causal models: an embedding saying which set of fine variables each coarse variable is made of, plus a per-variable map saying which fine values collapse to which coarse values, with the requirement that the square between "coarsen then compute" and "compute then coarsen" commutes for every interventional signature the coarse graph admits. The explanation is deliberately *partial*: it says the coarse model is a quotient of the fine one for the purposes of interventions, and says nothing about which parts of the fine model are unexplained. Its predictive content is the commuting square itself — given the fine model and the map, every coarse interventional distribution is determined; and the graphical criterion tells you, from the graph alone, which coarse graphs could possibly have that property.

## How they judged success

By proof and by worked counter-examples; there is no experiment, no benchmark, no metric and no number reported anywhere in the paper. The success criteria are internal to the theory: each claimed unification is an if-and-only-if theorem or a derivation of a named earlier theorem as a corollary; each claimed distinction is a specific example the other framework cannot express; and the sufficiency-and-necessity claim for the main definition is stated as the paper's central result. The two relaxations are justified by examples in which the strict definition is unsatisfiable by construction rather than by showing that relaxing them changes a downstream number.

## What they found

The central result is that the categorical definition and the distributional definition of a causal abstraction agree exactly, so the categorical machinery adds no strength and loses none — what it adds is a language in which composition and graphs become checkable. From that, abstractions compose automatically (an abstraction of an abstraction is an abstraction), which yields a category whose slice and coslice objects are, respectively, the implementations of a model and its sub-models — a formal reading of "this subnetwork implements this algorithm". The graphical characterisation is the sharpest practical output: an abstract graph is consistent under interventions exactly when it can be obtained by deleting non-confounder nodes and merging nodes, so the set of admissible coarse graphs is decidable from the fine graph alone, and the confounder restriction is not a technicality — the proof shows that deleting a confounder breaks the embedding because the fine model has an interventional morphism with no counterpart in the coarse one. The two-directional result is that domain coarsening is legitimate under two different conditions, one about shared effects on children and one about shared vulnerability to parents, and these are not the same relation; the voter example shows the second allowing a strictly coarser model. Also: the strong-versus-constructive conjecture from the earlier abstraction literature fails once strictness is relaxed, since the lax case produces strong abstractions with no per-variable alignment.

## Limits and open problems they name

The models are acyclic and the main theorems are stated for stochastic kernels, with do-calculus validity proved only in that setting; general Markov categories, cyclic models, and interventions that do not coincide with individual variable domains are named as future work, as is whether the exact-transformation framework fits at all. The lax construction is justified by examples rather than by a criterion for when a lax functor is a legitimate model. The effect-focused abstraction is derived for a chain and defined generally, but its admissibility conditions beyond sufficiency of the coarsening are not characterised graphically the way the forward direction is. Nothing is said about how one would *find* the abstraction, and there is no treatment of approximation — every definition is exact, so the whole framework is silent on the case where an account holds approximately, which is the only case our own accounts are ever in.

## What we take from it

- **The admissibility of a coarse graph is a decidable graph check, and it has one sharp veto.** Their Proposition is exactly what our site-set proposals need: a proposed grouping is admissible for interventions iff the coarse graph is reachable by deleting non-confounder nodes and merging nodes. The veto is the interesting half — a merged-away node that has more than one child cannot be deleted, because the fine model's interventional morphisms for that node have no counterpart up top; in a transformer the analogue is a site that feeds two downstream computations, and the rule says flattening it into a parent-to-child shortcut silently changes which interventions exist. Mechanical use: **before scoring any correspondence between named concepts and site sets, run the delete/merge check on the induced graph and report which nodes were vetoed as confounders.**
- **Two distinct abstraction directions, and we have only ever been testing one.** The forward direction groups values by their shared effect on children; the reversed one groups them by their shared dependence on parents, and the voter example shows this can be strictly coarser. Every probe battery we run asks the forward question (do these sites induce the same downstream behaviour) and never the reversed one (do these sites respond identically to upstream influence). That is a free second axis: **for each account, report both a forward and an effect-focused grouping, and when only the effect-focused one exists, say so rather than reporting a failure.**
- **Superposition is a statement about monoidality, and that makes it a testable structural claim rather than a slogan.** Their reading is precise: if the number of concepts exceeds the dimension, the functor from concepts to activations cannot be strict — the product of the per-concept domains (one direction each, jointly the span) is larger than the domain of the affected block, which is a lax functor, and an SAE is then an attempt to *find* a strict functor into a set-based category that the lax one factorises through. For us that is a diagnostic about a block: **measure whether the span of the claimed concept directions has the dimension the concepts require; when it does not, the account is lax and its interventions on individual concepts are not independent by construction, and any battery that assumes they are is measuring a fiction.**
- **Abstraction is free to compose, so a chain of accounts is a single account — but only because composition here is exact.** This paper's compositionality is the exact case of what run 523's entry made quantitative: there, composition of approximate abstractions needed a distance that is subadditive, and here composition requires nothing because there is no error. Worth keeping the pairing straight in our write-ups: the categorical statement "an abstraction of an abstraction is an abstraction" is not a licence to chain approximations, and the two results are consistent only if the composed claim is stated at the exact level and the approximations are attached separately.
- **A formal framework that recovers everything exactly still cannot say how much.** This is the limit that matters for our paper: the framework unifies the definitions by removing approximation entirely from the definition, and the folder already knows (`arxiv-2103.15758`, `arxiv-2608.15645`) that our accounts are approximate by nature. Read as a division of labour: use this framework to *state* what an account claims (the maps, the graph check, the direction) and the earlier error theories to say *how well* it holds. Neither does both.
