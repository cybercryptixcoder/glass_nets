## What they wanted, and why

Conditional independence testing is load-bearing for structure learning: graphical-model edge selection, constraint-based causal discovery and invariant prediction all reduce to asking whether two variables are independent once a third set is held fixed. The field's practice is to report such tests with significance stars. The authors set out to show that this practice cannot be made universally sound, and then to say what the honest replacement looks like. Their target is a test that keeps its nominal type I error over the *whole* class of null distributions while still having power against something; if that is impossible, the goal becomes a family of tests whose assumptions a practitioner can actually inspect. The larger aim is to move the burden of correctness from an unverifiable claim about the joint distribution to a familiar, checkable claim about regression quality.

## What they studied

No model or network: distributions of a triple of random vectors (X, Y, Z) with the joint law absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure, and tests as measurable functions of an i.i.d. sample of size n. The unit of analysis is the test statistic on the sample. Theory is stated for univariate X and Y; the multivariate extension assumes d_X·d_Y ≥ 3 and aggregates per-pair statistics. Experiments use synthetic data generating processes with Gaussian noise, a Gaussian-kernel RKHS and boosted regression trees (R package `xgboost`, ten-fold cross-validation over `maxdepth`); competing tests are KCI, the residual prediction test and a residual-then-HSIC test.

## How they did it

The hardness result is a coupling argument. Given any alternative Q under which X and Y are dependent, they discretise the variables by truncating their binary expansions, then splice the digits of the discretised X onto the end of the discretised Z so that X becomes recoverable from Z; adding a little noise keeps the new triple absolutely continuous while making X and Y conditionally independent given the new Z, and the splice point is chosen far enough down the expansion that samples from the old and new laws are close. Any bounded rejection region therefore fires on both laws with nearly the same probability, and a region spread over many disjoint embeddings is handled by a finite union of boxes plus a probabilistic argument. For the positive half they regress X on Z and Y on Z, form the per-point product of residuals, and normalise its mean by the sample standard deviation of those products; this generalised covariance measure (GCM) is tested two-sided against a standard normal. They prove the normal limit under a product-of-mean-squared-prediction-errors condition, extend to multivariate X and Y by taking the maximum of the per-pair statistics with a multiplier-bootstrap-style Gaussian approximation for the quantile, and give a worked case where the regressions are kernel ridge with a data-dependent ridge chosen to minimise a penalty bound. Simulations then compare rejection rates at level 0.05 against the three competitors across null and alternative models at sample sizes from 50 to 400, and separately sweep a frequency parameter through a family of null laws to demonstrate the no-free-lunch theorem empirically.

## What counts as an explanation here

An impossibility theorem plus an instrument: the theorem names what can never be certified and the statistic names what can. The explanation of the negative side is a construction — every alternative admits a nearby null law, so no test can be sound; the explanation of the positive side is a decomposition of the sample statistic into a degenerate central-limit term plus leakage terms whose size is controlled by prediction error. It is explicitly partial: validity holds inside a declared subclass of null laws, and power is a separate, weaker claim. It predicts concretely — given a null law and regression error rates one can say whether the normal approximation is licensed, and the kernel-ridge analysis identifies the quantity (the RKHS norm of the conditional means) whose growth breaks it.

## How they judged success

Nullable level: a test has valid level if its size is at most α at sample size n, uniformly asymptotic level if the worst-case size converges under α, pointwise asymptotic level if each null's size converges. Power is measured as the infimum rejection rate over the alternative class. The GCM is scored on (i) proof of a standard-normal limit under explicit prediction-error conditions, with a uniform version over the null class; (ii) empirical type I error in five null models at α = 0.05, with a 100-replicate binomial check (a rejection rate is compatible with size 0.05 at the 0.01 pointwise level when it stays below 0.11); (iii) power after adding a 0.2·X term to each alternative; (iv) the no-free-lunch demonstration, sweeping the oscillation frequency of the conditional mean at n = 100, 1000, 10000 to show a null law exceeding the nominal size in each case. Separation of null from alternative is quantified as a total-variation lower bound of 1/24 and, in an appendix, an exact KL expression.

## What they found

Theorem 2: any test with valid level α for the null of conditional independence over bounded absolutely continuous laws has rejection probability at most α under *every* alternative — so no test has power against anything, at any sample size. Corollary 3 extends this to the absence of uniformly asymptotic level; Remark 4 notes the proof survives when X and Y are discrete, so testing equality of two conditional distributions is equally untestable, while continuity of Z is essential (a discrete Z reduces to a finite collection of unconditional tests). The untestability is not the familiar dense-null phenomenon: Proposition 5 exhibits an alternative at total-variation distance at least 1/24 from every null law, and an appendix shows a bivariate Gaussian alternative at KL divergence ½·log((1+σ²)/σ²) from the null — so the null and alternative are genuinely separated yet the convex hull of the null still covers the alternative, which is what drives the impossibility. On the positive side, the normal limit for the GCM holds when the product of the two regressions' mean squared errors is o(n⁻¹) and the residuals have finite fourth-moment-type control; this is satisfied when each mean squared error is o(n^(−1/2)), and by kernel ridge regression whenever the conditional means lie in the RKHS with uniformly bounded norm and the kernel's eigenvalue tail is small. For multivariate X and Y the maximum statistic is calibrated by a Gaussian approximation that remains valid with d growing relative to n. Empirically, the GCM held its level in all five null models once the sample size was large enough for the conditional mean to be estimable, while the residual-then-HSIC test failed level in the multiplicative-noise model (residuals uncorrelated but dependent) — exactly the failure predicted by their Example 1. On power, all methods except the residual prediction test rejected correctly with growing sample size. The no-free-lunch illustration is deliberate: with a Gaussian kernel and conditional mean proportional to exp(−z²/2)·sin(az), a null law with a = 6 at n = 100, a = 12 at n = 1000 and a = 18 at n = 10000 each produce type I error above 0.05, because the RKHS norm of that family grows like exp(a²) and the uniformity requirement in their own theorem is violated.

## Limits and open problems they name

The theorem needs Z continuous; the discrete case is testable. Validity is only uniform inside a restricted null class, and the GCM's guarantee inherits whatever the chosen regression method cannot do — the authors concede that choosing a good regression is "mathematically an equally impossible problem", merely one where domain knowledge helps. Power theory requires either strong assumptions (Donsker-type conditions) or sample splitting, and they state plainly that splitting is a device for analysis rather than practice because out-of-sample error is more sensitive to the covariate distribution and gives worse type I control. The multivariate moment conditions are technical and the maximum statistic trades power against alternatives spread over many pairs. They close by naming the type I/type II tradeoff inside subclasses of the null as the open question.

## What we take from it

Three things. **A warning with a theorem behind it:** every entry in this folder that reports a probe's residuals as "independent of X given the layer's activations", or that infers a boundary set from such a test, is reporting a statistic that cannot be sound over all plausible generative laws — so a conditioning claim should be an estimated *strength* at a stated sample size and conditioning dimension (which is what `arxiv-1910.12327` and `arxiv-1909.10140` supply), never a star. **A method template:** the decomposition into a central-limit term plus leakage terms whose size is governed by the product of two prediction errors is the cheapest way to license any sample statistic whose null we cannot compute directly, and it generalises past this test — whenever we normalise a residual covariance, the honest statement is "valid provided both regressions beat 1/√n". It also gives a *negative control that is a law rather than a shuffling*: their multiplicative-noise model, where residuals are uncorrelated but dependent, is the shape a shuffled-label null would certify as clean, and it is the exact shape the folder's partial-correlation machinery (`arxiv-2206.05829`) is blind to. **A framing for what a fixed point can mean:** the coupling construction — hide the interesting variable inside the conditioning variable's low-order digits, and no bounded test can tell — is the continuous-domain twin of the folder's recurring degenerate-explanation problem (a read-out that fires everywhere explains nothing, a predicate with full coverage scores at chance), so "the null and the alternative are separated but the null's convex hull covers it" is the precise statement of why a statistic can look sharp in-sample and carry no information.
