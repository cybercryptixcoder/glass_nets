# Monotone Operator Equilibrium Networks

- **Key:** arxiv-2006.08591
- **Authors / organisation:** Ezra Winston; J. Zico Kolter — Carnegie Mellon University, Pittsburgh, United States
- **Date:** 2020-06-15
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.08591
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

Two families of implicitly defined (effectively infinitely deep) models each have half of what is wanted. Deep equilibrium networks match or beat ordinary deep networks on some sequence tasks and are cheap in memory because the backward pass is analytic, but they converge to a solution only after careful initialisation and regularisation, and nothing guarantees that an equilibrium exists at all, so the model's output can be ill-defined. Neural ODEs do guarantee a unique solution, but they train badly and underperform on standard benchmarks. The authors want an implicit architecture that keeps the memory advantage while *proving* that a unique equilibrium exists and publishes an algorithm with a known convergence rate to find it. The tool they import is monotone operator theory: they observe that plain forward iteration in a weight-tied network coincides with forward-backward splitting on a particular pair of operators, which is what lets a whole classical convergence theory be applied to a neural layer.

## What they studied

Weight-tied, input-injected networks of the form z ← σ(Wz + Ux + b), i.e. a single hidden layer applied repeatedly, called MON. The unit of analysis is the linear operator W (dense, convolutional, or block-bidiagonal over several resolutions) plus the activation understood as the proximal operator of some convex function. Small models — 81k to 172k parameters, and two larger CIFAR-10 models at 854k and 1.01M — on MNIST, SVHN and CIFAR-10. The comparison set is the two other implicit-depth families that guarantee existence: Neural ODE and Augmented Neural ODE.

## How they did it

The equivalence is established first: with A(z) = (I−W)z − (Ux+b) and B the subdifferential of a convex f, forward iteration equals forward-backward splitting with step 1, so the fixed point is a zero of A+B. Existence and uniqueness are then bought with a single sufficient condition — that I−W be strongly monotone with constant m, i.e. its symmetric part exceed mI. Proposition 1 shows this condition holds exactly when W can be written as (1−m)I − AᵀA + B − Bᵀ for arbitrary A and B, so the network is re-coordinatised through unconstrained square matrices instead of being projected or penalised. The equilibrium itself is found by operator splitting rather than naive iteration: forward-backward is a damped version of the layer update, Peaceman-Rachford needs the resolvent of the linear part and converges for any positive step. Backpropagation uses the implicit function theorem, giving (I−JW)⁻¹J applied to the input-gradient of the affine map, with J the Clarke generalised Jacobian of the activation; a second theorem rewrites that solve as *another* monotone splitting problem so the backward pass also has a guaranteed linear convergence rate and can reuse the same resolvent. For convolutions the resolvent is made cheap by the discrete Fourier transform: circular convolutions are diagonalised into blocks that are inverted channel-pair by channel-pair, and strided sub-diagonal blocks in the multi-tier model are handled with a Woodbury correction and a perfect-shuffle permutation. Zero-padding is enforced by clipping border units to zero, which is itself a proximal operator, so it stays inside the framework. Training runs Peaceman-Rachford with a loose tolerance of 1e-2, a weight-normalisation rescaling to keep A and B gradients on the same scale, and the splitting step re-tuned during training as the Lipschitz constant of I−W drifts upward.

## What counts as an explanation here

Nothing in the interpretability sense — this is a construction, and what it returns is a guarantee rather than a description. The guarantee is complete and predictive in one specific respect: the parameterisation is proved necessary *and* sufficient for the monotonicity condition, so the feasible set of W is exactly the image of unconstrained A and B, and every point in that set provably has one and only one equilibrium. The accompanying derivative formula is equally exact: the gradient exists for every such W and is shown nonsingular by a similarity argument on J^{1/2}WJ^{1/2}. The object produced is a stable weight space, not an account of what the weights compute.

## How they judged success

Three places. Guarantees are judged by proof: existence and uniqueness from strong monotonicity, convergence of both the forward and backward splitting procedures, and nonsingularity of the backward system. Practical success is judged by test accuracy on three image benchmarks against Neural ODE and Augmented Neural ODE at matched parameter counts, which is the only apples-to-apples comparison available because those are the only other implicit models with an existence guarantee. Efficiency is judged by hours of training on one RTX 2080 Ti and by the average number of function evaluations per minibatch (solver steps or splitting iterations), plus convergence curves of the two splitting methods across step sizes on a trained model.

## What they found

Against implicit models with the same guarantee the gain is large at equal size: on CIFAR-10 the 172k single-convolution MON reaches 74.1% where Augmented Neural ODE reaches 60.6% and Neural ODE 53.7% (the paper reports the latter two from prior work), and on SVHN the same MON reaches 88.8% against 83.5% and 81.0%. On MNIST the fully connected model ties Augmented Neural ODE at 98.2% while the convolutional variants go higher. With augmentation the largest CIFAR-10 models reach 82.5% and 89.7%. Training cost is reported as 1.4, 4.4 and 3.3 hours for MON, Neural ODE and Augmented Neural ODE at roughly 170k parameters over 40 epochs, with 20, 96 and 90 function evaluations per minibatch — but the 1M-parameter model needs 65 epochs and 16 hours, so the advantage shrinks with scale. Forward-backward stops converging above a step near 0.125 while Peaceman-Rachford converges at any positive step; the number of Peaceman-Rachford iterations nonetheless *rises* over training because the Lipschitz constant of I−W increases, which is measurable drift away from the comfortable regime and is the price of the guarantee.

## Limits and open problems they name

The condition is sufficient, not necessary — networks trained normally leave the monotone regime, but there are stable fixed-point models that this parameterisation cannot express, and the paper does not characterise that excluded set. The parameterisation is over-parameterised (A and B are both free square matrices) and the authors note only that a triangular choice would remove the redundancy, not whether it matters. Circular convolution is required for the FFT inversion, with padding bolted on afterwards. Multi-tier parameterisation becomes an implication chain — once W is bidiagonal the off-diagonal B blocks are forced — which is a structural restriction the authors adopt for convenience. The iteration count grows during training and the splitting step has to be retuned; nothing in the construction prevents the operator from drifting toward the loose end of its own guarantee. Empirically the experiments stay far from standard deep-network scale: the honest upper bound on accuracy here is 89.7% on CIFAR-10, and the authors present closing that gap as future work.

## What we take from it

A framing worth stealing: *a fixed point is a zero of a sum of operators*, and once that is true the field's convergence theory transfers wholesale — including the often-quoted fact that ReLU is a proximal operator, so the non-differentiable activation that makes recurrent iteration unstable becomes well-behaved once strong monotonicity is imposed on the linear part. Two warnings matter for a white-box account. First, the one place in the paper where a quantity that describes the network is actually *computed* and *changing* is the Lipschitz constant of I−W, which is observed to grow during training and which forces the solver's step size down; that is a scalar summary of a trained checkpoint that nobody inspected, and it is exactly the kind of running, data-free statistic we would plot rather than treat as a nuisance. Second, and more important for our goal, the construction makes the *operator* the object — I−W, its symmetric part, its resolvent, its Fourier symbol — and never the individual units: the guarantee is about a spectral property of one big matrix, so a MON of this kind makes a whole-network prediction easy and a per-unit or per-feature explanation no easier than before. The parameterisation by AᵀA is also a reminder that any weight re-coordinatisation with a surjectivity proof costs nothing at training time and should be preferred to a penalty, echoing the equilibrium-net line.
