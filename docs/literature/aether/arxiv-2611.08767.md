## What they wanted, and why
Dimensionality-reduction embeddings (PCA, t-SNE, UMAP) are how working scientists look at high-dimensional data, but the maps are black boxes: nothing in the plot says which of the original features put a point where it sits, and the plots are known to be sensitive to preprocessing and hyperparameters. Existing tools that explain embeddings are local surrogates bolted onto the *algorithm* (LIME-style neighbourhood fits, t-SNE gradients, genetic programming), and their answers move with the number of neighbours chosen and other surrogate settings. The authors want feature attributions for any embedding — including a held-out point that was never embedded — without reformulating the DR algorithm and without the neighbourhood-sensitivity of local surrogates. The larger goal is the standard interpretable-ML one applied to unsupervised structure: know which inputs a discovered cluster or trajectory is made of, and be able to flag when a map is lying.

## What they studied
Three settings, all with two-dimensional teachers. MNIST-64, a 64-pixel digit benchmark with ten classes, teacher = t-SNE. Single-cell transcriptomics: an immune-cell subset of a melanoma tumour study (6,912 genes, t-SNE at the authors' default perplexity 30, cell-type labels from the original study) and the Tasic mouse cortex atlas (23,800 cells, 133 reported cell types folded into 18 meta-types, teacher = the t-SNE reproduced by a standard embeddings paper). Unit of analysis is the *original input feature* — a pixel, a gene — scored per embedding coordinate; the thing that gets an explanation is the embedding, not a network's internal state.

## How they did it
The construction is a two-stage detour. First, treat the published embedding as a teacher and train a small neural encoder to reproduce each point's two coordinates from its raw input by least squares on the embedding values alone — no labels, no reconstruction. Second, because the encoder is a differentiable network, hand it to any neural attribution method (they use Integrated Gradients throughout, and name LRP, DeepLIFT and SmoothGrad as equally applicable) coordinate by coordinate, then aggregate each input feature's per-coordinate attributions by L1 norm into a single importance score. Their own instantiation, MEDAL, adds a decoder trained to reconstruct the original input, weighted by a large coefficient on the distillation term, so the encoder is a perfect fit to the teacher's coordinates subject to keeping the input recoverable; the decoder is used as a *diagnostic* (reconstruction error per point) and never as the object attributed, on the argument that attributing the reconstruction conflates what the embedding kept with what the decoder can restore. Baselines on MNIST are three embedding-explainers (BIR, LIMEADE, and t-SNE gradients, the last re-implemented by the authors and abandoned after 14 hours of compute returned near-zero maps everywhere); on the transcriptomics data there is no attribution baseline, only marker-gene annotations from the original studies and the Allen Brain Atlas. Because unsupervised attributions have no ground truth, the MNIST validation is indirect: compare them with *supervised* attributions on the same pixels — MDI from a random forest (96.88% test accuracy) and Integrated Gradients on an MLP (98.96%) — by per-sample Pearson and Spearman correlation.

## What counts as an explanation here
A per-input-feature importance vector for each embedding coordinate, in the pixel/gene alphabet a domain scientist already reads. It is explicitly a *surrogate* explanation: the authors concede that matching an embedding's coordinates does not mean the student uses the same inputs as the teacher, citing the predictive-versus-explanatory fidelity distinction from distillation. So the artifact is partial and local — it says which inputs move a point's position, not why the DR method placed it there — and its predictive claim is limited to: able to score a new point's features without refitting the embedding.

## How they judged success
No self-contained faithfulness metric existed, so success is argued three ways. (1) Agreement with supervised attribution on MNIST, per-sample Pearson and Spearman with standard errors, on train and test splits. (2) Recovery of known biology: whether the top ten attributed genes for a labelled cell type are its canonical markers. (3) Qualitative inspection of attributions on the worst-reconstructed points, taken as evidence the method can surface embedding distortions. There is no ablation of the distillation quality itself beyond asserting near-zero fit, no random-encoder control, and no shuffled-label or random-attribution floor in the tables.

## What they found
On MNIST the distilled-encoder methods track supervised attributions where the three embedding-explainers do not: Spearman correlation against MLP-plus-IG attributions is 0.314 for the plain distilled encoder and 0.307 for MEDAL, against 0.028 for BIR and 0.008 for LIMEADE; against random-forest MDI it is 0.509 and 0.483, against 0.230 and 0.113; the Pearson column points the same way (0.377/0.342 vs −0.010/−0.016). The encoder-only variant beats MEDAL on most of those cells, so the reconstruction objective is not what buys the agreement. Per-class correlations are reported as stable across train and test splits, so the scores are not a memorisation artefact of the fitted points. On the melanoma immune subset, top attributions for B cells land on CD79B and MS4A1, and Natural Killer cells get NKG7 and GNLY even though the map shows no distinct NK region — their reading is that gene-level information survives in a region the picture does not resolve. On the mouse cortex the L6b and Pvalb subclasses return markers consistent with the Allen annotations, and attributions for the astrocyte population, computed by rank-percentile because the normalised scores were too low to rank, contain no consistent marker, which the authors report as a caution rather than a success. The worst-reconstructed digits are ones a 1 is rebuilt as a 2 and placed among the 2s, and the attribution highlights the pixels that belong to 2 and not 1.

## Limits and open problems they name
Surrogate fidelity is not explanatory fidelity, and in DR there is rarely a ground-truth attribution to check against — they name this as the central limitation and offer no test that would close it. They state the method must not be read as causal and does not replace differential-expression analysis. Beyond that, the paper's own evidence base is thin relative to its claims: the quantitative validation is a 64-pixel toy benchmark whose "ground truth" is a supervised model's attributions rather than any property of the embedding, the biology is presented as selected top-ten lists rather than as recall over all annotated markers, and one of the three baselines never produced a usable output.

## What we take from it
Three things, one of them a warning worth more than the result. (1) *A shortcut we can use:* distillation turns an opaque function into a differentiable one we can attribute, and the same trick applies to our black-box network — fit a student to the box's outputs, then read the student. (2) *A warning that we have now met from several directions:* the student matching the teacher's output does not license reading the student's internals as the teacher's mechanism. This paper says so explicitly and then proceeds anyway, and its validation cannot detect the failure, because agreement with a *supervised* model's attributions on MNIST measures whether both methods find digit-shaped pixels, not whether the distilled encoder uses the pixels the t-SNE used. (3) *A measure worth borrowing:* the disagreement set. The points where the student's reconstruction fails are exactly the points where the map is untrustworthy, and attributing them localises the distortion to specific inputs — a cheap instrument for flagging where our own surrogate read-outs should not be trusted. Note also the baseline that failed silently: the t-SNE gradient method ran for 14 hours and returned near-zero maps everywhere, which is the folder's recurring lesson that an explainer returning nothing looks like a null result rather than a broken tool.
