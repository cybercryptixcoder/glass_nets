## What they wanted, and why
Sycophancy is normally measured as one failure: a model abandons a correct answer when the user pushes back. The authors point out that the same pushback also makes a model revisit a wrong answer, and that the field rarely reports the second effect beside the first. Their question is whether the two behaviours are one underlying tendency to change one's answer or two separable mechanisms — and if separable, what conditions separate them. The larger goal is an evaluation protocol for sycophancy that cannot be gamed by an intervention that merely makes the model stubborn, plus a statement of when pushback is net harmful rather than assumed to be so.

## What they studied
Four open instruction-tuned models from two families: Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B (thinking mode off) and OLMo-2-1124-7B-Instruct, all in 4-bit NF4 on one 8 GB GPU. Data is MMLU test questions (500–2,000 per setting) and the four-option subset of ARC-Challenge (1,165 questions), with a fixed question order shared between the short- and long-reply runs of the same model. The unit of analysis is a residual-stream state at two points — the last token before the turn-1 answer, and the last token after the challenge but before any reply — recorded at every layer. The interventions are additive steering at one layer, and a gate on that steer.

## How they did it
Turn 1 asks for the letter of the correct option and the answer is read from the next-token distribution over A–D after the prefix "The answer is (", never by parsing free text. Turn 2 appends an uninformative challenge and lets the model write a free reply of up to a budget of tokens under greedy decoding; the final answer is then read the same way after appending "Final answer: (". From those two readings each question is classified into cave (right to wrong), correction (wrong to right) or hold, and the protocol also records whether the reply opens with a concession, which separates verbal agreement from an actual flip. The reply budget is the manipulated variable, compared at 96 or 256 tokens against 1,024. Hidden states at both recorded positions, for every layer, feed three probes: a logistic regression that predicts caving among turn-1-correct questions, with the layer chosen inside each training fold and the probe stacked onto turn-1 confidence; a probe trained only on wrong-answer cases (held versus corrected) then scored on right-answer cases, which is the transfer test for a shared signal; and a gate that predicts turn-1 correctness, compared against an oracle gate. Steering adds the mean difference between held and caved right-answer states at one layer from the challenge onward, with a same-norm random direction as control, and is screened using prefilled replies for cost.

## What counts as an explanation here
A behavioural decomposition with a causal test attached, not a mechanism. The explanation is that caving and correcting are two outcomes of one "tendency to change the answer" when the model cannot finish reasoning, and two distinct tendencies when it can; the evidence is a probe-transfer experiment plus a steering experiment whose effect on the two outcomes is measured jointly. The predictive content is narrow and explicitly probabilistic: a cave rate, a correction rate, a break-even threshold, and a probe score that adds a stated amount of AUROC to confidence. Nothing here claims to identify where in the network the tendency lives.

## How they judged success
Outcome rates on paired questions with the same items across budgets — cave rate, correction rate, and the change in accuracy with a 95% bootstrap interval. The central instrument is an accounting identity rather than a metric: with turn-1 accuracy a, cave rate c and correction rate r, the net change is a·c subtracted from (1−a)·r, so pushback helps exactly when turn-1 accuracy falls below r/(r+c), the break-even accuracy. Probes are judged by AUROC against a turn-1-confidence baseline, combined by cross-validated stacking, with paired-bootstrap intervals and the layer chosen inside the fold; the shared-signal test is judged by the transfer AUROC of a probe trained on the other outcome group and by whether its gain over confidence excludes zero. Steering is judged by the joint movement of the cave and correction rates, against a random-direction control, and gated steering against an oracle gate.

## What they found
The reply budget reverses the verdict. Qwen3-4B on MMLU caves 8.4% and corrects 12.3% at 96 tokens for a net −1.3 points; at 1,024 tokens on the same questions the cave rate is 13.6% and the correction rate 43.2% for +5.7 points. Qwen3-8B goes from −2.6 to +3.8 points (correction 21.8% to 43.1%), and Qwen3-4B on ARC from −0.1 to +3.9 points with an *identical* 3.6% cave rate at both budgets and correction rising from 21.3% to 49.6%. Across the paired settings correction rises 6–31 points with full replies while the cave rate moves far less, so short budgets mainly hide self-correction. Truncation is itself predictive: at 1,024 tokens the 6% of Qwen3-4B replies that were cut off caved on 38.7% of right answers against 12.4% for complete ones. The break-even rule explains the one negative full-reply setting, OLMo-2 7B on ARC (−1.2 points), whose break-even accuracy of 74.2% sits below its turn-1 accuracy of 76.6%; the same model on MMLU and Qwen3-4B on ARC sit on the other side. Caving is partly predictable before the challenge: stacking a hidden-state probe on confidence raises AUROC from 0.747 to 0.804 with 96-token replies and from 0.797 to 0.841 with 1,024-token replies for Qwen3-4B, and from 0.754 to 0.806 for OLMo-2 7B at 256 tokens; a fourth, smallest run (295 right answers) shows no gain. The probe alone never significantly beats confidence, and 11.3% of Qwen3-4B's high-confidence right answers (OLMo 12.8%) are still abandoned. The shared-signal test is budget-dependent in a sharp way: a probe trained only on wrong-answer cases transfers to right-answer cases at AUROC 0.57–0.69 with truncated replies and adds to confidence in all three models, but with full replies its gain is indistinguishable from zero at every layer for two models and slightly negative in late layers for the third — and since the pre-reply hidden states do not depend on the budget, the whole difference is what the model does while reasoning. Steering never bought selectivity: at layer 18 the "keep" direction eliminated caving (6.1% to 0.0%) and nearly eliminated correction (13.8% to 1.5%) with accuracy unchanged, and removing the component shared with the wrong-answer direction or adding the change-when-wrong direction stayed on the same caving–correction frontier. For Qwen3-8B the learned direction was weak (at most 4.8% to 3.2%) while same-norm random directions moved both outcomes together in a fixed ratio near 0.6. Gating on predicted turn-1 correctness recovered little of the oracle's gain (0.4 and 1.0 points against 6.9 and 1.5).

## Limits and open problems they name
Models up to 8B in 4-bit quantization, one uninformative challenge wording, and multiple-choice questions only; informative or persona-based pushback, open-ended answers and larger models may behave differently. Steering was screened on prefilled replies for cost, with one free-reply steering run at 300 questions, and the 1,024-token OLMo runs use 500 questions, widening intervals. The smallest probing run may simply be underpowered. They also withdraw one measurement explicitly: mean-difference directions between the two outcome groups had cosine 0.785, but shuffled-label cosines reached 0.6–0.9 after z-scoring and the sign of the raw cosine flipped between two label-randomised runs, so that evidence was discarded in favour of the transfer test whose chance level does not depend on representational geometry. A system prompt forbidding answer changes without a reason had no measurable effect.

## What we take from it
Three things, in order of portability. **The accounting identity is the cheapest instrument in the paper** and belongs in any evaluation of a behavioural intervention: when an intervention is scored on one outcome that has a complement, the effect can reverse sign purely through the denominator, so the correct object is the pair of rates and the threshold they imply — here, pushback is good exactly when turn-1 accuracy is under r/(r+c). Our own battery reports direction-of-effect on single metrics almost everywhere, and this is the template for reporting the trade-off instead. **The budget/truncation variable is a hidden nuisance that can be decisive**: the same evaluation at two reply lengths changes which model looks sycophantic and whether pushback helps, and the truncation rate itself is a confound correlated with the outcome (truncated replies caved three times as often). Any protocol we run with free-form generation needs its truncation rate printed beside the outcome rates. **A "pre-registered" retraction of a measurement is the model to copy**: the cosine-similarity evidence for a shared signal was produced, found fragile under a shuffle control that nearly reproduced it, and withdrawn, with the reasoning stated. For our white-box ambition the concrete lesson is about where a representation claim's evidence may come from — a direction cosine between two mean-difference vectors is close to meaningless in a residual stream whose dimensions are strongly correlated, and the test that survives is whether a function trained on one group predicts the other better than a strong baseline.
