# On the Emergence of Induction Heads for In-Context Learning

- **Key:** arxiv-2511.01033
- **Authors / organisation:** Tiberiu Mușat, Tiago Pimentel, Lorenzo Noci, Thomas Hofmann; ETH Zürich (Alessandro Stolfo and Mrinmaya Sachan appear in the affiliation block; see limits below)
- **Date:** 2025-11-02
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.01033
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (arXiv HTML v1; abstract, §1–§9, references, Appendices A–F including the whole weight-structure proof and the whole emergence-time proof, the sympy-verified gradient expressions, and the training-detail appendices)
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

Induction heads are the field's canonical example of a circuit with a mechanistic *and* behavioural signature: the match-and-copy pair of heads whose formation coincides with a sharp drop in loss. Existing theory shows they *can* emerge under staged or constructed learning rules, but not what gradient descent actually does at each step. The authors ask how the circuit forms under ordinary training, and they want the answer in the same currency a circuit analysis uses — which directions in the weights carry the mechanism, in what order they grow, and how long each takes as a function of the task. The larger goal is a bridge between two literatures that rarely meet: the weight-level description of a mechanism, and optimisation-theoretic statements about training dynamics. For our project this is the reverse direction of everything else in the folder — not "a circuit exists in a trained network" but "here is the low-dimensional path along which a network acquires one," which is exactly the kind of object a predictive white-box model would have to reproduce if it is to be a model of the network rather than of its activations.

## What they studied

Two settings. First, a standard order: two attention-only layers, one head each, no MLPs, learned token and positional embeddings, cross-entropy on the final query position only, trained on a synthetic ICL task (a list of item–label pairs, then a query item whose label must be produced) with vocabulary 32, block size 32, embedding dimension 2048, AdamW at 0.001 for 300 steps of 512 fresh sequences. Second, a minimal formulation: a disentangled-residual transformer (attention output *concatenated* rather than added) with a merged key-query matrix and no value or output projection, run on an idealised version of the same task where items, labels and positional embeddings are drawn i.i.d. and each label carries a fixed rotated copy of its pair's positional embedding. The unit of analysis is the **pseudo-parameter**: a scalar coordinate along one structured direction in the weights, not an individual weight. The attributes under study are the structure of the learned weight matrices (which token/position subspaces they act in), the scalar trajectories of the pseudo-parameters over training, and the emergence time of each.

## How they did it

Step one is an interpretable transformation of an ordinary trained model. Because only token and positional embeddings are ever written into the residual stream, each weight matrix splits into blocks indexed by *which embedding* a row and column correspond to, and the whole network can be re-indexed by those interpretable directions — the authors point out that this re-indexing lets the attention scores for any possible input be read off directly, so the transformed matrix picture is a complete description of the model's behaviour rather than a sampled one. They read off the structure visually: layer one attends to the previous position, layer two attends to what layer one retrieved, and the output layer copies what layer two retrieved.

Step two is the theory. Under zero initialisation, population gradient descent, and an isotropy assumption on the data (items, labels and positional embeddings individually invariant to orthogonal transformations), they prove that the weights keep a fixed block-sparse structure for the whole of training, leaving 19 scalar pseudo-parameters out of the full parameter count. The proof is a symmetry argument run inductively: the isotropy assumption means a carefully chosen rotation of the data leaves the expected gradient unchanged, while the same rotation acts on each weight *block* as a similarity transform; a block that is invariant under all such transforms must be constant on its diagonal and equal across its blocks, or else zero — so any block that is not one of the structured ones has gradient identically zero and stays zero forever.

Step three is the empirical check that only three of the nineteen matter. They train with ordinary SGD from that structure, plot the 19 pseudo-parameters and the loss together, and find three that grow large: one making each label attend to its preceding item in layer one, one making the query attend to the correct label in layer two, and one reading the retrieved label out in the final linear layer — jointly exactly an induction head. They then re-train with the parameters *constrained to the 3-dimensional subspace* spanned by those three and find the dynamics essentially unchanged, slightly faster.

Step four fixes the three-parameter system, derives the loss as a closed form in the three scalars and the context length, and integrates the gradient flow in continuous time to get emergence times as functions of the context length. This part is done both symbolically (the gradients are checked against SymPy differentiation) and empirically, by measuring per-parameter emergence times across context lengths.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **complete description in two parts, of a deliberately idealised object**. The first part is a weight-level statement: the block structure of every matrix, plus the assertion that all four residual-stream subspaces that are ever activated are interpretable because they are spanned by the token and positional embeddings that the model was given. The second part is a program for how that structure is reached: three scalar trajectories, proven to be the only ones that move, with an ordering and an asymptotic timing law. It is complete for the toy task in a way almost nothing else in this folder is — the loss is an explicit function of three numbers — and it is explicitly not a claim about natural-language ICL, large models, MLPs, or multi-head structure. The predictive content is genuine but narrow: given the context length, the theory predicts which sub-mechanism appears first, in what order the rest follow, and the scaling of the wait.

## How they judged success

Three checks, none of them a benchmark. (i) *Structural agreement*: visual comparison of the weights at the end of real SGD training against the theoretically predicted block pattern — the authors present this as confirmation, and note residual noise from random initialisation and stochastic descent. (ii) *Subspace sufficiency*: re-train with every parameter outside the three-dimensional subspace frozen; the pseudo-parameter trajectories and the loss curve are compared qualitatively with standard training, and the claim is that they look strikingly similar and are slightly accelerated, offered as evidence that the mechanism is self-contained rather than assembled from a scaffold of helper parameters. (iii) *Timing law*: measure each emergence time — defined as the first time a parameter crosses 0.5 — for several values of the context length and compare against the proven asymptotics, on a log axis, with the caveat that the empirical run uses a threshold of 0.1 for two of the three parameters, rather than the theoretical 0.5, to make the separation visible. There is no held-out task, no baseline architecture, and no comparison against any other explanation of induction heads.

## What they found

The mechanism is three scalars and each has a natural role: the first-layer attention slope makes labels find their own items, the second-layer slope makes the query find the label whose item matches, and the output coefficient copies the retrieved label. Only a handful of other pseudo-parameters grow at all, and the joint growth of these three tracks the loss drop. Restricting training to their subspace leaves the dynamics essentially unchanged, so the sixteen other directions are not scaffolding for these three — they are simply not used.

The timing result is the part with transferable content. Under orthonormal inputs, the three parameters provably emerge in a fixed order — output coefficient first, then the second-layer slope, then the first-layer slope — and their emergence times scale as: output coefficient linear in the number of item–label pairs, the two attention slopes quadratic in it, hence total time to ICL quadratic. The mechanism behind the ordering is a chain of vanishing gradients: with the attention untrained, each layer still returns a context average, and the average contains the correct label, so the output layer has something to fit first, with a gradient falling as one over the context length; once the output is fitted the second layer has a strong enough signal to learn its matching, but it is fed by layer one's weak, one-over-length retrieval, so its gradient falls as one over the squared length; once both are in place the first layer gets a strong gradient and follows quickly. Increasing the context therefore slows emergence quadratically, and the authors connect this to the known data-dependence results — corpus properties such as burstiness can be read as shortening the effective context, and they suggest positional schemes could do the same.

## Limits and open problems they name

They are candid about the idealisation. The proof needs zero initialisation, the population (infinite-batch) gradient, and isotropic data, none of which holds in real training; the population-loss and zero-init assumptions are borrowed from the theoretical literature as a stand-in for small random init. The minimal architecture drops the value and output projections and the residual addition, and the task's labels carry a pre-built positional correlation that real language does not. The empirical validation is visual for the weights and small-scale for the timings. The discussion only hypothesises about how context-length effects connect to corpus statistics; no experiment tests that.

Two problems are mine rather than theirs, logged so they are not mistaken for the authors' words. First, the author block as extracted is garbled: two further names appear attached to the affiliation line, so I record the four names listed on the paper itself and flag that the byline may be longer. Second, the paper's own Appendix B summaries contain an index inversion — the summary text says the first parameter to cross is γ after Θ(N) and calls that T₁, then restates the total as T_α = T₁ — while Theorem 2 and the body of the proof both give the order γ, then β, then α with α last. I read the body and the theorem, not the summary, and the ordering I report is theirs.

## What we take from it

**A template for how a component's identity can be made low-dimensional by symmetry, and three specific instruments.**

1. *The symmetry argument is the reusable method, not the induction-head result.* If a data distribution is invariant to a group of transformations, the expected gradient is constrained to the subspace of weights that commutes with that group, so the parts of a matrix outside the invariant structure provably never move. Applied to any pair of interpretable bases we already care about — token/position, source/target, pre/post-instruction, two model checkpoints — this gives a *pre-registered zero*: the off-structure blocks should stay at initialisation, and if they do not, the symmetry assumption is false for that data. That is a much cheaper falsification of "only these directions matter" than fitting and inspecting, and it turns our "which directions could possibly be learned here" question into a weight-space computation with no forward passes.
2. *The (layer × mechanism) trajectory, ordered, is a prediction object.* The paper's output is a statement of the form "parameter A moves first with rate scaled by 1/N, B second with rate scaled by 1/N², and the order is forced" — i.e. not just which component implements a behaviour but in what sequence and at what rate its parameters become load-bearing. That is a shape our predictor could be scored against at the *training* level, and it is the natural companion to the folder's layer-grid entry for looped models: same discipline of not collapsing an axis that has structure.
3. *Emergence time is a measurement with a definition, and theirs is arbitrary in a way worth copying.* Define emergence as the first crossing of a threshold on a named pseudo-parameter, then sweep the task parameter and check the asymptotic scaling. The arbitrary threshold is stated, and the empirical run deliberately uses a different one from the theory — which is the honest version of a comparison that cannot be exact.

**Warnings that constrain what we may transfer.** The model is three scalars deep: no MLP, one head per layer, no residual addition, and an idealised data distribution that was *chosen* to make the mechanism reachable. The result therefore tells us that a mechanism can be acquired along a provably low-dimensional path when the data distribution permits it, not that real networks acquire their mechanisms that way. And the quadratic slowdown with context length is a statement about *this* parameterisation with its one-over-length averaging; reading it as a general law about long contexts would repeat the folder's oldest mistake — importing a scaling law measured in an idealised setting as if it were a fact about language models.
