# NeuralExplorer: State Space Exploration of Closed Loop Control Systems Using Neural Networks

- **Key:** arxiv-2007.05685
- **Authors / organisation:** Manish Goyal, Parasara Sridhar Duggirala; Department of Computer Science, University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill
- **Date:** 2020-07-11
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.05685
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Testing a feedback-controlled system is a search problem: the state space is continuous, the designer has a handful of test runs, and there is no principled way to ask for a run that lands somewhere specific. Existing falsification tools answer a narrower question — find one trace that violates a temporal-logic specification — and their output is only usable for the specification they were given; if the designer raises the error threshold, the previous analysis is worthless. What is missing is the middle of the workflow, before deployment: a way to steer the system toward a region of interest, using only the sample trajectories already run. This paper builds that: it learns, from a small set of simulations of a black-box closed loop, how a change in the initial state propagates to a change in the trajectory, and then inverts that learned relation to choose new initial states.

## What they studied
Closed-loop systems of the form `ẋ = f(x, g(x))` with a state dimension of 2 to 6 in every experiment. The unit of analysis is not a network but a **trajectory**: the object being predicted is the map from initial condition to state-at-time-t. Fourteen benchmarks, split three ways: continuous nonlinear dynamics (Brussellator, Buckling, Lotka, Jetengine, Van der Pol, Lac operon, Rössler, Lorenz, Steam, coupled Van der Pol — the last four at 3-4 dimensions, with Lorenz and Rössler chaotic), hybrid systems (hybrid oscillator and smooth hybrid oscillator), and neural-network-feedback systems (Mountain Car and Quadrotor, the latter with 5 discrete modes). Models trained are ordinary multilayer perceptrons, not the systems' own controllers.

## How they did it
Two functions are defined. **Sensitivity** is the vector difference between the trajectory started at `x₀` and the one started at `x₀ + v`, after time `t`. **Inverse sensitivity** is the perturbation to the initial condition needed to displace a trajectory by `v` at time `t` — i.e. the inverse of that map. For smooth systems both are Lie-derivative series that can be truncated; for hybrid or non-smooth closed loops that series cannot be evaluated at all, and that is the motivation for learning them. Training data is harvested from a user-specified set of simulated trajectories by exploiting two facts: any prefix or suffix of a trajectory is itself a trajectory of shorter duration, and any two sampled points `x₁, x₂` at the same step supply their own sensitivity label (`ξ(x₂,t) − ξ(x₁,t)`) and inverse-sensitivity label (`x₂ − x₁`) for free. Each benchmark uses roughly 30 or 50 trajectories, split 90/10 train/test, with a user-given ODE step size. Each network is an 8-layer, 512-wide Keras MLP (ReLU activations, except sigmoid for Mountain Car), trained with Levenberg-Marquardt backpropagation on mean absolute error, evaluated by RMSE and mean relative error. The exploration routine is a fixed-point iteration: simulate from a random initial state, take the residual `x(t) − z` to the target, ask the inverse-sensitivity net for the initial-condition correction that would remove it, apply it, and resimulate — repeating up to a user-set iteration count. Falsification reuses this routine by drawing random points inside the unsafe set and looping until one is reached. A third regime uses the forward sensitivity net to predict trajectories from a few expensive "anchor" simulations instead of running all of them, and a greedy density search walks an anchor toward the unsafe set using predicted displacements.

## What counts as an explanation here
A learned surrogate for a dynamical property: two neural networks that stand in for the forward and inverse trajectory-displacement maps. It is explicitly *not* a proof and not an enclosure — nothing here is sound, nothing is a bound. It is meant to be predictive-but-partial: the claim is only that the learned function generalises beyond the training trajectories well enough to be useful for steering, and the paper defends that claim with a held-out error figure and with one out-of-distribution demonstration. It also doubles as a geometric explanation: because a learned inverse sensitivity can be queried for "what initial perturbation moves me toward that region", the designer gets directional guidance even when no violating trace exists — the paper positions this as filling the gap where a falsifier simply returns "no counterexample found".

## How they judged success
Four different yardsticks, none of them a soundness guarantee. (1) **Generalisation error** of the learned functions: RMSE and mean relative error on the held-out 10%, reported per benchmark for both networks. (2) **Target-reaching accuracy**: after 1 and 5 iterations of the steering loop, the L2 distance `d_a` from the reached state to the target and its ratio `d_r` to the initial distance, averaged over 500 runs per benchmark; also wall-clock milliseconds per generated trajectory. (3) **An out-of-distribution test**: expand Mountain Car's initial set from `[-0.55,-0.45]` to `[-0.60,-0.40]` and check whether the net still finds a valid far-hill maximum. (4) **Comparative falsification**: overlay plots of trajectories generated by an STL falsification tool versus theirs on Brussellator, Lac operon, Lorenz, Buckling and Van der Pol — including figures shown specifically for the cases where the baseline wins. There is no ablation of the network architecture, no sample-size sweep, and no statistical test beyond reporting ranges across ten targets.

## What they found
Both functions are learnable from a small trajectory budget, and the picture is more mixed than the paper's own summary implies. Test MRE is under 0.20 for only **7 of 14** benchmarks in the sensitivity table (worst: Mountain Car 0.79, Jetengine 0.63) and **9 of 14** in the inverse table (worst: Mountain Car 0.70, Lac operon 0.46); the paper's conclusion says both stay "under 20% relative error", which the tables do not support. Absolute MSE is no more uniform — inverse MSE ranges from 0.0011 on Quadrotor to 1.8 on Lac operon, and sensitivity MSE from 0.015 on Mountain Car to 2.38 on Buckling — so neither average is representative and the entry's own reading is that the per-benchmark spread is the result, not any aggregate. Training costs 10-115 minutes per network. Target-reaching is where the design pays off: after 5 iterations the **minimum** relative distance in every benchmark's range falls at or below 0.30, and 6 of 14 have a full range under or at 0.30 (best: Jetengine `[0.006,0.14]`, Brussellator `[0.01,0.12]`, Mountain Car `[0.03,0.12]`); the worst upper ends reach 0.67 (Lorenz, Quadrotor, Van der Pol). Reading it as "below 10% in almost all cases" is the paper's summary of the ranges, not the table's mean. Per-trajectory generation costs 8.7-284.96 ms at 1 iteration and 31.34-668.78 ms at 5 — sub-second in every case — against tens of minutes of one-time training. The out-of-distribution test is the strongest single result: on the widened Mountain Car initial set the method finds a maximum left-hill distance of 1.3019 against the 1.17 seen for the original set, showing the learned map extends past its training trajectories. On falsification the paper claims a sample-efficiency win on some benchmarks and openly shows others (Lorenz, Buckling, Van der Pol) where the STL tool terminates faster; the reported advantage is stated qualitatively with no counts on either side.

## Limits and open problems they name
Only initial-condition perturbation is modelled — not actuation or environmental input, which they list as future work. The learned map is an approximation with no error bound, so the steering loop is a heuristic descent with a fixed iteration cap; the authors attribute the worst relative distances to high dimension or long target distance and hope more iterations help, but offer no criterion for when the loop will converge. Falsification behaviour is not characterised: the paper shows both wins and losses without explaining which regime produces which, and calls improvement "future work". No soundness claim is made anywhere, and no comparison against reachability tools is attempted — the position taken is explicitly complementary. Training data volumes and hyper-parameters are described as empirical.

## What we take from it
A **method** and a **measure**, plus one deliberate warning. The method is the anchor-and-correct pattern: run a small number of expensive exact simulations, fit a cheap model of *how errors propagate* rather than of the system itself, then use the inverse of that model to choose where to look next. This is the first cost axis in the closed-loop thread that is not indexed by a set representation at all — the budget is a number of queries, and the quantity being predicted is a displacement, not a region. That suits our goal directly, because a white-box account of a network that predicts *how an input perturbation moves the output* is a strictly weaker and far cheaper object than one that predicts the output's whole range, and this paper is evidence that the weaker object is enough to steer. Two things we take as a warning. First: the errors reported here are **relative to a random starting distance**, which makes every benchmark's error look good and makes them incomparable to each other — if we report a relative fidelity figure we must state the reference it is relative to, or the number measures the sampling distribution. Second: this paper is the cleanest case in the thread of the trade running the other way — the surrogate is unsound by construction yet *more useful to a designer* than the sound tools, because it answers "which direction" instead of "is it safe", and it says so when a falsifier says nothing. So an unsound account is not a failed account; it is an account answering a different question, and our report must name the question. Read the falsification figures as the honest part of the paper and the missing falsifier counts as the gap.
