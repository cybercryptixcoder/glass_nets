## What they wanted, and why
An LLM can be asked to write down a probabilistic program for a problem, and a whole collection of programs can be sampled from one prompt; a recently proposed pipeline ("Large Language Bayes") does exactly this and then combines the per-program posteriors by weighting each program with its marginal likelihood, i.e. ordinary Bayesian model averaging. The authors' complaint is that the sampled collection is essentially never a class that contains the true generating process — the class was written by a language model, so the closed-world premise of model averaging fails. In that regime marginal-likelihood weights are known to concentrate on one member, so the averaged answer stops being a hedge and becomes a hardest-winner pick dressed as an average. Their goal is narrow and stated as such: keep the generation and per-model inference exactly as they are, and change only the **aggregation rule**, so that the weights reflect predictive usefulness under misspecification rather than evidence. The larger motivation is that automated model-building is only as trustworthy as the rule that merges its candidates, and that rule currently inherits an assumption nobody checked.

## What they studied
Probabilistic programs drawn from a text prompt: 250 candidate NumPyro programs per task, generated by Qwen2.5 Coder 32B, deduplicated by normalised code hash and filtered to those that compile and pass shape validation. Unit of analysis is the **weight vector over candidate models** (and the resulting mixture posterior), not any single model. Three tasks, all county-level expected annual loss from the FEMA National Risk Index: wildfire, inland flooding, hurricane — 20 observed counties per task, 10 held out, losses strictly positive and heavy-tailed.

## How they did it
For each candidate model and each observation they define the leave-one-out predictive density of that observation given the rest of the data, and the stacking weights as the point on the probability simplex that maximises the sum of log mixture predictive densities — the standard stacking objective, which targets out-of-sample predictive accuracy rather than posterior model probability. Two additions make it work here. First, because the programs have latent variables, the leave-one-out densities are intractable, so each is replaced by a variational lower bound (an ELBO computed under a Gaussian variational family, fitted for every (model, held-out point) pair) and the objective becomes a log-sum-exp over these bounds. Second, a KL penalty pulls the learned weights toward the original evidence-based weights, with the penalty strength chosen on a validation split from a small grid. The objective is optimised in log space through a softmax parameterisation with BFGS and analytic gradients. Each retained model is fitted with NUTS. Baselines: the original evidence-weighted rule on the identical collection. Diagnostics: the effective sample size of the weight vector (inverse sum of squared weights) and the top weights printed per dataset, plus held-out expected log predictive density. Appendix A recomputes the ELBO trajectories for six points per task and reports that bounds stabilise after roughly ten to fifteen iterations, within twenty-five.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not an explanation of a model in the interpretability sense — the object is an **aggregation rule**, and the "understanding" offered is a *diagnostic of the mixture*: how many candidates effectively carry weight, and which ones. Complete over the sampled collection only; it says nothing about a model outside the collection, and the authors are explicit that the weights are not an epistemic quantity.

## How they judged success
Two axes, deliberately separated: (i) concentration, reported as effective sample size of the weights plus the top-two weights per dataset; (ii) predictive performance, reported as held-out expected log predictive density against the same collection under evidence weighting. No ablation isolates the KL penalty's strength, no arm replaces the variational bound with a tighter one, and no synthetic control exists in which the true generating process *is* one of the candidates — which is the case where the two rules are supposed to agree.

## What they found
Evidence weighting collapses: the largest weight is 1.000 on one model for hurricane and for inland flood, and 0.998 for wildfire. Stacking on the same collections spreads mass — hurricane 0.759 / 0.238 / 0.003, inland flood 0.588 / 0.412, wildfire 0.666 / 0.334 — again pairing different models in some cases and the same top model in others. Predictive performance is essentially indistinguishable: hurricane −5.999 against −5.983, inland flood identical at −370.340 for both, wildfire −177.897 against −177.922 (stacking marginally better). Their reading is that the difference is not predictive power but *representation of uncertainty*.

## Limits and open problems they name
They concede in the discussion that the experiment does not establish that the learned weights recover the true epistemic uncertainty, and that the mixture is bounded by the quality and diversity of what the language model generated. Beyond what they name: 30 test points total, three tasks from one dataset, one generator model, one criterion (log score) used both to fit the weights and to grade them, and a dispersion claim resting on effective sample size while the predictive numbers are flat to three decimals — a combination in which "comparable" and "identical" are not distinguished.

## What we take from it
A framing and a warning. The framing: when our account set is produced by a generator we cannot certify (a screen, a search, a language model), the aggregation rule must be chosen for the regime we are actually in — the true account is not in the set — and the correct move is to pick weights by predictive criterion rather than by likelihood or score, because evidence-like weights degenerate to selection exactly there. The warning is sharper and is the reason this entry earns relevance 2 despite thin results: their headline improvement is **dispersion with unchanged accuracy**, and unchanged accuracy is compatible with weights that have no informational content. A weighted ensemble whose predictive numbers match the collapsed baseline is evidence that the extra spread is decorative. The transferable discipline is to always print the dispersion statistic *beside* the criterion the weights were fitted on, and to refuse to read an increase in the former as an increase in the latter.
