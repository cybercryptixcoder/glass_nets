# FADE: Why Bad Descriptions Happen to Good Features

- **Key:** arxiv-2502.16994

- **Kind:** benchmark

## What they wanted, and why

Automated interpretability pipelines describe a latent feature with a short natural-language label, and the field scored those labels mainly with a simulation metric: ask a model to predict a feature's activations from the description, then correlate predicted with real. The authors argue this single number is a poor certificate — it can be high for a description that captures one of a feature's several meanings, and it says nothing about whether the feature actually does anything to the output. Their goal is a model-agnostic, automatic evaluation of feature-to-description alignment that decomposes the failure into identifiable parts, so that pipelines can be compared and debugged the way ordinary models are. They then use it to attribute bad descriptions to their causes: the explainer model, the prompt, the number of samples, the layer, and the feature type. The larger aim is to make feature descriptions trustworthy enough that a dictionary of them could serve as a usable account of a model, rather than a plausible-looking text artefact.

## What they studied

Unit of analysis is a single latent feature and the sentence that describes it. Subjects: Gemma-2-2b MLP neurons at layers 3, 12, 20 and 25; Gemma Scope residual-stream SAEs at layer 20 in 16K and 65K widths; Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct neurons at layer 19. Roughly a thousand randomly chosen features per setting from a single layer. Natural data is the uncopyrighted Pile: a 6 GB extract (~88.7 M sentences) used to find maximally activating samples and to generate descriptions, and a test partition (~5.4 M sentences) used for evaluation. Descriptions come from three sources: the pre-existing Neuronpedia / Transluce label sets, a TF-IDF baseline, an unembedding-projection baseline, and the authors' own MaxAct* pipeline. A separate large model (GPT-4o-mini by default, GPT-4o as the reference rater) acts as the evaluating LLM.

## How they did it

For each feature, the top thousand activating sentences are collected in one forward pass, and a small uniformly drawn subset of them (5 to 50) goes to an explainer model that writes a one-line concept label. FADE then runs four measurements on that label. Clarity: the explainer is asked to invent text that expresses the described concept; the label is clear if the invented samples separate from Pile sentences drawn as controls, measured as the absolute Gini coefficient over the two activation populations. Responsiveness: five hundred natural sentences are sampled across activation percentiles, the evaluator rates each on a three-point presence scale, the ambiguous middle is discarded, and the same absolute-Gini separability is computed — this asks whether real text containing the concept actually lights the feature up. Purity: average precision over the same rated set, at every threshold — this asks whether the strongest activations are exclusive to the concept, so a feature that fires on unrelated topics is punished. Faithfulness: fifty random Pile sentences are continued by the subject model while the feature's activation is multiplied by factors from −50 to +50, the continuations are rated for concept presence, and the score is the largest gain over the zeroed-out baseline, normalised by the remaining headroom. Faithfulness is run only when Clarity and Responsiveness both clear 0.5, which is stated as a cost control and is a real selection effect on the reported correlations. The same four metrics are then applied across feature sources, SAE widths, layers, prompt formats, shot counts and explainer models.

## What counts as an explanation here

A text description of one feature, plus a four-number profile reporting how well that text is aligned with the feature's actual behaviour. The description is meant to be partial and open-vocabulary: the paper's position is that a feature does not need to be monosemantic, it needs to be *fully described* — a feature may carry several unrelated topics provided the label covers all of them. The four numbers are explicitly a decomposition of causes rather than a single certificate, and the authors argue against collapsing them into one score. Predictive power is limited and stated as such: Clarity predicts that synthetic samples can be generated which activate the feature, Responsiveness predicts that natural concept-bearing text activates it, Purity predicts that other text does not, Faithfulness predicts that the activation is load-bearing for output content. Notably no metric tests whether the description predicts what the feature will do under a *new* intervention; only one of the four touches causality at all.

## How they judged success

Four metrics, all automatic, each with a defined chance behaviour (absolute Gini and average precision over a binarised set). Their own positive control is not a null but a model-size ladder for the evaluator: agreement with GPT-4o's concept ratings, reported per class (absent / partial / clear) plus the share of samples classed correctly. Baselines for description quality are the pre-existing Neuronpedia labels, TF-IDF and an unembedding-projection label. Ablations: SAE width 16K against 65K; layer 3, 12, 20, 25; delimiter-highlighted versus numeric-token input; 0, 1, 2, 5, 10, 20 shots; 5, 15, 50 samples; four explainer model sizes. Distributions are shown as kernel-density estimates rather than only means, and costs are quoted per thousand features.

## What they found

The evaluator needs size: Llama-3.2-3B agrees with GPT-4o on 77.4 per cent of clearly-absent and 70.3 per cent of clearly-present ratings but only 9.9 per cent on partial ones, and Llama-3.1-8B is named as the practical floor. Single-metric optimisation does not carry over: descriptions produced by a pipeline fine-tuned on the simulation metric, ranked into its own top decile, gain only marginally on Clarity and Responsiveness and get *worse* on Purity. On Gemma Scope 16K, MaxAct* scores 0.57 / 0.78 / 0.69 / 0.21 (Clarity, Responsiveness, Purity, Faithfulness) against Neuronpedia 0.43 / 0.67 / 0.60 / 0.21, TF-IDF 0.42 / 0.72 / 0.53 / 0.21 and the unembedding baseline 0.38 / 0.65 / 0.61 / 0.29 — the unembedding label is worst on three of four metrics and best on Faithfulness, exactly because it is built from the output side. Bigger SAEs are not better described: 16K scores 0.57 / 0.78 / 0.69 / 0.17 against 65K's 0.46 / 0.71 / 0.66 / 0.15. Layer matters more than width: layer 12 is the worst on Clarity at 0.44 and Purity at 0.43 while layer 20 is best at 0.67 and 0.61, though the authors flag that layer 20 is the one their prompt was tuned on. More samples and more shots both help monotonically but plateau — 0.60 / 0.79 / 0.72 at 5, 10 and 20 shots is barely above 5 shots, and 50 samples reaches 0.69 Clarity against 0.63 at five. Explainer capability helps roughly monotonically from Llama-3.2-1B (0.39 / 0.56 / 0.35) through GPT-4o (0.61 / 0.80 / 0.73). Faithfulness is the odd metric throughout: it is 0.01 for MLP neurons against 0.17 for SAEs in the same table, correlates weakly with the other three (Purity–Faithfulness 0.12 for MaxAct* 16K and 0.23 for Neuronpedia 65K), and is reported as highest at layer 25.

## Limits and open problems they name

Everything is measured through a language model's judgement, so the evaluator's cultural, domain and language biases enter the numbers; the authors say a feature encoding a concept in a language other than English may be described differently. The Pile is unbalanced, so rare concepts give unstable Purity and Responsiveness. Faithfulness does not check that the steered continuations stay grammatical or meaningful, and they suggest KL-based steering control as the fix. The metric cannot represent inhibitory features — a feature that suppresses a concept shows no *increase* under manipulation — though they note a reframing in affirmative terms would let it work. Sampling from the top thousand optimising samples is deliberate outlier avoidance but leaves more sophisticated sampling untried. They also concede that a description can be close to the concept yet useless: their own example feature encodes an idiom while its label says only "belt", which scores well on Responsiveness and Purity and fails on Clarity.

## What we take from it

Three concrete borrowings, one warning. (1) *A checklist for any description our account emits.* Four separable questions — can the description generate inputs that hit the feature, does natural text with the concept hit it, do non-concept texts also hit it, does manipulating the feature change the output — each scored on its own, each with a printed chance level. This is the cheapest available test of the middle of a feature→description→intervention chain, and unlike a simulation score it cannot be satisfied by a label that names one of a feature's several meanings. (2) *A positive control we can copy: the evaluator-size ladder.* Agreement with the strongest evaluator, reported per class, is how they show their own instrument is not the bottleneck — and the finding that partial-presence ratings are where small evaluators collapse (9.9 per cent agreement at 3B) is a specific warning for any LLM-judged measurement in our account. (3) *The "described, not monosemantic" position* is the right stance for our account: a feature may hold several topics, and the obligation is coverage rather than purity of concept. The warning is the metric that is zero by construction — Faithfulness is pre-set to zero for features below threshold and the composite correlations are then reported as if it were measured, which is the same family as the control-named-but-unreported problem the folder has flagged before. Mechanical rule: **no feature description in our account is credited on an activation-separation score alone; every label carries the four-way profile (generatable, responsive, pure, causal) on a printed sample with its chance level, and the causal component is computed for every label that is reported in a correlation rather than being skipped by a gate and then entered as zero.**
