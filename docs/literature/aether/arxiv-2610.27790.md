# Are Linguistic and Arithmetic Circuits Dissociated in LLMs?

- **Key:** arxiv-2610.27790
- **Authors / organisation:** Riku Kisako (Nagoya University), Tatsuki Kuribayashi (MBZUAI; Tohoku University), Ryohei Sasano (Nagoya University)
- **Date:** 2026-10-20
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27790
- **Kind:** paper (AACL-IJCNLP 2026 main)
- **Read from:** full text (arXiv HTML v1, ~68k clean chars: abstract, §1–§8 including limitations, references, Appendices A–F with the raw cross-task faithfulness matrices for all five models)
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
The paper borrows a question from neurolinguistics — are language and arithmetic handled by separate brain systems — and asks the same thing of language models: do their circuits for a linguistic task and an arithmetic task overlap, and can one stand in for the other? The authors' framing is that a model may develop an ability-based, modular organisation purely from statistical learning, with no architectural pressure toward it, and that in-silico circuit comparisons give more control over vocabulary, format and subtask than the human fMRI literature ever could. Their specific complaint against earlier LLM work of the same kind is granularity: prior studies compare circuits across broad task suites but do not ask whether a task's circuit survives a change of surface form, or whether a task's circuit includes its own subtasks. Arithmetic is chosen because the surface form is easy to vary (digits versus number words), the subtask boundary is easy to cut (calculate versus spell out versus decide precedence), and the syntactic parallel to natural language is easy to construct (nested parenthesised expressions versus nested noun phrases).

## What they studied
Five open-weight models — Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-1.7B, Llama-3.1-8B, Gemma-2-2B — and ten hand-built tasks with 500 clean/corrupted pairs each. The tasks are arranged in a deliberate lattice: two pure arithmetic tasks (digits and number words), three purely linguistic ones adopted from earlier formal/functional work (subject–verb agreement, gendered pronoun agreement, hypernym judgement), GSM-Plus word problems as a cross-domain reference, two expression-conversion tasks that use arithmetic input but require no computation (spelling an expression out and its inverse), and a matched pair of parsing tasks — operator precedence in an arithmetic expression versus the lowest constituent's head word in a short sentence. The unit of analysis is the **edge** of the computational graph (attention-head-to-head, head-to-MLP and similar directed messages), grouped into a task circuit by attribution score.

## How they did it
Every task circuit is extracted with Edge Attribution Patching with Integrated Gradients: gradients averaged over five interpolation steps between the clean and corrupted input, dotted with the sender's activation difference, giving one score per directed edge. For each model–task pair the top-scoring edges are kept, and the paper reports results under three boundaries to show nothing depends on that choice — top 2.5% of edges, top 5.0%, and an adaptive set defined as the smallest edge set whose self-faithfulness passes 0.85. Structural overlap is Intersection-over-Union of the two edge sets. Functional overlap is the authors' key move: to ask whether circuit B can do task A, they run the model on task A's clean input and patching in task A's own corrupted activations for every edge *outside* B, keeping B's edges clean — so the transferred object is B's edge mask, not any activation from task B — and read the normalised faithfulness, i.e. how much of the clean-versus-corrupted gap in task A's logit-difference metric is recovered. That matrix is then normalised column-wise by each target's own self-faithfulness, and the rows (each circuit's transfer profile over all ten tasks) are hierarchically clustered with Ward linkage to see which circuits behave alike. Model-to-model agreement of those dendrograms is scored with Adjusted Rand Index at several cut levels plus a Pearson correlation over cophenetic distances.

## What counts as an explanation here
A circuit — a sparse subgraph of the model's computational graph, honest about being partial, and made predictive in one specific direction: the claim being tested is not "this subgraph explains the task" but "this subgraph is sufficient for this task and, if it is really the task's mechanism, should not be sufficient for a different one". The dissociation claim is therefore a *negative prediction under intervention*, and the paper's strongest argument is the one it does not make with a picture: two circuits can share many edges and still be functionally separate, and two circuits can share few edges and still substitute for each other, so overlap alone is treated as non-diagnostic throughout.

## How they judged success
Two primary measures (IoU for structure, cross-task faithfulness for function) that are reported side by side rather than combined. Robustness is shown by re-running the whole analysis under the three circuit-size definitions and by an Appendix E re-scoring at the sequence level for the multi-token tasks. Generality is shown across five models and by clustering agreement (ARI per pair, over ten pairwise model comparisons, plus cophenetic correlations). Task accuracy on each of the ten tasks is reported first as a precondition — the tables are only interesting where the model can actually do the task.

## What they found
The dissociation is the headline and it holds across every model and every circuit-size definition: arithmetic circuits and linguistic circuits have low overlap and low cross-task faithfulness, and the authors report that this holds under the top-5% and adaptive boundaries too, getting clearer rather than weaker under the adaptive one. Three results carry more weight than the headline. First, **surface form does not determine the circuit** — a calculation in digits and the same calculation in number words produce circuits that are similar to each other and to neither the spelling tasks nor the word-problem circuits, so vocabulary is not what fixes the mechanism. Second, **instruction does** — the same arithmetic expressions, asked to be spelled out instead of computed, give a circuit that recovers most of the word-problem and spelling targets but little of the calculation target, and the calculation circuits fail the spelling targets just as badly. Third, **the parsing pair dissociates most sharply** even though it was constructed to be the case where cross-domain sharing would be most likely: the arithmetic-precedence circuit scores near zero on the sentence-parsing task and vice versa, in most models to within a rounding of zero, while both still recover a decent fraction of their own task. Cross-model clustering agreement is moderate — mean ARI mostly between about 0.45 and 0.63 depending on the cut level — with a strange shape in the appendix: the three Qwen models and Gemma agree with each other at cophenetic correlations of 0.95–0.98, while Llama-3.1-8B sits apart from all of them at 0.45–0.59, and agreement does not track family or size in any simple way. The arithmetic-precedence circuit also refuses to cluster cleanly with the arithmetic group in several models, which the authors read as evidence that models never learned that precedence is a subtask of calculation.

## Limits and open problems they name
Only one extraction method (EAP-IG); main results scored at the first answer token only, with a sequence-level re-evaluation in an appendix rather than a sequence-level attribution objective; all prompts English; the linguistic tasks heterogeneous; GSM-Plus accuracy low enough that the word-problem condition should be treated as a reference point rather than a result; a finite set of open-weight models; and the bridge back to the human literature left as an open question. The authors also note that low accuracy on GSM-Plus weakens the interpretation of anything involving it. The limits they do *not* name are the ones that matter for using this as a measurement design: there is no random-edge or shuffled-label circuit as a null; the faithfulness scores at the diagonal regularly exceed 1.0 (self-circuits scoring above the clean run, e.g. Llama's agreement circuit at 1.60 and Gemma's sentence-parsing circuit at 1.26), which the paper explains as small-baseline amplification but does not guard against; and the IoU numbers themselves are shown only as heatmap figures, so the structural half of every comparison is quoted nowhere as a number in the text.

## What we take from it
A method and a warning. The method is the cross-application protocol: hold the target task's inputs and its own corrupted activations fixed, transfer only the *mask* from the other circuit, and read recovered fraction of the clean-corrupted gap. That is the cheapest available test of whether a structure we recover is specific to the behaviour we claim it for, and it is stronger than an overlap statistic because it prices edges by what they do. The warning is the paper's own accident: they build a deliberately matched pair of parsing tasks specifically to give cross-domain sharing its best chance, and the pair turns out to be the most dissociated case in the suite — which is exactly what a reader would not predict, and the reason to construct the matched-hard pair *before* deciding what the dissociation result means. For our own batteries, three concrete carry-overs: (a) whenever we claim a component set is task-specific, we should publish the cross-application matrix and not just IoU, because the paper's discussion section is right that a few high-impact shared edges can make low overlap meaningless; (b) self-faithfulness above 1.0 must be a flagged condition in our reporting, not a footnote, because every cross-task number in the matrix is divided by it; (c) the appendix's model-disagreement pattern (four models agreeing at 0.95–0.98, one at 0.45–0.59) is a reminder to report per-model agreement rather than an average across models, since the average here would describe none of the five.
