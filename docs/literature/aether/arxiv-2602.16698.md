# Position: Causality is Key for Interpretability Claims to Generalise

- **Key:** arxiv-2602.16698
- **Authors / organisation:** Shruti Joshi (Mila / Université de Montréal); Aaron Mueller (Boston University); David Klindt (Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory); Wieland Brendel, Patrik Reizinger (MPI for Intelligent Systems / ELLIS Tübingen / Tübingen AI Center); Dhanya Sridhar (Mila / UdeM)
- **Date:** 2026-02-18
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16698
- **Kind:** position paper (with a small empirical pilot study appended)
- **Read from:** full text (arXiv HTML v1, ~165k chars read end to end: abstract, §1–§5, references, Appendices A–G incl. the notation glossary, the philosophical section, the technical review, the full ladder reference, the method-family estimand table, the claim taxonomy, and the pilot study with codebook and calibration rationales)
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

Interpretability routinely produces local successes that do not survive deployment: a probe reads a concept at high accuracy but steering its direction does not move behaviour; an ablation kills a behaviour on the test set and the circuit proves brittle under distribution shift. The authors argue that most of these failures are not experimental sloppiness but *claim–evidence mismatches* — the paper asserts a stronger kind of causal fact than the method could possibly have established. Their fix is terminological and formal rather than experimental: state every interpretability claim in the language causal inference already has — an estimand, an intervention class, an equivalence class, a transportability condition — so that what a result licenses and what it does not become explicit, comparable across papers, and checkable before deployment. The larger goal is a field-wide vocabulary in which "this head does X" is either a licensed claim or visibly an overreach.

## What they studied

No single model. The objects are **claims and methods**: five interpretability method families (feature attribution, probing/logit-lens, activation patching and circuit analysis, structured decomposition such as SAEs and PCA, and theory-grounded methods such as DAS and SSAEs), mapped onto Pearl's three-rung causal ladder (L1 associational / L2 interventional / L3 counterfactual). The unit of analysis is a sentence in a paper paired with the procedure that backs it. The empirical component is a pilot annotation study of **50 mechanistic-interpretability papers (186 claims)** drawn from NeurIPS, ICLR, ACL, EMNLP, AAAI, TMLR and arXiv preprints 2021–2026, spanning circuit discovery, knowledge localisation, SAEs, steering vectors, evaluation benchmarks and applied/production work — selection deliberately not formally stratified. Running example throughout: refusal.

## How they did it

Four moves. (1) **Ladder mapping.** Each interpretability question is placed on a rung — decodability from a probe or SAE attribution is L1; patching, ablation and steering are L2; necessity in a single instance is L3 — and the paper stresses that evidence on rung k never licenses a rung k+1 claim. (2) **Identifiability.** Having the right rung is not enough: the estimand must also be pinned down up to an equivalence class. They import causal representation learning, whose central results say unsupervised recovery of latents is impossible without extra structure, and read this as a statement about *what the available interactions afford*: you cannot separate sentiment from topic from activations unless your data contains settings where one varies without the other (their paired-sample SSAE example, where if natural perturbations always move sentiment and topic together, the identified quantity is neither). (3) **Diagnosis.** A claim–evidence gap is decomposed into *rung mismatch* (evidence is lower than the claim requires) and *identification gap* (right rung, but the target is only identified up to an equivalence class); five worked counterexamples are given — decodability→encoding, local sensitivity→global importance, existence→uniqueness, alignment→ontological identity, subspace→coordinates. (4) **Pilot calibration.** Claims were annotated by Claude Opus 4.5 inside Claude Code, with the arXiv API for retrieval, against a structured codebook of method→rung and linguistic-marker→rung mappings; gap score = max(0, claim rung − method rung); hedged phrasing ("may encode") was coded at the underlying claim's rung with reduced confidence, so the measure is *surface interpretive risk*, i.e. what a skimming reader or citing policymaker could take away, not author intent. Twelve of the 50 papers (43 of 186 claims) were fact-checked by a human against the original arXiv source.

## What counts as an explanation here

Not an explanation of any model — an **explanation standard**. The object the paper wants researchers to produce is a claim that carries its own licence: estimand stated; evidence rung declared; claim rung declared and shown ≤ evidence rung; the equivalence class named; the robustness dimensions varied (prompt template, seed, ablation strategy); and the scope bounded ("GPT-2 small, English subject–verb agreement" and not beyond). Their practitioners' checklist is the concrete artifact. It is explicitly partial: identifiability characterises what is invariantly recoverable *given the interactions performed*, not what exists in the model, so the framework tells you when a claim overreaches rather than what the model is doing.

## How they judged success

For the framework: argued by construction and by the worked counterexamples, with the pilot study as evidence that the problem is prevalent and not hypothetical. Metrics of the pilot: gap score per claim, distribution over gap sizes, gap rate overall and split by claim location (abstract vs body/results/intro) and by paper type, annotator confidence on a 1–5 scale, and a replication-status tag searched via Google Scholar, GitHub issues, Alignment Forum and author retrospectives. Controls and sensitivity checks: 12 papers fact-checked against source (84% of verified claims needed no correction; the corrections were mostly claim-location errors and two method misclassifications where interventional components were missed); a **conservative sensitivity check** reclassifying all 12 claims whose gap rests only on definite articles or conventional verbs, which moves the headline rate from 53.5% to 47.0%; a note that claims are nested within papers (mean 3.7 each) so percentages are not independent observations. They state plainly that **no inter-rater reliability exists** and that the LLM annotator's own bias would propagate uniformly.

## What they found

Of 185 scorable claims, **53.5% had gap > 0**: the language admits a stronger causal reading than the method licenses. Most gaps are one rung — 76 claims (41.1%) at +1, typically activation patching narrated as mechanism — with 23 (12.4%) at +2, typically probing narrated as encoding; 86 (46.5%) had no gap. The gap is **not an abstract-writing artefact**: 53.3% in abstracts (72/135) against 52.9% elsewhere (27/51). By paper type, mean gap rate is highest for circuit discovery (0.64, 16 papers) and knowledge localisation (0.50, 3), then "other" (0.47, 19), evaluation/benchmark (0.45, 5) and applied/production (0.35, 7). Annotator confidence falls monotonically with gap size — mean 4.94 for gap-free claims (95.3% at level 5), 4.39 at +1, 4.04 at +2 — and rung-1 methods (probing, SAE attribution) attract lower confidence than rung-2 ones (4.33 vs 4.73), which the authors partly attribute to the annotator's own internal uncertainty rather than to judgment difficulty. Only 3 of the 99 gap-positive claims (3.0%) had a note flagging an explicit hedge in the source paper. Two named **positive calibration anchors** show the target prose style: an SAE-evaluation paper whose verbs describe what the method does ("struggles", "mediates") rather than what the model does internally, and a production-probing paper where the absence of mechanistic vocabulary ("encodes", "represents", "computes") removes the main source of rung elevation. Three anonymous anchors illustrate the recurring elevation patterns: circuit discovery → mechanistic narrative via definite articles and functional verbs; knowledge localisation → storage language ("stores", "contains"); algorithm reverse-engineering → "fully reverse-engineering", in one case a work whose findings were independently replicated, which is the point — **elevated language can coexist with strong evidence**, and the rung distance measures convention, not weakness.

## Limits and open problems they name

The pilot is a pilot: 50 papers, single LLM annotator, no inter-rater reliability, paper list unpublished, annotation data withheld until authors have a review period, and the LLM's own flagging bias unmeasured. The binary rung scheme conflates naming convention with commitment — a paper that writes "THE circuit" in its abstract but qualifies "other circuits may exist" in the discussion scores exactly like one that never qualifies — and the paper says a within-paper qualification flag would be needed to separate them. Hedging is captured only as reduced confidence, not as a lower claim rung. Prospective validation of the framework is explicitly future work: they do not show that claim–evidence alignment *predicts* which findings replicate, only that misalignment is widespread and diagnosable. They also concede that extensive empirical robustness testing partially covers the same ground, and that their framework's contribution there is to say which distribution shifts should bite. My addition, marked as mine: the two calibration anchors are named while the three elevation exemplars are anonymised, so the positive examples are checkable and the negative ones are not, which weakens the pilot as an audit even though the reasoning for anonymising is sound.

## What we take from it

- **A template for our own write-ups.** Every entry we produce should carry an estimand, a declared rung, and a named equivalence class. The five counterexample patterns — decodability≠encoding, local≠global, existence≠uniqueness, alignment≠identity, subspace≠coordinate — are exactly the ways a white-box model of a black-box network can overclaim, and they read as a checklist for the paper rather than as background.
- **The distinction that matters most for us is L2 vs L3.** Standard activation patching performs the *action* step of abduction–action–prediction but supplies neither the abduction that fixes exogenous factors nor the structural model needed to propagate — which is precisely why patching is not a predictor of what the model *would* have done, only a report of what it *did* under one edit. A predictive white-box model is by construction an attempt to close that gap, and this paper gives the vocabulary for saying so precisely and for stating what would have to be assumed (which quantities are held fixed) for it to hold.
- **Identifiability-as-affordance is the sharpest available caution about our probing contract.** What our probes can recover is bounded by the variations our prompt set affords; if the contrast we construct always co-varies two factors, the direction we recover is a blend and no amount of downstream intervention will untangle it. This is a construction-time rule, not a post-hoc criticism, and it is testable cheaply: check whether the contrast set contains cases where each factor moves alone.
- **Contrast-indexed labelling.** A feature's label is a property of the pair (direction, contrast set), not of the direction — so every label we emit should be reported as (z, c, π). This is the formal version of the recurring note in this folder that a feature's base-model meaning and its post-training role can differ.
- **A field-wide number to cite carefully:** 53.5% of claims in 50 papers admit a stronger reading than their evidence licenses. The authors themselves insist this is not an indictment of the findings, and their own sensitivity check moves it to 47.0%. Use it as evidence that the vocabulary is missing, not that the literature is wrong.
