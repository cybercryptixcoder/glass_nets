# Sensitivity and Generalization in Neural Networks: an Empirical Study

- **Key:** arxiv-1802.08760
- **Authors / organisation:** Roman Novak, Yasaman Bahri, Daniel A. Abolafia, Jeffrey Pennington, Jascha Sohl-Dickstein — Google Brain (Bahri under the Google Brain Residency programme)
- **Date:** 2018-06-18 (v3; v1 2018-02-23)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1802.08760
- **Kind:** paper (ICLR 2018 conference paper)
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

The field's classical complexity measures say large models should generalize worse, and observation says the opposite: in their own sweep of thousands of networks the best test performance came from a model with roughly four orders of magnitude more parameters than the smallest one that could fit the data. The authors want a complexity notion that is defined *on the learned function around the data* rather than on the size of the parameter count, so that the tension dissolves instead of being explained away. Their motivating analogy is a one-dimensional one: a cubic-sine function looks far more complex than the identity, but restricted to a narrow interval around zero it is nearly flat while the identity stays linear — complexity, they argue, has to be assessed on the input domain that matters. The larger goal is model selection: a number computable from a trained network, without labels and without training a second model, that ranks networks by how well they will generalize. The paper is deliberately an empirical study over a huge hyperparameter space, not a theory paper — it cites a companion bound but offers no bound of its own.

## What they studied

Fully connected networks without biases, on image classification: MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, CIFAR10 and CIFAR100 (the CIFAR images cropped to a 24x24 centre). Scale is the point — one large experiment trains 335,671 networks with random hyperparameters over five activation functions (ReLU, ReLU6, Tanh, HardTanh, HardSigmoid), five optimizers (SGD, momentum, Adam, RMSProp, L-BFGS), three learning rates, two batch sizes, four initialisation scales, widths from 1 to 2^16 and depths from 2 to 65, true and random labels, and seeds 1-8; a smaller grid has 2,160 networks at 100% train accuracy on CIFAR10. The **unit of analysis is the whole function**, not any internal object: the two read-outs are properties of the input-output map of a trained network, measured at points in input space. Except where stated, only networks that reach 100% training accuracy are kept, so trainability and expressivity are factored out by construction and what is left to explain is generalization alone.

## How they did it

Two sensitivity measures are defined for a piecewise-linear network treated as a piecewise-linear map of input space. **(1) Jacobian norm:** the Frobenius norm of the Jacobian of the *class probabilities* — softmax composed with the logits — with respect to the input, averaged over test points; the paper notes that the logit Jacobian was tried and was less predictive, and that the quantity needs no labels since it is a derivative. It is argued to be the average-case sensitivity because for a small isotropic Gaussian input perturbation the expected squared change in output equals the perturbation variance times the squared Frobenius norm. **(2) Transitions:** each unit is assigned a code naming which linear piece its pre-activation sits on (0/1 for ReLU, 0/1/2 for ReLU6), the concatenation of codes identifies the linear region, and the number of code changes along a closed one-dimensional trajectory through input space counts how often the region changes — a first-order stand-in for curvature. Trajectories are made to stay near the data by interpolating horizontal translations of a test image, or by fitting an ellipse through three anchor points. The experiments then ladder in generality: (a) measure both metrics along three kinds of trajectory — random ellipse, ellipse through three anchor points of *different* class, ellipse through three of the *same* class — to compare behaviour on and off the data manifold; (b) train *pairs* of networks identical except for one binary factor known to matter (true versus random labels, with versus without translation augmentation, ReLU versus HardSigmoid, mini-batch versus full-batch) and plot each pair's two generalization gaps and two sensitivities as a scatter against the diagonal; (c) sweep everything at once over the 335,671-network space and correlate Jacobian norm with generalization gap per dataset; (d) ask whether the Jacobian norm predicts the *individual* test point's loss. An appendix derives approximate bounds relating the full Jacobian norm to per-point cross-entropy loss.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **scalar function of the trained weights**, computable on unlabelled inputs, that predicts held-out performance — plus an interpretive frame for it (a prior that natural-image classifiers should be robust near the data, i.e. a sensitivity-based Occam's razor). It is explicitly partial: no claim about which internal units, features or circuits produce the sensitivity, and no attempt to predict the network's output; the prediction target is a generalization quantity, never an activation or a behaviour on an input. The paper is careful about what its correlation means — it argues in an appendix that the reasoning that makes *parameter count* a bad Occam factor does not rescue it just by being Bayesian, and conjectures that big networks simply have access to a larger volume of robust solutions while small ones are overconstrained into extreme weights; that conjecture is offered as conjecture, not as a result, and the authors say plainly it does not explain *why* large networks converge to less sensitive functions.

## How they judged success

Correlation between a sensitivity measure and a generalization quantity, judged across several designs of increasing strength: within-pair consistency (the scatter should sit off the diagonal in the predicted direction, for every one of thousands of pairs), cross-dataset monotonicity of Jacobian norm against generalization gap, and per-point predictive power (Jacobian norm versus individual cross-entropy loss). Controls are the matched pairs themselves — identical architecture, optimiser and seed, one factor flipped — and the on/off-manifold contrast, where the same metric is measured along trajectories that do and do not pass near data. Seeds 1-8 with standard-error bars on every figure except the two per-point ones; generalization numbers over full 50,000/10,000 train/test sets; trajectories sampled at 2^20 points. The competing metric (transitions) is carried through the same suite as a built-in negative control. There is no held-out split of the *correlation* itself, no formal statistical test of the correlation coefficients, and no evaluation on any architecture other than a fully connected one.

## What they found

**On versus off the manifold is the sharpest result:** both metrics drop markedly near training data and stay roughly uniform along a random ellipse, so the learned function is much more stable where the data is; after training, the pre-logits layer's region boundaries become strongly anisotropic with the data points sitting in low-transition-density regions. **Every one of the four binary factors moved sensitivity in the direction the generalization gap predicted** as measured by Jacobian norm — random labels raise it, augmentation lowers it, ReLU lowers it relative to HardSigmoid, mini-batch training lowers it relative to full-batch; transitions matched on three of the four and failed on the optimiser comparison. The ReLU result is the one they flag as counter-intuitive, since unbounded activations might have been expected to be the fragile choice. **Across the large sweep**: 335,671 networks, Jacobian norm tracks generalization gap on all four datasets — the paper's headline figure. **Transitions do not**: on 2,160 CIFAR10 networks and 2,097 CIFAR100 networks at (near) 100% train accuracy the transition count stops correlating once network size varies, because unit count mechanically inflates the count — so of the two instruments only one survives the wide sweep, which is exactly why the paper keeps it as its second read-out. **Per-point prediction is real but non-monotone-injective**: Jacobian norm at a test point tracks its cross-entropy loss through an analytic relation derived in the appendix (the target-class Jacobian norm is bounded above and below by expressions in the loss of the form e^-l (1 - e^-l) times a constant), and high-Jacobian points are mostly misclassified, but some misclassified points have Jacobian norms orders of magnitude *smaller* than correctly classified ones, so the map from sensitivity to loss is not bijective. The authors point to active learning and confidence estimation as the natural uses.

## Limits and open problems they name

Fully connected networks only, image classification only — the authors say explicitly that extending to convolutional or other architectures, and to tasks without an image-like robustness prior (they name weather forecasting), is left for future work. The interpretive Occam's-razor story is a hypothesis they cannot test, and they state the part that is actually missing: no explanation of why large networks converge to less sensitive functions. Transitions are reported as an inferior instrument rather than dropped. Speed is the reason the study is fully connected — the two metrics are expensive per network, and the large sweep additionally sampled small configurations more heavily than large ones for compute reasons, which they argue but cannot prove is harmless. The per-point experiments use a single seed. No formal bound is proved; the appendix's loss-Jacobian relation is an approximation resting on an assumption that logit derivative magnitudes are roughly uniform across logits.

## What we take from it

- **A label-free, fit-free read-out we can add to our battery tomorrow.** The softmax-probability Jacobian norm needs only forward and backward passes on unlabelled inputs, has no probe capacity to choose, no class balance to match and no null hypothesis to calibrate — and it is a *stability* measurement to sit beside our separation measurements. Same instrument `arxiv-2007.04440` uses as its stability metric, read here at source.
- **The measurement must say where in input space it was taken.** The on/off-manifold contrast means a single global average of any sensitivity statistic mixes two regimes with very different values; the transferable rule is to report sensitivity on the data manifold and off it separately, which for us means along interpolations of real examples versus random directions in activation space.
- **Matched-pair design as the standard for any "property X affects behaviour" claim.** Comparing networks that share architecture, optimiser and seed and differ in exactly one factor removes essentially all confounds for free; it is the cheapest causal design in this folder and it is what made the four-factor result believable.
- **A caution about instrument cost versus size:** the weaker instrument (transitions) looked fine in the paired comparisons and broke down only when network size varied across the sweep. Any statistic that can be inflated mechanically by the number of units, layers or directions has to be validated on a size-varying population, not on a matched-pair one, before it is used to compare models of different scale.
- **An example of the right hedging:** the paper says explicitly that this is a correlation in image classification and that it rests on a prior it cannot justify for other domains. Our prediction claims should carry the same scoping sentence.
