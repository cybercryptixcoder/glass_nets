# Many Circuits, One Mechanism: Input Variation and Evaluation Granularity in Circuit Discovery

- **Key:** arxiv-2606.06267
- **Authors / organisation:** Alireza Bayat Makou, Jingcheng Niu, Subhabrata Dutta, Iryna Gurevych; UKP Lab, Technical University of Darmstadt (Gurevych also ATHENE)
- **Date:** 2026-06-04
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06267
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Circuit discovery papers routinely treat a recovered subgraph as *the* circuit for a task, and treat structural differences between two recovered circuits as evidence of two different mechanisms. The authors ask whether that inference survives a change that leaves the task untouched: vary only the statistics of the input tokens. If a discovery algorithm returns different edges for the same computation, then structure does not identify mechanism, and every structural comparison in the field needs a functional test attached. The larger goal is a defensible reading of what a circuit claim licenses — which is exactly the question a predictive white-box model has to answer before it can say which components a prediction depends on.

## What they studied
Five Pythia models (70M, 160M, 410M, 1B, 1.4B) on Literal Sequence Copying, a non-semantic induction task: a five-token source prefix, a target token, ten distractors, then the prefix repeated, and the model must emit the target. The manipulated axis is token frequency in The Pile: the vocabulary is split into four length-matched, lowercase-only frequency bands (703 tokens per band after filtering; a fifth near-empty band is dropped) plus a frequency-weighted control drawn from all four, which makes five conditions. Three independent draws per condition give 5 models × 5 conditions × 3 draws = 75 circuits. The unit of analysis is the **edge** of the computational graph (attention heads and MLP blocks as nodes, residual-stream reads as edges), evaluated at two granularities.

## How they did it
Circuits are extracted with ACDC through the AutoCircuit library (chosen after the original codebase showed metric collapse on Pythia; EAP/EAP-IG were rejected because top-k gradient ranking can yield disconnected edge sets, scoring 2–15% of base accuracy at matched size versus 79–99% for ACDC). Corruption for patching replaces the repeated prefix with random same-band tokens, destroying the repetition signal while holding everything else fixed. The pruning threshold is chosen once per model on the **control** band only, by sweeping eleven log-spaced values and taking a Pareto point on edge fraction versus KL, and then applied unchanged to all five conditions — so no band gets its own threshold tuning. Each circuit is scored on four standard metrics (faithfulness as KL to the base model, sufficiency as accuracy, necessity as accuracy when the complement is ablated, minimality as edge fraction), against 100 size-matched random edge sets. Structural comparison is pairwise Jaccard over edge sets, within-band (different draws) versus between-band, with bootstrap intervals and a calibration against the spread of cross-draw Jaccard. Functional comparison is a 5×5 cross-band transfer matrix, plus a band-specific-edge boost test: take the edges unique to one condition, add them to the shared core, and measure the boost on their own band against the boost on the other four (the ratio is "transfer efficiency"). The falsification battery then adds per-example agreement, an independent EAP-IG replication (whose circuits overlap ACDC at Jaccard 0.28–0.60), adjacent-threshold sensitivity, a zero-ablation rerun, representational similarity (residual CKA, MLP selectivity, attention entropy, band-identity mutual information), and finally causal interventions: interchange patching at the prediction position and Boundless DAS.

## What counts as an explanation here
A sparse subgraph, deliberately defined as an operational and method-relative object: the set of edges whose preservation under a specified intervention recovers task performance. The paper's contribution is negative and structural — it shows what such a subgraph does **not** pin down. It is explicitly not complete, not unique, and the authors argue the right object is a distribution over valid subgraphs rather than a point estimate. It is predictive in only one narrow sense: the recovered edge set predicts the model's behaviour under resample ablation of the complement, and that prediction is close to vacuous at coarse granularity.

## How they judged success
Structural: Jaccard gaps with bootstrap CIs, containment asymmetry, per-edge sharing counts κ∈{1..5}, and a power analysis (2–5 draws needed to detect gaps of the observed size). Functional: edge-level accuracy and retention, transfer efficiency as a ratio of boosts with 95% bootstrap CIs, Cohen's d and permutation tests for same-versus-cross advantage, per-example agreement rates, and a Holm-free BH-FDR correction across the whole battery. Representational: CKA, selective-probe layer sweeps, attention entropy, mutual information, all with within-model versus pooled correlations kept separate. Causal: Interchange Intervention Accuracy at the peak layer, over all 25 band pairs, plus a layer-sweep positive control that has IIA 0 at early layers and 0.92–0.98 at the peak; and Boundless DAS for the effective dimension of whatever distinguishes the bands.

## What they found
First, the appearance of specialization is real and reproducible. Within-band Jaccard exceeds between-band in all five models by 0.013–0.032, and low-frequency circuits are systematically larger (Pythia-1.4b: 3.2% of edges at low versus 2.5% at very high). Edges unique to one condition are 5–263× more common than a noise-only null predicts, so ACDC really is sensitive to the input distribution. Cross-band transfer is also asymmetric: Pythia-1.4b's low-band circuits reach 92.1% on high-band data while the reverse direction gets 71.7%.

Then the specialization evaporates under functional tests. The band-specific edges boost other bands nearly as much as their own: transfer efficiency 81.4% (Pythia-70m) to 97.0% (Pythia-1.4b), versus 1–3% for random edges of the same count. The residual same-band advantage is a flat 0.016–0.029 accuracy points across all scales even as the generic boost grows fivefold — a fixed offset, not growing specialization — and it disappears entirely under zero ablation (Cohen's d falls from 0.22–0.71 to ≤0.18). Band-specific edges alone achieve 0% accuracy. Relaxing from "shared by all five conditions" to "shared by at least three" recovers ≥99% of full circuit accuracy for models above 70M, and those k≥3 edge sets are themselves band-agnostic (Jaccard 0.656 versus 0.366 for the full circuits in the 1.4B model). The strict universal core contains the canonical copy mechanism: previous-token heads enriched at odds ratio 3.7–14.4, induction heads universal in the larger models, BOS-sink heads depleted, and its logit-lens trajectory correlates r=0.77–0.99 with the full circuit while peaking 0.5–3.3 layers later at 1.6–3.1× lower amplitude — the same algorithm, attenuated.

Third, extraction noise is structurally large and functionally irrelevant. On Pythia-1.4b only 21–27% of edges appear in all three draws on the same band, yet cross-draw transfer ratios run 0.990–1.017 with every CI spanning one. This bounds the inert fraction of any single discovered circuit at roughly half or more of its edges.

Fourth, methodology hides all of it. Source-level evaluation (keeping all outgoing edges from any node that contributed at least one circuit edge) inflates apparent accuracy above the edge-level number by 0.22 (70M) to 0.85 (1.4B) points, and the inflation grows with scale — precisely where the phantom is strongest. Variance decomposition attributes the dominant share of every metric to model identity, not band (93.1% versus 2.6% for circuit accuracy), and pooling across models produces Simpson's paradox in 52/80 structure–function metric pairs and 68/80 structure–representation pairs.

Fifth, the representational and causal checks agree. Pruning preserves the base model's geometry (residual CKA ≥0.93, MLP selectivity ρ=0.84–0.99), band-identity mutual information loses under 10% under the circuit, and interchange patching gives IIA ≥0.94 across all 20 cross-band pairs for models ≥160M with a same-versus-cross difference of at most 0.004. Boundless DAS localizes band-distinguishing information to 12–31 dimensions, 0.6–6.1% of the width, shrinking with scale and symmetric in both directions — a subspace that is neither necessary nor sufficient for the copy computation. Frequency does modulate dynamics: convergence is later for rare tokens, with the fractional delay growing from about 0.06 to 0.29 of depth, robust to tuned-lens calibration. So the model does something different with rare tokens; it just does it inside the same circuit.

## Limits and open problems they name
The single biggest hole is stated plainly: **no within-task positive control for the full pipeline was constructible.** A reverse-copy variant (target before prefix, requiring offset −5) fails at 0.0% accuracy on Pythia-160m — the model copies offset +1 in 94.2% of cases, so its induction heads are wired forward only, and no distinct mechanism could be elicited. A zero-distractor variant is solved at 88.4% but uses the same circuit, transferring at 96.7% with lower structural overlap (Jaccard 0.539 versus 0.557) — phantom specialization reproduced on a second axis, but still no ground truth for "what genuine specialization would look like". The layer-sweep positive control validates interchange patching's sensitivity, not the ACDC→structural→transfer pipeline. Other limits: one model family, one non-semantic task, ACDC as primary method, three draws (enough for functional but not structural precision; 736 GPU-hours total), Pythia-70m a boundary case at 30–67% base accuracy where several metrics diverge, and the confound that character length is length-matched as a proxy even though it is invisible to the model at token level. Whether feature-level circuits over SAEs or transcoders show the same non-uniqueness is left open, as is learning whether gradient-descent redundancy guarantees it.

## What we take from it
- **Warning, and the sharpest one in the folder for circuit claims:** structural difference is not evidence of functional difference. Any two circuits we compare — across conditions, checkpoints, seeds, methods — need a cross-condition transfer matrix before the word "specialization" appears.
- **Method:** transfer efficiency is a cheap, interpretable statistic (ratio of cross-condition to same-condition boost from the condition-specific components). It converts a vague "are these the same mechanism" into a number with a stated null of 1.0.
- **Measure to import:** always report edge-level, never source-level. Source-level evaluation is a permissive test that lets unselected pathways carry the computation, and its inflation grows with model scale, so a scaled-up comparison will look more faithful while being less informative.
- **Method:** multiple extractions with majority-vote aggregation. Given that 21–27% cross-draw edge stability coexists with 0.99 transfer ratios, a single extraction's edge set is a biased sample of an equivalence class, and the inert fraction should be stated as a reported quantity rather than left implicit.
- **Framing for our predictor:** the object to predict is the *function*, and the circuit is one of many graphs that realize it. This is the structural counterpart of the non-identifiability result for directions — there, a vector is recovered up to a null space; here, an edge set is recovered up to an equivalence class — and both say the right target for a predictive model is a behavioural contract, not a canonical artifact.
- **Diagnostic worth copying wholesale:** rerun the same contrast under a second ablation rule. The residual same-band advantage here is a resample-ablation artifact that vanishes under zero ablation; that pattern (an effect that exists under one corruption and not another) is a general test for whether a small effect is computation or corruption geometry.
