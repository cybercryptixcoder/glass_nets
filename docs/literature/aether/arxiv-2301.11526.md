# Direct Parameterization of Lipschitz-Bounded Deep Networks

- **Key:** arxiv-2301.11526
- **Authors / organisation:** Ruigang Wang, Ian R. Manchester; Australian Centre for Field Robotics, The University of Sydney
- **Date:** 2023-01-27
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.11526
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Version read:** arXiv v1 (the abs page lists a later online date of 2023-06-06)
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

Certifying that a network is γ-Lipschitz had become a solved convex problem for a fixed set of weights — write the slope-restriction of the activation as an incremental quadratic constraint with a positive diagonal multiplier, stack all hidden layers into one block matrix, and ask a semidefinite program whether the resulting three-by-three block inequality holds. Training *under* that condition is a different and much harder problem, because the inequality constrains the weights themselves and the feasible set is badly non-convex; the existing answers (alternating directions with an SDP in the inner loop, barrier terms, projections) were described by the authors' own related-work survey as a computational bottleneck at roughly a thousand neurons. The goal of this paper is therefore not a tighter bound but the same bound without the solver: a smooth map from free coordinates onto the feasible set, so that ordinary stochastic gradient descent stays inside by construction. The larger ambition is that this makes Lipschitz-bounded training a standard layer choice rather than a convex-optimisation project, and the paper is explicit that its parameterisation is meant to target the *best known* certificate rather than an easier weaker one.

## What they studied

Fully connected networks of the standard recursive form and, separately, small 2-D convolutional classifiers. The unit of analysis is the whole stacked hidden-state vector: all hidden layers are concatenated into one state and the network is written as a single nonlinear map of that state, so the certificate is one matrix inequality over the entire depth rather than one per layer. The free objects are a positive diagonal scaling per boundary (one number per unit per layer, exponentiated), and for each boundary a pair of matrices obtained through the Cayley transform of a freely chosen block — the pair is constrained only so that its two Cayley images are jointly an isometry on the boundary space. Experiments use MNIST with small fully connected nets (~300k parameters) and CIFAR-10 with a KW-large network (~3M parameters), plus two one-dimensional curve-fitting problems.

## How they did it

Step one: restate the certificate. With the stacked formulation the block matrix has a chordal structure — each layer's weights couple only two adjacent blocks — and any semidefinite matrix with a chordal structure can be written as a sum of small blocks supported on its cliques. Step two: factor the certificate as a product of a lower bidiagonal matrix with itself transposed, so that the certificate becomes a set of algebraic identities: each diagonal block is the sum of two squares, and each off-diagonal block is a product of the two matrices that straddle the boundary. Step three: read the weights off the factorisation. The weight of layer k becomes a scaled product of two matrices, one belonging to the boundary objects on either side — this is where the paper's "interlacing" appears: a single weight depends on the parameters of two adjacent boundaries, so free coordinates are not per-layer. Step four: parameterise the boundary objects so the identities hold automatically, using an exponential map for the diagonal scalings and the Cayley transform for the matrices, since the Cayley image of an antisymmetric matrix is orthogonal and the transform maps the whole free space onto the orthogonal group minus one eigenvalue. Step five: prove that this is not merely sufficient. Given any weights and multipliers satisfying the certificate, the construction can be inverted: read the diagonal factor off the multiplier, compute the off-diagonal factor from the weight, and obtain the remaining factor as a Cholesky factor times an arbitrary orthogonal matrix chosen to avoid the eigenvalue the Cayley transform excludes. Step six: rewrite the network in a second coordinate system in which the composition is a chain of standalone nonexpansive layers (each layer is a scaled linear map, an activation, and a scaled adjoint linear map), so parameters no longer couple across adjacent layers and the layer can be shipped as a module; prove each such layer is nonexpansive by exhibiting the one-block instance of the same certificate. Step seven: carry the construction to convolutions by making the two linear maps convolutional, using circular (doubly block-circulant) operators so that the large structured inverse the Cayley transform needs becomes a batch of small complex matrix inverses under a 2-D Fourier transform, one per frequency bin, with the channel scaling applied identically at each pixel. Step eight: train unconstrained with Adam, cross-entropy and a piecewise triangular learning rate, against a semi-orthogonal-layer baseline and a plain network with matched parameter counts, and measure test error, an empirical lower Lipschitz bound from a projected-gradient-like search, and robustness under L2 perturbations.

## What counts as an explanation here

A parameterisation and a certificate, not an explanation of behaviour. The object delivered is a recipe plus a proof: weights are a closed-form function of free coordinates, and every point in the image satisfies the certificate, with the converse also proved, so the image is exactly the feasible set of the best-known convex relaxation. There is a second, more interesting object: the layer-form rewriting gives each layer its own nonexpansive certificate, which means every boundary carries a diagonal scaling and an isometric pair that *together* certify the layer. These are per-layer quantities with a guarantee attached. The paper uses them only to argue expressivity and to make the code modular; it never asks what a fitted scaling means, whether a large entry in a scaling marks a unit operating near saturation, or whether the isometric pair's alignment across boundaries describes coupling between layers. The explanation is complete with respect to the certificate and empty with respect to computation.

## How they judged success

Three measures, all empirical. First, tightness of the imposed bound, reported as the ratio of an empirical lower Lipschitz bound (from a PGD-like search) to the prescribed γ, on two curve-fitting tasks where the answer is known, with a semi-orthogonal baseline and a plain network of matched parameter count. Second, classification: test error and accuracy under a swept L2 perturbation on MNIST and CIFAR-10, mean and standard deviation over five runs, for sandwich layers at three prescribed bounds against orthogonal layers at three prescribed bounds and an unconstrained network. Third, cost: training wall-clock per epoch and epochs-to-match-baseline for the three models on both datasets. There is no ablation of the individual ingredients (the diagonal scaling, the extra parameter block, the interlacing), no measurement of how often training lands near the edge of the feasible set, and no comparison against the SDP-at-every-step method on the same task — the cost claim is made against the general class, not against a measured baseline.

## What they found

On the curve-fitting tasks the bound is nearly attained: on the square wave the empirical bound is within a fraction of a percent of the prescribed one at all three settings (roughly 99% across γ = 1, 2, 5), where the semi-orthogonal baseline reaches roughly three quarters of the prescribed value; on the multi-sine fit the sandwich model lands near 98% against roughly 61%. The paper's diagnostic here is sharper than its headline: per-layer, *both* architectures are tight (about 98% per layer), and the gap opens only when the bounds are composed over depth — which is the paper's argument that accounting for coupling between neighbouring layers, rather than multiplying layer norms, is what buys the tightness. The same figure reports that the product of spectral norms for the sandwich model reaches about 122 while the true bound is 1. On CIFAR-10 the sandwich model reaches substantially lower test error than both the semi-orthogonal model and the unconstrained CNN at every prescribed bound, with errors falling as the bound is loosened, and robustness improving monotonically as the bound is tightened. Cost: training an epoch takes roughly twice a vanilla model, but the model passes the vanilla model's final error in fewer than half the epochs in both settings, so the wall-clock to a given accuracy is comparable or better. One incidental observation the authors flag as novel: the unconstrained models show epoch-wise double descent while both Lipschitz-bounded models improve test error monotonically.

## Limits and open problems they name

The convolutional construction is restricted to circular, unstrided convolutions, with striding handled by an emulation borrowed from the semi-orthogonal layer paper and non-circular (padded) convolutions left to future work — so the convolutional half of the completeness theorem covers a class of kernels narrower than what real CNNs use. The completeness proof itself has small technical exclusions: the inverse construction requires an orthogonal factor chosen to avoid one eigenvalue, and the case of a zero or rank-deficient intermediate factor is patched by resetting it to the identity or substituting a pseudo-inverse, with the rank deficiency handled by a Moore–Penrose step rather than by a clean argument. The paper requires the activation to be slope-restricted in the unit interval, which excludes activations like GELU and is stated as an assumption rather than tested. And the whole account is a certificate, so it bounds sensitivity and says nothing about what the network does.

## What we take from it

Four things, the first two methodological. **First, the missing half of the constraint head's exactness ledger is supplied here and it is cheap to state**: this is the paper that the 1-D Cayley-plus-Gramian member cites for "equivalent to the necessity-and-sufficiency result", and the proof's second direction is a recursive inversion five lines long — read the diagonal factor off the multiplier, the off-diagonal factor off the weight, the rest from a Cholesky factor. That means the family's asymmetry is not a theorem-level obstacle but an effort asymmetry, and any construction we import should be tested for invertibility before being written off as a sampler. **Second, a readable accounting object appears**: the certificate is decomposed as a sum of small blocks supported on adjacent-layer cliques, so the *contribution* of each boundary to the whole-network certificate is a separate positive semidefinite block with explicit entries. That is a finer attribution instrument than a single slack number per layer, and it is computable from weights alone. **Third, a diagnostic we should copy in our own reporting**: the per-layer and per-network tightness are reported as two different numbers, and the gap between them is the paper's whole argument — a layer-wise bound composed across depth is loose even when every layer is tight, so any composition claim in our account must be reported at both scales. **Fourth, a warning that recurs across this head**: the completeness claim is proved for the stacked formulation and for circular convolutions, and the completeness of the layer-form rewriting (the one users would actually deploy) merely follows from the same identities — the modular, shippable version of an exact claim is stated as a corollary and inherits the preconditions silently.
