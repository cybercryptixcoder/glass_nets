# UNIDE: Cross-PDE Learning for Noise-Robust Discovery of Governing Equations

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.20678
- **Authors / organisation:** Xiaoyu Xie (Independent Researcher)
- **Date:** 2026-11-16
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20678
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Classical equation discovery — sparse regression over a candidate library, or symbolic regression — refits a fresh model for every observed space–time field, and it does so on features built from numerical derivatives, which amplify any observation noise. The authors' premise is that governing PDEs are assembled from a small recurring vocabulary (advection, diffusion, dispersion, reaction) and that each mechanism leaves a characteristic signature in a space–time field, so identification can be treated as a transferable inference problem instead of a per-system optimisation. The larger goal is a model that has already learned what those signatures look like across many systems and can therefore read a new, heavily corrupted trajectory once. They frame the contribution as joining two lines of work that had run separately: cross-system learning, and noise robustness.

## What they studied
Six one-dimensional periodic PDE families with known term structures: advection, diffusion, advection–diffusion, Burgers, KdV (a stabilised variant with a fixed fourth-derivative term counted in the ground truth) and Kuramoto–Sivashinsky. Each family carries 20 coefficient settings × 20 trajectories = 2,400 simulated fields, each with its own random initial condition, generated with pseudo-spectral solvers on top of APEBench; 1,000 spatial grid points and 501 stored frames per trajectory. The unit of analysis is one **trajectory** (a single space–time field); a **system** is one equation observed through many trajectories, and a **family** is an equation form with free coefficients — three levels the paper fixes in a terminology table before any experiment. The candidate library has twelve terms; across all six families only five are ever active. The object to be recovered is two things at once: the support (which terms are present) and the coefficients.

## How they did it
Three stages, of which only the first two are learned and both are shared across families. **(1) Denoise.** A residual dilated convolutional network with circular spatial padding is trained with a blind-spot objective (Noise2Void) — a random fraction of space–time entries is masked, each masked entry is replaced by the value of a neighbouring pixel at a random small offset, and the loss is computed only at the masked entries against the noisy values — so no clean target is ever needed. Training crops draw a fresh noise realisation each step from a range up to the model's target level. **(2) Select terms.** The denoised field, its eleven non-constant library channels, time features and sampling metadata (grid spacing, time step, duration, domain length, point and frame counts) are stacked; a per-frame spatial CNN encodes each frame, time features are attached, a small transformer runs across frames, and attention pooling fused with a metadata embedding gives a trajectory representation whose term head emits one probability per library term. The hard support comes from a threshold tuned on validation F1. Critically, the encoder is trained **on denoised features**, not clean ones. **(3) Fit coefficients.** Coefficients are never predicted by the network: given the predicted support, a weak-form least-squares solve (WSINDy style) multiplies the PDE by smooth compactly supported test functions on ~400 random space–time subdomains and integrates by parts, so all derivatives up to fourth order move off the data and onto the test functions. Then greedy backward pruning removes a selected term while its removal raises the normalised weak-form residual by no more than a validation-tuned tolerance. One denoiser+encoder pair (a "specialist") is trained per target noise level; per-trajectory baselines are SINDy (pointwise STLSQ), WSINDy (weak form, coefficients re-solved on its own support) and PySR (genetic symbolic search, then a least-squares refit of the library terms appearing in the expression). Two test sets: 900 held-out trajectories of systems seen in training, and 360 trajectories of systems with larger coefficient norms than any training system. Noise levels run 1% to 80%, where a level of *p*% means a noise standard deviation of *p*/100 times that of the field.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **sparse symbolic structure plus fitted coefficients**: the output is a short list of named library terms and numbers. It is a complete, checkable description of the dynamics, not a partial account — and because the library is physical and small, the explanation is a *mechanism claim* (this system advects, that one disperses) rather than a description of a function. The architecture deliberately splits the two halves: learned components decide what is present, a classical least-squares solve decides how much. The paper is explicit that this split is load-bearing for interpretation — nothing neural touches a reported coefficient. It is predictive by construction: a recovered equation can be simulated beyond the observed window, which is exactly the property the authors say motivated the work, though they do not run that extrapolation test here.

## How they judged success
Four metrics, read in pairs because they fail differently: **exact match** (fraction of trajectories whose predicted support equals the truth — the primary metric), **Term F1** averaged per trajectory, **coefficient RMSE** and **coefficient relative error**, with both coefficient metrics computed only on the true active terms. That last choice is stated with its consequence in a worked example: a spurious extra term hurts the structure numbers but is invisible to coefficient error, whereas a missing true term hurts both, so a low coefficient error with low exact match is diagnostic of *over*-selection. Baselines have their thresholds tuned on noisy validation trajectories at each level. The main evidential move is a **matched-control table**: sparse regression in weak form is given the identical denoiser, the identical ten candidate terms and windows, identical residual pruning and the same full validation partition, so the only difference left is learned versus sparse term selection. Ablations then remove one component at a time (denoiser, denoised-feature training, weak form, pruning) and, separately, one auxiliary loss at a time. Robustness is checked three ways: five encoder seeds, three observation-noise draws at two levels, and transfer from a 10%-trained model to every other noise level with weights frozen and thresholds recalibrated per level from validation labels.

## What they found
Exact match is highest at every noisy level and the margin widens with noise — 0.866 at 1%, 0.960 at 10%, 0.924 at 80%, against WSINDy's 0.820 → 0.003 and a collapse to essentially zero for SINDy and PySR. Term F1 stays above 0.91 from 5% to 80% while WSINDy's falls from 0.789 to 0.413; at 1% the two are level (0.938 vs 0.941). On clean data the per-trajectory baselines are strong — PySR reaches 0.953 exact match — and UNIDE's clean variant gets 0.999, so the headline gap exists only under noise. Coefficient accuracy inverts the picture at low noise: WSINDy's weak solve is more accurate up to 5%, the two are close at 10% (4.99% vs 6.19% relative error, but the five-seed mean is 7.3%), and from 20% onward UNIDE is far ahead (8.04% vs 43.01% at 80%). The matched controls are the paper's real result: at 80% noise, weak STLSQ on the raw field scores 0.010 exact match, with the denoiser 0.484, with denoiser plus pruning 0.834, and with learned selection 0.924 — so denoising and pruning both help the classical selector substantially and neither closes the gap. Coefficient accuracy shows no consistent winner in those controls (UNIDE worse than matched sparse regression at 10% and under coefficient extrapolation, better at 80%), which the paper states rather than smooths. Ablating the denoiser drops exact match 0.960 → 0.667; training the encoder on clean features instead of denoised drops it to 0.393; replacing the weak form with pointwise least squares leaves exact match at 0.670 and takes relative coefficient error from 4.99% to 48.85%; removing pruning drops 0.960 → 0.670 and slightly *improves* coefficient error (4.73% vs 4.99%). The per-loss-term ablation is a clean negative: removing any one of the four auxiliary losses leaves exact match at or above the full objective (0.670 → 0.679–0.711 unpruned), though dropping the residual or family loss raises coefficient error. On held-out large-coefficient systems at 10% noise, exact match 0.906 against WSINDy's 0.494, but coefficient error is *worse* than WSINDy's (9.88% vs 4.98%), almost entirely because of Burgers. Inference is 0.45 s per trajectory, faster than WSINDy and PySR, with about 130 ms in the learned stages and the rest in the weak-form solve; fully frozen transfer from a 10%-trained model falls from 0.960 exact match at 10% noise to 0.489 at 80%, with Term F1 holding at 0.696 — structure transfers far better than coefficients.

## Limits and open problems they name
Every test equation shares one of the six term structures seen in training, all fields are synthetic, one-dimensional, periodic and fully observed. The authors concede that with known structures, choosing among family templates is a strong alternative, and their own diagnostic confirms it (fixed family-template support reaches 0.760–0.847 exact match); held-out *operator combinations* are the test that would separate template matching from genuine term-level inference, and they do not run it. Two failure modes are named: on Burgers the encoder substitutes the convolutional transport term for the advective one, which removal-only pruning cannot repair because it can only delete — most Burgers errors are exactly this family swap, and Burgers alone drags down the coefficient error in the extrapolation setting; and on KdV pruning deletes the small fixed stabilising term when its contribution falls inside the tolerance, on up to 29 of 150 trajectories. Pruning is described as a model-selection heuristic on the observed trajectory, not a certificate of correct physics. Each noise level uses its own specialist, and the noise-matched protocol is applied uniformly including at zero noise rather than chosen per level from test results.

## What we take from it
Three transfers, ranked. **(1) The matched-control ladder is the instrument, not the ablation table.** Giving a classical selector the same denoiser, the same candidate set, the same pruning and the same validation budget, and reporting the ladder raw → +denoise → +prune → +learned, attributes the advantage to one component instead of to the whole pipeline — our read-out comparisons should be structured as exactly this ladder rather than as "our method versus a baseline" with several differences at once. **(2) A decomposition of the headline into halves that fail differently, published with the reason it can fail.** Structure and coefficient error are reported separately, and the paper states the asymmetry in advance: over-selection is invisible to coefficient error, under-selection is visible in both, so the pair diagnoses the failure mode. Our probe batteries report one aggregate; splitting into "found the right thing" and "got it right" with a stated reason the two disagree is a one-table change. **(3) The auxiliary losses that carry no signal.** Removing any one of four auxiliary objectives left exact match at or above the full objective, i.e. the primary loss was doing all the work — a candidate explanation the authors do not pursue, and a reminder to ablate *before* claiming a multi-term objective is necessary. A fourth, smaller transfer: calibrated transfer and frozen transfer are reported as two distinct rows (0.747 vs 0.489 exact match at 80% noise for the same 10%-trained weights), which is the honest way to publish "the model generalises" when per-level threshold recalibration is doing some of the work.
