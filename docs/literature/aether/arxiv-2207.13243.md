## What they wanted, and why

The authors start from the gap between "passes a test set" and "understood": a model that scores well can still have learned a harmful bias, a brittle shortcut or a deliberately deceptive solution, and held-out data is a proxy that cannot search for problems nobody thought to write down. Their proposed remedy is *inner* interpretability — methods that characterise the internal components and representations of a network rather than its inputs, outputs or aggregate behaviour — because only a reading of the inside can support manual modification, reverse engineering, and the detection of latent knowledge that never shows up in deployment behaviour. They survey more than 300 works, organise them, and then argue that the field as practised is largely unproductive: it mistakes suggestive pictures for findings and does not produce tools an engineer would use. The deliverable is therefore both a map and an indictment, and the second half of the paper is a set of prescriptions for what an interpretability result should be.

## What they studied

The unit of analysis is explicitly plural — they taxonomise by *what part of the computational graph a method explains*: weights, individual neurons, subnetworks, and latent representations. Each of the four is then split by *when* the method runs, intrinsic (the model is trained to be readable, or carries a structure that is interpretable by construction) versus post hoc (the trained model is analysed afterwards), with a third category for methods that mix the two. The models discussed span convolutional classifiers, generative models and transformers up to language models; much of the literature they cite is small-scale and task-specific, and they treat that as a defect of the field rather than a scope choice. Data is whatever the surveyed methods used, so the survey's own "corpus" is the 327-item bibliography rather than any unified experiment.

## How they did it

There is no experiment; the method is taxonomic and argumentative, and it is worth describing because the taxonomy does real work. First, the four graph-level targets are laid out in order, and under each the intrinsic and post hoc families are enumerated with representative citations — e.g. under neurons: dataset-based dissection, feature synthesis, perturbation/ablation, gradient attribution; under representations: self-explaining models, adversarial training, disentanglement, token and attention analysis, concept vectors, probing, and cross-network representation comparison. Second, each section flags a *hazard* specific to that target — frivolous weights, polysemantic neurons, frivolous neurons, entanglement, cherry-picking — so that every family arrives with its known failure mode attached rather than with a caveat in a later section. Third, they draw connections outward to five adjacent literatures (adversarial robustness, continual learning, modularity, compression, and work on the human visual system), arguing each connection runs in both directions — interpretability tools can build adversaries, and adversarially trained networks have been reported as more readable. Fourth, they convert the critique into a list of properties an evaluation should have, and close with eight directions for future work. The rhetorical device that carries the paper is a worked comparison: ImageNet is offered as the exemplar of a benchmark that reorganised a field, and the paper asks what the interpretability equivalent would be, then answers with a concrete proposal — an adversary implants a flaw in a model and a method is scored on whether it helps a human find that flaw.

## What counts as an explanation here

Nothing in the paper is offered as an explanation of a particular network; the object under discussion is the *form* explanations take, and the survey is deliberately ecumenical about it — text descriptions of neurons, feature visualisations, masks over weights, circuits, probing accuracy, disentangled coordinates all count as candidate forms. What the paper does insist on is a status for each: an interpretation is a **hypothesis**, not a conclusion, and it becomes knowledge only when it yields a testable prediction that survives on a named data distribution. The survey's own contribution is therefore a classification plus an evaluative standard, and it is explicitly partial: it excludes input attribution, black-box and neurosymbolic methods, and says so.

## How they judged success

The paper's central evaluative claims are judgements about the literature rather than measurements, and the few numbers in it are borrowed from the works it cites (for example, that pruning can remove close to two orders of magnitude of weights with little performance cost, and that certain methods only work on a fraction of the examples they are tried on). The stated ladder of evaluation strength is precise and worth copying: merely *suggesting* a characterisation is the weakest form; making one simple testable prediction and validating it is better; and the strongest form is showing that the method helps a human or engineer do something useful with the model — build an adversary, edit a network predictably, or reverse-engineer a system. Named success criteria for the field are a flaw-implantation benchmark, competitions for implanting and rediscovering flaws, controlled comparisons against random baselines, multiple trials, and explicit statements of where a method fails.

## What they found

Six conclusions organise the discussion. Interpretability and adversarial robustness appear to be mutually reinforcing in the literature they survey, in both directions. Continual learning, modularity and compression all make weights or neurons more intrinsically interpretable, and compressibility tracks readability. A method's usefulness is bounded by whether it survives scale — they note capsule networks being interpretable but not competitive beyond small image benchmarks, and ask authors to report compute. Interpretations are hypotheses, and treating them as conclusions is described as pervasive. Cherry-picking is described as pervasive in the same way, with the recommendation that methods be evaluated on randomly or adversarially sampled subtasks rather than on the cases that were amenable to interpretation. Finally, they distinguish two kinds of "success" that are usually conflated: a method that improves capability is not the same as a method that improves oversight, and they argue safety rather than capability should be the goal. On mechanistic interpretability specifically, they characterise the field's record as a limited number of successes concentrated on small models, simple tasks, and heavy expert effort, and suggest program synthesis as the route to automating hypothesis generation and validation.

## Limits and open problems they name

The paper's honesty about its own genre is unusual: it is a survey with no measurements of its own, and it concedes that widely accepted benchmarks do not exist, so the field cannot be ranked. It names several difficulties it cannot solve — that deceptive behaviour and latent knowledge are by definition invisible in deployment behaviour; that human concepts and model concepts may not correspond, requiring an ontology-translation method nobody has; and that interpretability of a system superhuman on some task may be limited in principle. It also names the practical bottleneck that the strongest methods to date needed many hours of expert attention even for trivial tasks, so human involvement must move from generating explanations to screening them. Notably, the paper's recommendation that techniques be combined is supported only by scattered synergies — it states outright that there appear to be no works dedicated to studying interactions between methods, so that recommendation is an argument from absence rather than from evidence.

## What we take from it

Directly relevant to the white-box-modelling goal, three things. **A framing**: the paper's distinction between a suggestion, a validated prediction, and an engineering-useful tool is the grading scheme our own account should be reported against, and its insistence that validity is only granted on the distribution where tests ran is the standing limit on every extrapolation we will want to make. **A measure**: "helps rediscover a flaw an adversary implanted" is a task-shaped benchmark that is much harder to game than a human looking at a feature and nodding, and it maps almost exactly onto our prediction-under-intervention criterion — implant a known computation, then ask whether our account recovers it. **A warning with a specific hazard catalogue**: frivolous weights and neurons mean that interpretable-looking subsets can be causally inert, polysemanticity means a one-neuron-one-concept reading is incomplete, entanglement generalises that to layers, and attention maps are named as a form of explanation that is not reliably faithful — four failure modes we will meet on the way to any predictive account. **A shortcut** from its own critique: because everything in the survey is a hypothesis until tested, the cheapest improvement over this literature is to make the test the deliverable — for each claim, the prediction it licenses and the check that would falsify it.
