- **Date:** 2024-10-26 (NeurIPS 2024)

## What they wanted, and why

Two strands of the causal-discovery literature sit on assumptions that do not both hold in a real lab. The design literature picks intervention targets well but assumes that each chosen intervention then yields unlimited data; the Bayesian literature tolerates finite data but is tractable only on restricted graph classes (trees) or only under parametric assumptions about the mechanisms (linear-Gaussian noise, known form). The authors want a posterior over graphs that is both non-parametric — no assumption about the functional form of the mechanisms — and computable on general graphs, while treating interventional data as genuinely scarce. Their concession is asymmetric and explicit: observational data is assumed abundant and the observational joint distribution known exactly, so the equivalence class is settled before any interventional sample is drawn; only the interventional draws are rationed. The larger goal is a quantifiable exchange rate between interventional samples and confidence in the oriented graph, which is a different currency from "how many experiments do I need".

## What they studied

Discrete variables on a causally sufficient, faithful DAG whose conditional probability tables are strictly positive. The object of inference is not the graph but the *cut configuration*: for each intervention target, the set of orientations taken by the edges crossing from the target to the rest of the graph. By faithfulness plus the identification result they cite, the post-interventional distribution determines exactly that cut and nothing finer, so a target's inferential content is a small finite set of candidate distributions. The unit of analysis is therefore (target, cut configuration) pairs, with the number of candidates bounded by a term exponential in the target size times the maximum degree. No neural network is trained; the machinery is a separating system, a DAG sampler, Meek-rule closure and an explicit posterior update.

## How they did it

Six steps, in order. (1) From the observational distribution, PC plus Meek rules give the essential graph; by the earlier chain-component theory the undirected part splits into chordal components that can be oriented independently, so the only remaining problem is fully orienting those components. (2) Build an intervention family that cuts every edge of that graph: they use two constructions, the string-labelling (n,k)-separating system of Shanmugam et al. (each target has at most k vertices, family size bounded by a product of the ceiling of n/k and its logarithm) and a colouring construction specific to the given graph (perfect elimination order by maximum cardinality search, then greedy colouring in reverse order, giving one intervention set per colour, with the clique number as the bound on family size). (3) For each target, enumerate *all* orientations of the edges leaving it; discard those that create a cycle or a new unshielded collider; for each survivor apply Meek rules to get a maximally oriented partially directed graph. (4) For each such maximally oriented graph, orient the leftover chordal components by sampling a DAG from that component's equivalence class with a polynomial-time uniform sampler (Wienöbst et al. 2023) — the step that replaces exponential enumeration of the class — and read off the interventional distribution from the sampled DAG by the truncated factorisation. (5) Set a prior over cut configurations that is not uniform: count the size of each configuration's interventional equivalence class as a product over its chordal components, and normalise those counts; this is the "each DAG equally likely" prior computed exactly. (6) Stream samples: for each of N total interventional draws, pick a target uniformly at random from the separating system, draw one sample under a randomly chosen intervention value, evaluate its likelihood under every candidate configuration for that target, and update the posterior with the previous posterior as the new prior. The final answer is assembled greedily — repeatedly take the highest-posterior configuration among targets not yet used that is consistent with those already fixed, and splice its arcs into the graph; because every intermediate state is a valid posterior the method is anytime, and can emit a candidate graph after every single sample.

For the theory, they treat each target in isolation: a likelihood-ratio argument plus a concentration bound gives posterior consistency for the true cut, and inverting the posterior bound gives a closed-form sample count per target that suffices for a credibility threshold, with a union bound over targets in the family.

## What counts as an explanation here

A posterior over graph structures, and — the part that matters for us — an *account class with a size and a count*. The explanation is not a narrative or a circuit; it is a distribution over a finite, enumerable set of candidate structures, each with a likelihood, where the set is chosen so that its elements are identifiable by one target's data and mutually exclusive given the observational class. The predictive claim is of two kinds: a graph with a posterior, and — more interesting — a *quantity computed by averaging over that posterior*: in the case study they do not output a graph at all, they output an expected divergence of the estimated causal effect from the truth, averaging over configurations weighted by their posterior. Partiality is designed in: unresolved parts of the graph remain a mixture rather than being collapsed to their most likely completion.

## How they judged success

Two measures, one theoretical and one empirical. Theoretical: per-target sample count m for which the posterior of the true cut is at least 1−gamma with probability at least 1−delta, with the constant depending on the minimum pairwise KL divergence between candidate distributions for that target, on a bounded-log-likelihood-ratio parameter, on maximum degree and target size, and on the prior mass on the truth; summed over a family of p targets. Empirical: structural Hamming distance between the returned DAG and the ground truth as a function of the number of interventional samples actually collected, averaged over 50 randomly generated graphs per setting, with a standard-deviation band. Baselines: random target selection from the separating system with independence tests on accumulated samples; the directed-clique-tree active learner (Squires et al. 2020); and the adaptivity-complexity search of Choo and Shiragur. Second baseline comparison, in the appendix, is against a parametric Bayesian learner (AVICI) fine-tuned on the same random graphs. Test data: chordal DAGs from a modified Erdős–Rényi generator (random vertex ordering, in-degree drawn as a floored binomial truncated below the order, parents chosen uniformly among predecessors, then chordalised by elimination in reverse order); settings span orders 5, 6, 7 at full density, order 10 at densities 0.1/0.15/0.2, order 20 at densities 0.05/0.1/0.15, and scale-free graphs of order 7 from a Barabási–Albert generator. The case study generates 50 graphs at orders 5, 6, 7 with density 0.3, picks a non-adjacent pair, intervenes on the neighbourhood of the source and collects 100,000 samples while plotting the averaged KL and total-variation divergence from the true effect against sample count.

## What they found

Empirically the ordering is unambiguous in the reported figures: the Bayesian curve drops to a low structural Hamming distance in far fewer interventional samples than any of the three main baselines, and — the claim the authors emphasise — the sample count it needs does *not* grow much with graph order, whereas the baselines' required sample count rises steeply with order, and the baselines' curves also need much more data as density rises while theirs stays roughly flat. The same ordering holds on order-20 graphs and on the scale-free graphs. Against the parametric Bayesian learner the comparison is lopsided (that method fails to recover the structure at high expected distance in this setting), which the authors attribute to its mechanism assumptions and its inability to keep improving as interventional samples accumulate; they also note explicitly that the competing method comes with no convergence guarantee. The case study is reported as decreasing mean divergence and shrinking variance as interventional samples grow at each of the three orders. The theoretical contribution is the closed-form sample count above; the case study's companion derivation uses Hoeffding's inequality to justify approximating a posterior expectation of an indicator by sampling DAGs from the equivalence class, with accuracy set by the number of samples.

## Limits and open problems they name

The authors name the space of tracked posteriors as the primary limitation: on large dense graphs, enumerating the interventional distributions of a target is intractable, and their own complexity appendix concedes that for a complete graph of order n they track a number exponential in n (though smaller than the factorial many distributions a fully Bayesian treatment would need) and show it is only polynomial in n for sparse graphs with logarithmic degree and small targets, via a clique-chain construction whose equivalence class is super-polynomial. Second, they flag the assumptions as candidates for weakening, listing causal sufficiency and faithfulness by name. Third, they note societal risk from intervening in real systems and from partial or biased understanding. What is implicit rather than named: the observational distribution is taken as exact, so no observational error is propagated; intervention values are drawn at random rather than chosen; and the cost per experiment, as opposed to per sample, is nowhere in the model.

## What we take from it

Three things, in decreasing order of portability.

*A count, not an enumeration, as the prior.* The move that makes this Bayesian and tractable is that a prior over accounts can be computed by counting the members of each account's equivalence class with a uniform sampler, rather than enumerating them. For us that is the recipe for putting a prior over a candidate account class that is too large to list: sample uniformly from each class, count the samples, normalise. It also means the prior is a *declared choice with a computable content* rather than a uniform default — and their uniform prior over configurations would be a different, less defensible choice, which is worth remembering when we are tempted to write "uniform prior" and move on.

*A quantified exchange rate between draws and confidence, with every term named.* The sample-count formula is not just an order of magnitude: it says which quantities the cost scales with — the inverse square of the KL separation between the hardest-to-distinguish pair of accounts for that target, the logarithm of the number of candidates, the boundedness of the log likelihood ratio, the prior mass on the truth and the requested credibility. Two consequences for our batteries: the *hardest pair*, not the average pair, sets the price, which is the same "worst-case separation" instinct as the sample-cost lower bound in `arxiv-2405.11548` but derived per target rather than per instance; and a well-founded prior buys a logarithmic saving while a wrong one is a linear-looking penalty in the log term — worth reporting as a sensitivity row rather than as a constant.

*The decomposable account: orient one cut at a time, then recombine under a compatibility check.* Their identification result is that a single intervention on a target identifies the orientation of the edges crossing it, independently of the rest. That is the structural template for a probe battery with a combinatorial cover: each probe answers a small, local, mutually exclusive question, and the global account is the composition of local answers constrained to be jointly consistent. The greedy recombination step also shows the honest cost of the template — their recombiner is greedy and only weakly documented as a guarantee, which is precisely where a global posterior would have been preferable.

One warning worth carrying: their anytime property and their theoretical guarantee live at different levels. Any sample count gives a graph; only a *stated* sample count gives a graph with a stated credibility. Reporting the former as the latter is the mistake the stopping-rule discipline in this folder exists to prevent.
