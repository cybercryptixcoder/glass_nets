# Correcting Attribution Patching with a Few Exact Measurements

- **Key:** arxiv-2610.27393
- **Authors / organisation:** Luyang Zhang, Minxing Zheng (equal contribution); Carnegie Mellon University. Jialu Wang; University of California, Santa Cruz.
- **Date:** 2026-10-19
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27393
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (arXiv HTML v1, ~85k clean chars: abstract, §1–§5, references, Appendices A–I incl. the label-acquisition protocol, the ProbeCal curvature sketches, the three theorems and their proofs, every within-prompt control and ablation table, the error-structure diagnostics, the six-cell confirmation protocol with its Bonferroni family, the cost frontiers and the cost accounting). **No figure inspected** — Figures 1–11 are budget-frontier curves and error histograms, so every number below is a table cell or an explicit sentence, and stated intervals are the paper's own descriptive or adjusted intervals rather than readings off a plot.
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central) — the folder runs attribution patching constantly as the cheap stand-in for intervention, and this is the first entry that treats the *distribution of its error* as the object to be measured and then spends a fixed measurement budget against that measured distribution; the result is a design rule for how to buy accuracy in any cheap-versus-exact estimator pair we build.

## What they wanted, and why
The problem is a cost cliff in the middle of causal interpretability: exact activation patching gives the real effect of one component on one prompt but costs one forward-and-restore per component per prompt, while attribution patching gives every component's effect from a single backward pass and can be badly wrong. Verifying estimates one by one simply rebuilds the cost the cheap method was supposed to remove, so the question is whether the *errors* of the cheap method have enough structure that a few exact measurements can repair many estimates. Their larger goal is to make component-level effect vectors cheap enough to compute at the scale where interpretation is actually done — tens of thousands of neurons per layer, thousands of prompts — without giving up the accuracy that makes a ranking trustworthy. They also frame the accounting explicitly: budget is counted in exact measurements, and methods are compared by the error left at matched compute, calibration included.

## What they studied
Transformer LLMs on three controlled behaviours, unit of analysis a **single component's isolated effect** on a prediction score. Within-prompt experiments use MLP neurons of Gemma-2-9B (14,336 per layer), Qwen3-14B (17,408) and Qwen3-32B (25,600); across-prompt experiments use attention heads of Qwen3-8B (1,152 heads) and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (784). Behaviours: indirect-object identification, factual completion, and subject-verb agreement, scored as the logit difference between the correct token and a counterfactual one. The cheap estimator is the gradient of the score at the clean run; the exact one is a real single-component patch with downstream recomputation.

## How they did it
1. **Define the two quantities and the error.** For component `i`, the exact effect is `Δᵢ = F(vᵢ) − F(0)` where `F` scores the prompt after every component receives an additive shift along its patch direction, and the cheap estimate is `gᵢ = ∂ᵢF(0)`. The residual `eᵢ = Δᵢ − gᵢ` is what everything else is about; accuracy is mean absolute error of the effect vector against exhaustive patching in FP32.
2. **Look at the residuals before designing anything.** On a GPT-2 toy analysis, the top four components carry about 80% of the test error, and each component's calibration error correlates strongly with its test error while being consistently biased away from zero. Three patterns follow: error is concentrated, biased on a single prompt, and repeatable for a component across prompts of a task.
3. **Within-prompt arm (no labels beforehand).** Split the budget into a small *pilot* and a verification remainder. Draw the pilot stratified by attribution magnitude in eight equal rank strata, so it represents the typical component at every scale of effect; take the **median** pilot residual as one shared shift applied to every unmeasured estimate; spend the rest of the budget exactly verifying the largest magnitudes. The estimator is therefore exact on `B` components and shifted-cheap on the rest.
4. **Across-prompt arm (needs calibration prompts).** Fit two things on labels gathered from earlier prompts of the same task: a per-component correction, and a log-linear *size* model predicting how much error remains after correction from the component's attribution. Later prompts get corrections for all components and the budget goes to the `B` components with the highest predicted remaining error. A whole component can also be corrected at its dataset mean without measuring the new prompts at all.
5. **Write the theory for when each arm wins.** Within a prompt, the gain over spending the same budget on verification alone is decomposed into the benefit of the shared shift, minus the cost of estimating that shift from a finite pilot, minus the verification displaced by the pilot; a Hoeffding-style bound covers the pilot. Across prompts, a median correction from representative labels cannot be far worse than the best shift, and a proposition shows a selection score must approximate the *expected size* of the remaining error, not the size of the expected error.
6. **Run the controls the design implies.** Verification-plus-shift (same 256 magnitude-ranked measurements, but the shift learned from the selected components instead of a representative pilot), no-pilot centering, uniform-versus-stratified pilot, mean-versus-median, pilot size, fixed component populations of 1,024/4,096/all, FP64 rescoring at fixed estimates, and for across-prompt a random ranking arm, a capacity-matched AP-only learner, and a curvature-probe label source as an alternative to exact labels.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not an explanation of any behaviour — this is an **estimator paper**, and what it produces is a better *measurement of component effects* plus a small quantity of theory about error structure. The object it hands over is an effect vector that is exact where measured and corrected elsewhere, together with the residual law it learned. It is explicitly a per-component, isolated-intervention object: the authors state that low error on isolated effects does not imply that a jointly intervened circuit is faithful, which is the boundary of what the artefact can support.

## How they judged success
Primary metric: per-prompt mean absolute error of the effect vector against exact FP32 activation patching, with relative AP error (summed absolute error over summed absolute effects), dataset-average error, and recall of the ten largest effects as companions. Cost is *measured warm runtime per layer*, including attribution, pilot, fitting and assembly, with the across-prompt arms compared at matched projected total cost across analysis lengths of 100, 1,000 and 10,000 prompts. Uncertainty is paired group bootstraps (20,000 draws) with Bonferroni adjustment over a prespecified family of 360 across-prompt comparisons; within-prompt intervals are descriptive. Baselines: verify-more (same budget, largest magnitudes, no correction), AtP*, selective HVP, MS-HVP at four and five steps, singleton integrated gradients at four and eight points, exhaustive AP, plus a random ranking and a capacity-matched learner.

## What they found
**Within a prompt the shared shift is small but real, and it is the shift — not the selection — that does the work.** Against runtime-matched verify-more, MAE falls 17–26% on Gemma-2-9B (17.4% IOI, 26.2% factual, 19.9% SVA), 11–24% on Qwen3-14B and 14–18% on Qwen3-32B while measuring only about 1% of the 32B neurons. The fitted shift captures 96–97% of the best achievable common shift's benefit. Per-group, Guided-AP wins on 96/100, 97/100 and 98/100 Gemma groups.

**The control that separates the pilot from verification is the paper's sharpest internal result.** Learning the shift from the same magnitude-ranked components that verification would have measured *raises* error in eight of nine settings — relative MAE 1.93 vs 1.00 baseline on IOI, 1.80 on SVA, 0.97 on factual, and Guided-AP reaching 0.83 / 0.74 / 0.81 against the better of the two. A representative sample and a high-attribution sample of the same size are not interchangeable, and the correction is worth 23–57% over verification-plus-shift depending on cell.

**Median beats mean in all nine settings; pilot size saturates.** Pilots of 32 or 64 improve on 16 and 128 adds nothing consistent, so the budget split is forgiving. Frontier-cohort comparisons at 256 measurements put Guided-AP (0.952 / 1.230 / 0.723% relative error at 2.69 / 1.30 / 1.64 s per layer) below AtP* (1.286 / 1.721 / 0.898%), selective HVP (1.321 / 1.758 / 1.029% at about 19 s) and every MS-HVP and IG setting (about 73–170 s) at a fraction of their runtime.

**Runtime gains are target-dependent and the paper prints the losses.** At a 1% mean relative AP error target on Gemma, Guided-AP is 7.9× faster than verification on IOI, but on SVA verification reaches the target with 32 checks in 0.43 s against 1.25 s, and on factual completion Guided-AP *misses* the 1% target (1.20%) entirely. At Guided-AP's own achieved error on Qwen3-32B the post hoc ratio is 5.9–14.7× across tasks, with bootstrap intervals of 4.2–19.5.

**Across prompts, the correction is strong and the selection is weak.** Correcting dataset-average effects cuts average MAE on factual completion by **75–79%** on both models with no measurement on the evaluated prompts (error ratios 3.98 and 4.78 uncorrected-over-corrected), against 46% for the curvature-probe label source. Using the same calibration measurements directly as the average effects is **5.8–14.9× worse** than modeling the residual — the gain is from the error model, not from having more labels. But allocation of verification by the learned size model is mostly a wash: at matched projected cost it gains 12% on Qwen3 IOI at B=16 and 2–4% on SVA, and *harms* four cells (−29%, −20%, −18%, −2%), while ProbeCal's curvature labels do better on factual allocation (31–35%). At 100 prompts verify-more wins everywhere; at 1,000 prompts 108 of 120 adjusted comparisons favour it; break-even is 1,900–24,000 prompts at B=16. And on Qwen2.5 IOI the dataset-average correction *raises* MAE by 52%, which the authors attribute to selection bias in how calibration labels were acquired.

**Three warnings buried in the appendices.** Lower MAE does not imply better recall (one ProbeCal setting cuts MAE 11% while top-ten recall falls from 99.2% to 97.0%, and some IOI settings lose 0.03–0.07 recall). The dataset-average residual is sign-consistent rather than large: the absolute mean signed error is 81–96% of the mean absolute error, so the average-level correction is a bias repair and says nothing about per-prompt error. And the curvature-probe signal's noise grows with inter-component coupling — median off-diagonal-to-diagonal Hessian ratio rises from 5.8 to 77.2 between 32 and 1,152 heads — which is why the probe alternative helps where exact labels are sparse and hurts where components interact.

## Limits and open problems they name
One architecture family and three short controlled behaviours; the authors ask for other architectures, other component representations (notably sparse features, where patching is most expensive), and open-ended behaviours. Accuracy and runtime depend on task, budget, hardware and implementation, and the projected costs assume a fixed loading and batching schedule. The effect measured is defined relative to a chosen corruption and output metric, the estimates describe isolated components, and faithfulness of a jointly intervened circuit is out of scope. Future work is to combine the within-prompt and across-prompt settings rather than run them separately.

## What we take from it
**A method, and it is cheap.** The estimator pair (one gradient pass for everything, plus `B` exact wrecks) is exactly the shape of a probe-then-verify workflow: rank candidates cheaply, spend a bounded number of real interventions, and **shift the unmeasured tail by the median residual of a representative pilot**. The one rule to carry verbatim is that the pilot must be drawn to represent the population — stratified by cheap-estimate magnitude, not top-`B` — because the control showing the naive version doubles the error is the most reusable negative result in the paper.

**A measure.** "Error left at matched compute, calibration included" is the right way to report any surrogate we use for a real intervention, and it forces three numbers we normally omit: the pilot size, the verification count, and whether the gain survives when the correction model is fitted by a matched-capacity learner on the same number of labels.

**A warning that binds our own reporting.** Selection and correction need different error statistics — selection needs the expected *size* of remaining error, correction needs its *sign* — and a large estimated effect is not a large error. The concrete trap for us: the components with the largest attributions are a biased sample of the error distribution, so any "validate on the top components we care about" protocol measures the wrong sub-population and, per the paper's own table, can be worse than not correcting at all. The same asymmetry appears in the negative transfer result (correction of per-prompt effects is safe, correction of dataset averages can invert if the labels were acquired by magnitude): whenever we correct an aggregate, the labels must be acquired uniformly across prompts, not preferentially.

**A shortcut for the fallback layer of a predictive white-box model.** A component's average effect over a task can be corrected from a handful of calibration prompts and then reused with no further measurement — a way to get an approximate per-component mean map cheaply for many prompts, with the caveat that the estimate carries a *bias* correction and not a per-input one. Read beside the folder's redundancy and self-repair findings, the honest reading is that this buys a well-calibrated average importance map at about 1% of component coverage (13–28% of heads covered by labels in the across-prompt cells), which is a prior for where to intervene and never a substitute for the intervention.
