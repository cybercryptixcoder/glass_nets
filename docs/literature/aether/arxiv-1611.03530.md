# Understanding deep learning requires rethinking generalization

- **Key:** arxiv-1611.03530
- **Authors / organisation:** Chiyuan Zhang (MIT; work done while interning at Google Brain), Samy Bengio (Google Brain), Moritz Hardt (Google Brain), Benjamin Recht (UC Berkeley; work done at Google Brain), Oriol Vinyals (Google DeepMind)
- **Date:** 2017-02-26 (v2; v1 2016-11-10; ICLR 2017)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1611.03530
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

The motivating puzzle is the gap between a network's capacity and its behaviour: these models carry far more parameters than the training set has examples, yet their test error often sits close to their (near-zero) training error, while other architectures of similar size generalize badly. Classical learning theory proposed an answer — VC dimension, Rademacher complexity, uniform stability, and the need for explicit regularizers when parameters outnumber data — and the paper's wager is that this whole family of explanations cannot be the right one, because it cannot even tell apart two networks that differ by orders of magnitude in generalization. The larger goal is the one this folder keeps running into: to know what property of a trained network predicts its behaviour, and what would count as a satisfying explanation of that behaviour. The authors frame their contribution as problematization rather than construction — they say plainly that they have not found the missing measure, only shown that the current ones fail.

## What they studied

Image classifiers on CIFAR10 and ImageNet ILSVRC 2012: a small Inception adapted to 28x28 inputs, a small AlexNet, Inception V3 at 299x299, and bare MLPs with one or three 512-unit hidden layers. The unit of analysis is the whole model and its training procedure — the paper is not about neurons, directions or circuits. The intervention is on the *data*: the label assignment and the pixels, held in five variants plus a noise continuum. Everything else in the learning problem (architecture, size, hyperparameters, optimizer, learning-rate schedule) is deliberately left alone, which is the design's whole point: the treatment is a data transformation and nothing else.

## How they did it

The method is a randomization test borrowed from non-parametric statistics, applied to learning curves. Each architecture is trained twice with identical settings: once on the true labels and once on a copy of the training set whose labels have been replaced by uniform-random classes. Six data conditions are swept — true labels, partial corruption where each label is randomized independently with probability p, all-random labels, a single fixed pixel permutation applied to every image, an independent pixel permutation per image, and Gaussian-noise images with matched mean and variance. The corruption ratio p is swept from 0 to 1 to trace the continuum between signal and pure noise; training loss, time-to-overfit and converged test error are recorded at each level. Then the same randomization experiment is rerun with explicit regularizers switched back on (weight decay, dropout, random cropping, flip-and-rotation augmentation), including on random labels, to see whether regularization rescues the situation. Implicit regularizers are probed separately by removing batch normalization and by reading the best-test-accuracy-along-training curve as an upper bound on what early stopping could buy. The empirical work is complemented by a finite-sample expressivity construction and by an analysis of SGD on linear models and kernels.

## What counts as an explanation here

Mostly a **negative result with a diagnostic instrument**, and the instrument is the part worth keeping: an experiment that separates a proposed explanation of generalization from a trivial one by holding the model fixed and destroying the data-label relationship. If a complexity measure, a regularizer or a stability property is claimed to be *the* reason a network generalizes, it must respond to a change that alters generalization while leaving the model untouched — and by construction these quantities cannot respond, because they are properties of the architecture or the algorithm alone. The paper's constructive pieces are a theorem (a two-layer ReLU net with 2n + d weights can fit any labelling of n points in d dimensions; equivalently depth k with width O(n/k)) and a worked linear-model argument showing SGD finds the minimum-norm interpolant, which is offered as a partial mechanism rather than as an explanation. Nothing here is predictive of a particular network's behaviour on a particular input.

## How they judged success

The primary read-out is training accuracy versus test accuracy — since training error goes to zero in nearly every condition, test error *is* generalization error — reported per architecture and per data condition, together with convergence time relative to the true-label run. Baselines are the same architecture with the same recipe on true labels, and the sweep of corruption ratio provides an internal control curve. Regularizer ablations toggle augmentation and weight decay independently (Table 1) and augmentation, dropout and weight decay jointly (Table 2), which is what lets the paper separate each regularizer's contribution from the others. Theoretical claims are proved outright (Lemma 1 plus the construction, with a corollary trading width for depth). There are no error bars and no repeated seeds reported; the evidence is the size of the effects, which are large — a 100-accuracy-point training/test gap under random labels, and an 18-point top-1 drop on ImageNet when all regularizers are removed.

## What they found

Networks fit random labels perfectly: Inception, AlexNet and both MLPs on CIFAR10 all reach 100% train accuracy (99.82–99.34% for AlexNet and the 1x512 MLP) on labels that carry no information, at test accuracy indistinguishable from the 10% of random guessing (9.78, 9.86, 10.48, 10.61%). They also fit pure noise — shuffled pixels and Gaussian images converge, and faster than random labels do. On ImageNet with a million random labels over 1000 classes, Inception V3 reaches 95.20% top-1 train accuracy (99.14% top-5) with 0.11% test top-1, and still ~90% top-1 with explicit regularizers on. Convergence slows only modestly as corruption rises and test error climbs smoothly toward the random-guess 90%. On the regularizer question, turning everything off drops CIFAR10 Inception from 89.05% to 85.75% test, and the authors conclude regularization is a tuning knob rather than a fundamental phase change; on ImageNet all-off costs 18 points top-1 (77.84% to 59.80%) yet still beats the 0.1% of guessing, and the unregularized 80.38% top-5 comes close to AlexNet's 83.6% on ILSVRC 2012, which the paper reads as evidence that architecture change buys more than regularization. Batch normalization improves CIFAR10 test accuracy by only 3–4%. On the kernel side, exact interpolation generalizes surprisingly well (1.2% test error on MNIST, 46% on CIFAR10 with raw pixels), but the minimum-norm solution's norm does not track performance — the MNIST norm rises from 220 to 390 with wavelet preprocessing while the error halves.

## Limits and open problems they name

No explanation is supplied, and the paper says so: we have not discovered the formal measure under which these large models are simple, and the appeal to linear models shows that even in the convex case the minimum-norm intuition is not predictive. They flag that their claim about early stopping is only potential, since it needs an isolated test set to be rigorous. Random-label fitting on ImageNet is not perfect with unchanged hyperparameters, and the authors concede tuning would likely close the gap. One regularizer does break the phenomenon — AlexNet with weight decay fails to converge on random labels — so the statement that regularization is neither necessary nor sufficient comes with at least one exception the design records but does not explain. Everything here is about generalization error as a scalar for a model family, not about what the model computes.

## What we take from it

Four things, and the third is a warning that bears directly on how this folder has used the paper. (1) **The corrupted-label construction is the cleanest instrument in this folder for separating "the model learned the structure" from "the model can represent the assignment"**, and it costs one run: hold the architecture, optimizer and schedule fixed, swap the labels, and any claim that a measured property causes generalization must survive the comparison. For a probe battery the analogue is a label-shuffled null run of the entire pipeline, not just of the probe fit — if the battery's read-outs look the same on shuffled labels, the battery is measuring capacity, not signal. (2) **Randomization as a test, not as an afterthought.** The paper's structure — sweep the intervention level from 0 to 1 and plot the outcome as a curve — is cheaper to trust than a single before/after pair, because intermediate levels test monotonicity; our own interventions should be swept, not toggled. (3) **A warning about how this paper's result gets cited**: fitting random labels shows that *capacity-based* measures cannot explain generalization, but it is not evidence that *no* white-box property does. The constructive theorem is about expressivity of the weight set on a finite sample, and the random-label run says nothing about whether activations, circuits or probes differ between the true-label and random-label networks — a comparison the paper does not run and which is exactly the experiment a predictive white-box model would want. (4) On the regularization result: prefer the ablation table's *marginal* reading (augmentation is the strong regularizer; weight decay on top of it buys 0.6 points on CIFAR10) over the aggregate, because the joint effect hides a very uneven split.
