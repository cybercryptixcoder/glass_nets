# Multi-Library Feature Engineering and Statistically Validated Classification for Power Swing Detection and Stability Assessment

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.20364
- **Authors / organisation:** Pallav Kumar Bera (Western Kentucky University); Samita Rani Pani, Ranjeeta Patel (KIIT Deemed University, Odisha)
- **Date:** 2026-11-16
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20364
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Distance relays in high-voltage transmission are tripped by the *apparent* impedance of the line, and two disturbances exploit that: a power swing can push the impedance trajectory into a protective zone with no fault present, and a genuine fault occurring during an already-running swing can be hidden by a blocking scheme that is correctly blocking. The authors frame the operational need as two decisions taken in sequence — first separate three-phase faults, faults that happen mid-swing, and swings with no fault; then, once a swing is confirmed, call it stable or unstable so blocking and out-of-step tripping can act. Their complaint about the existing learning-based detectors is specific and methodological: the feature sets are hand-built and narrow, and classifiers are compared by point-estimate accuracy with no formal test of whether the compared models actually differ. The paper is therefore as much a demonstration of a *comparison protocol* as a detector, which is what makes it relevant to a survey about how this field decides it has learned something.

## What they studied
A 9-bus, three-machine WSCC test system simulated in PSCAD/EMTDC at 60 Hz, with three-phase voltage and current waveforms recorded at one relay and cut into single-cycle windows (84 samples at 5 kHz). 4,845 events in three classes — 1,320 transmission-line faults across five fault types and varied location, inception and resistance; 1,320 faults injected partway through a stable swing (660 symmetrical, 660 asymmetrical); and 2,205 swing-only cases split into symmetrical stable (720), symmetrical unstable (540), asymmetrical stable (450) and asymmetrical unstable (495). The unit of analysis is a **feature vector over a window**, not a neuron, direction or circuit: this is a signal-classification paper with a per-event interpretability claim attached through SHAP values.

## How they did it
Three off-the-shelf time-series feature libraries (tsfresh in its efficient configuration, TSFEL restricted to statistical and temporal domains, and tsfeatures with its periodicity-dependent features disabled because an 84-sample window has no known season) are each run over all six relay channels and the results concatenated, dropping constant, undefined and non-finite columns and median-imputing the rest, to leave 3,286 features per window. Selection is deliberately two-stage: Boruta, which wraps a class-balanced random forest and keeps every feature that beats a shuffled copy of itself, is used as an *all-relevant* filter because a minimal-optimal wrapper started from 3,286 columns is unstable; then a LightGBM-based recursive elimination with cross-validated selection of the subset size, and separately an iterative SHAP procedure that repeatedly fits the model, ranks by mean absolute SHAP value, discards the weakest quartile and stops at the smallest set within one standard error of the best round. The intersection of those two is taken as the default 18-feature set. Fourteen classifiers spanning linear, tree-ensemble, kernel/instance, probabilistic and neural families are then benchmarked under repeated stratified cross-validation (5 folds × 5 repeats = 25 fold evaluations) on balanced accuracy and macro-F1. Model comparison runs a Friedman omnibus over the 25 per-fold scores in a first pass to answer "is anything different here at all", then two post-hoc procedures that disagree on purpose — a Nemenyi rank test and Nadeau–Bengio corrected paired t-tests against the leader with Holm adjustment. Confusion matrices, per-class scores and ROC-AUC are computed from out-of-fold predictions rather than from the model refit on everything. For the second task the same 18 features are reused unchanged, explicitly so that the effect of the label is separated from the effect of feature choice.

## What counts as an explanation here
Two forms, neither of them mechanistic. The first is feature-level importance: a ranked list of which library descriptors carry the decision, read as a claim about *what kind of signal property* distinguishes the classes. The second is the classifier comparison itself, which the paper treats as evidence about the structure of the problem — linear models scoring far below tree ensembles is offered as a statement that the classes are not linearly separable in this feature space. Nothing here predicts an internal computation; the explanation is a validated association between a named descriptor and a decision.

## How they judged success
Balanced accuracy and macro-F1 under 5×5-fold repeated stratified cross-validation; 95% confidence intervals from the t-distribution over folds; Friedman omnibus with its chi-square and p; Nemenyi post-hoc; Nadeau–Bengio corrected resampled paired t-tests against the leader with Holm–Bonferroni step-down; out-of-fold confusion matrices, per-class precision/recall and one-vs-rest ROC-AUC; mean absolute SHAP values over a representative subsample for importance; fit time in seconds recorded beside every score.

## What they found
On the primary three-way task Rotation Forest leads at 99.98% balanced accuracy (SD 0.04, CI 99.96–100.00) and, more importantly, **nine of the other thirteen classifiers are statistically indistinguishable from it** — Extra Trees, Random Forest, XGBoost, Gradient Boosting, CatBoost, LightGBM, SVM-RBF and the MLP all land within 0.12 points with Holm-adjusted p above 0.95, while Ridge, Naive Bayes, linear SVM and logistic regression are decisively worse (19.14, 13.84, 5.10 and 3.52 points down, all Holm-adjusted p below 10⁻¹⁰). k-NN's nominal difference (p = 0.02) does not survive correction (0.16). On the stable-versus-unstable task SVM-RBF leads at 97.97% (SD 0.80) but only Naive Bayes (9.11 points down) and Ridge (1.33 points down, p = 0.030) are significantly worse by the corrected t-test; eleven classifiers are ties — and here the two post-hoc tests explicitly disagree, with Nemenyi additionally flagging Extra Trees, Gradient Boosting, k-NN, MLP and Rotation Forest, which the paper attributes to a rank test being sensitive to small but consistent rank differences while the paired test compares score magnitudes. Out-of-fold performance is 97.82% accuracy on that task with an asymmetric error profile (class 1 recalled 96.15% against 99.73% precision, class 2 the mirror image), AUC 99.34% for both classes. The SHAP ranking is dominated by trend and autocorrelation descriptors rather than spectral ones — autocorrelation at lag 10 of the first difference, R² of a linear trend over 5- and 10-sample chunks, variance of trend intercept and standard error, AR(10) coefficients — with the largest single contribution 0.05 and the tail of the eighteen features at 0.01. The authors' own practical reading of the ties is the useful one: several tied classifiers train far faster than the leader (0.24 s for SVM-RBF and 0.97 s for XGBoost against 12.73 s for Rotation Forest on the first task) and are the better deployment choice for a protection function with no significant accuracy cost.

## Limits and open problems they name
One test system, 9-bus, and ideal operating conditions — no measurement noise, no inverter-based resources, nothing larger such as the 39-bus case. Every waveform is simulated, so the data distribution is the simulator's. The interpretability claim stops at descriptor importance: the highlighted features are named, not causally tested, and no feature is ablated to show the classifier depends on it. There is also an uncommented property of the pipeline worth naming: the swing-stability task reuses features tuned on the three-way task, so the second result is a transfer of a feature set chosen for a different label, and the paper does not report what selection tuned on the binary target alone would give.

## What we take from it
Three things. (1) **The two-stage statistical comparison is the transferable instrument**, and its main product is a *tie set* rather than a winner: run an omnibus first to establish the comparison is not noise, then report every model that is not separable from the best, with the times beside them, because in this paper the leader is 25× slower than its nearest rival for 0.01 points. That is the reporting shape our probe-versus-surrogate tables should have, and it pairs with the folder's equivalence-test entries. (2) **The Boruta-then-prune split is a framing worth borrowing for read-out selection**: an all-relevant stage that keeps everything carrying signal, used only for interpretation because it is too redundant to deploy, followed by a minimal-optimal stage for the model — with the intersection taken as the conservative default. Keeping both sets and reporting the intersection is a cheap way to say how much of the ranking is redundancy. (3) **A caution about importance scores as explanation**: eighteen features cover 3,286 candidates, the top one carries 0.05 of total importance, and no feature is deleted to see whether the classifier notices — so the SHAP list here is a description of the fitted model, not evidence about the signal, which is the same standing caution our own attribution tables need.
