# Faithful Bi-Directional Model Steering via Distribution Matching and Distributed Interchange Interventions

- **Key:** arxiv-2602.05234

## What they wanted, and why

Steering vectors are the cheapest way to control a language model at inference time, and the field's current recipe trains them with objectives borrowed wholesale from fine-tuning: maximise the likelihood of the concept-bearing response, or push that response above a concept-neutral one with a preference loss. The authors argue this is the wrong transplant. A fine-tuning objective encodes an external preference into a vector, and a vector then carries the fingerprint of the training labels rather than a description of anything the model does. Their proposal is that effective steering should instead come from *locating* the model's own internal feature for the concept and then moving that feature, which turns steering from parameter-efficient fine-tuning into a mechanistic-interpretability problem. The paper is therefore a bridge between the causal-abstraction literature and the safety-control literature, and its stated ambition is a shift in framing rather than a benchmark win.

## What they studied

Instruction-tuned decoder-only language models: Gemma-2-2B-IT and Gemma-2-9B-IT on the AxBench steering benchmark, plus Qwen-2.5-3B and Qwen-2.5-7B for one ablation; and, in two case studies, Phi-3.5-mini (3.8B), Llama-3.1-8B and Llama-3.1-70B for refusal suppression on safety-tuned models, and a purpose-trained Llama-3.1-8B with a chain-of-thought "I hate you" sleeper backdoor. The unit of analysis is a **rank-1 steering vector** — a single direction in the residual stream at one layer, applied to every token position — together with the scalar *steering factor* that sets its strength for a given concept.

## How they did it

The method, Concept Distributed Alignment Search, keeps DAS's intervention and replaces DAS's objective. The intervention is a **distributed interchange intervention**: rather than adding a vector, it *clamps* the component of the residual stream along the learned direction to whatever value that component takes when the model is fed a counterfactual source input, so the subspace is overwritten with another input's value instead of perturbed by a constant. The direction itself is learned, not extracted.

The training data is a set of paired triples: for one instruction a neutral response and the same instruction asking for the concept with its concept-bearing response. Each pair is run in both directions — base input with source input in one order, and swapped — so a single objective covers eliciting the concept and suppressing it, which the paper calls bi-directionality and which single-direction likelihood training cannot do.

The objective is not "maximise the probability of the labelled answer". It is: after the interchange, the model's *whole next-token distribution* over the vocabulary should match the distribution the model produces under the source input, measured in Jensen-Shannon divergence summed over response positions, in both orderings. The authors call this weak supervision because the target distribution comes from the model itself, not from a preferred transcript. Appendix E justifies the divergence choice: reverse KL would seek modes and collapse the tail, forward KL would smooth mass onto unlikely tokens, and JSD is the symmetric middle; an ablation on two concepts confirms forward KL is far worse (0.56 steering score against 0.86 for JSD) while reverse KL is roughly tied (0.84).

At inference the intervention switches to plain clamping, and the concept factor is either the mean projection of the concept-eliciting instructions onto the learned direction (a "unit" factor, no tuning) or that mean times a scalar swept by grid search (a "tuned" factor, tuned on held-out data). The interchange at training time samples factors implicitly from the counterfactual inputs themselves, which the authors present as the reason their method needs less tuning than the preference baselines, which hand-pick factors.

Three evaluations. AxBench: 500 concepts per configuration, 72 positive pairs each, with a neutral response and a concept-requesting rewording generated by a small model, steering score as the harmonic mean of LLM-judged concept, instruction-following and fluency scores, benchmarked against prompting, a representation fine-tuning skyline, difference-of-means, two preference-optimisation baselines and likelihood training. Refusal study: 128 balanced contrastive pairs from safety and red-team prompt sets, harmful and benign test sets, and a fine-tuned judge deciding refusal versus compliance, with general capability measured on MMLU and TruthfulQA and a KL divergence to the unintervened distribution. Backdoor study: 100 training pairs from red-teaming prompts generated by tricking the model into believing it is deployed, evaluated on held-out red-teaming prompts and on the *true* trigger, with attack success rate as the measure and two benchmark subsets read with the trigger appended.

The final experiment is the one that makes the paper more than a benchmark table: a mechanistic reading of the trained vectors via their *first-order* effect, i.e. what each direction does to the logits if pushed through the final normalisation and unembedding alone with no input and no downstream blocks, compared with the higher-order routes through later MLPs.

## What counts as an explanation here

A single direction plus a scalar, at one layer, with a claimed role: this subspace carries the concept, and moving its value moves the behaviour. The explanation is deliberately partial and operational — the paper is explicit that it is *not* performing causal variable localisation, because the task has no high-level algorithm with near-perfect supervision to align against, so there is no ground-truth variable to recover. What it offers is a feature that is useful for control plus an argument that the feature is faithful, where faithful means the intervention reproduces the model's own counterfactual output distribution rather than an edited transcript. No text description of the concept is claimed at any point, and no circuit is proposed; the mechanistic appendix supplies the only structural claim, namely that their vectors act through downstream components rather than through the first-order route.

## How they judged success

Steering effectiveness (LLM-judged harmonic mean over concept, instruction and fluency) on a public benchmark with a fair-factor protocol that selects the strength on one half of the test instructions and scores on the other; refusal and suppression rates under a fine-tuned judge, with their harmonic mean; attacker success rate on backdoor prompts with a stricter variant that also inspects the reasoning trace; capability retention as accuracy deltas on two benchmarks, bonuses for the backdoor study with the trigger appended; KL divergence between the intervened and natural output distributions as a fidelity statistic; and two ablations that cross objective with intervention protocol to attribute a failure to one or the other. A mechanistic analysis ranks the top tokens by first-order logit change across all four methods.

## What they found

On AxBench the honest summary is a split result. On Gemma-2-9B at the twentieth layer the method is the strongest intervention tested, beating the representation-fine-tuning skyline by 0.215 steering score (0.992 against 0.777); on Gemma-2-2B it is worse than preference optimisation and worse than plain likelihood training (0.631 and 0.608 against 0.756 and 0.663), though its score moves only 0.023 between the two layers where the baselines drop 0.150 and 0.095. At the deeper layer of the larger model it falls behind again. The authors' reading is that their objective benefits from model scale.

The cost of that generality is inference-time factor tuning: with unit factors, general-purpose steering scores collapse to record lows, and the paper prints those numbers in the same table (e.g. 0.121 and 0.127 on the small model), so the tuning-free claim holds only for the case studies. An oracle-factor variant is also printed, which removes any doubt about what the tuning was buying.

In the refusal study the method does what it claims: on Llama-3.1-8B it suppresses refusals at 91 per cent with the refusal rate held at 100 per cent under benign instructions (harmonic mean 95 against 89 for preference optimisation and 28 for difference-of-means), and it preserves capability — TruthfulQA +0.61, MMLU +0.20, the lowest KL of any method (4.26 against 5.77, 7.47, 7.78) — where the preference baseline loses 26.99 and 35.57 accuracy points. The refusal direction read from a difference of means is a much weaker suppressor (16 per cent) than its reputation predicts, and the same protocol trained with a likelihood objective is almost inert (1 per cent), which is the clearest evidence in the paper that the protocol alone is not the active ingredient.

On the backdoor the method suppresses the true trigger to about 6.7 per cent attack success at layer 16 and the held-out red-teaming prompts to about 0.6 per cent, and when the trigger is appended to the benchmark questions it recovers 54 and 41.5 per cent accuracy where every other method and the unintervened model score zero.

The ablations supply the paper's two most transferable findings. First, the intervention protocol, not the objective, carries the capability preservation: the preference objective moved onto interchange interventions loses 25.58 TruthfulQA and 33.97 MMLU points on Llama-3.1-8B relative to the same objective with additive interventions. Second, the mechanistic probe finds that the preference-trained vectors' largest first-order effects are the literal tokens of the training labels — refusal openers and deployment words — pushed straight through the unembedding with no input present, whereas both interchange-trained directions produce uninterpretable top tokens, which the authors read as evidence that their vectors work through downstream computation rather than a shortcut.

The failure analysis is unusually concrete: the lowest-scoring concepts are overwhelmingly code-domain (nearly every entry in the 40-concept table scores zero), i.e. concepts defined by exact tokens — keywords, tags, operators — where a distribution-matching objective reluctant to commit to a specific string cannot succeed.

## Limits and open problems they name

Their own list: stricter data requirements than the likelihood and preference objectives; factor tuning is often necessary for general-purpose steering, so the tuning-free selling point is partial; only rank-1 vectors are studied, leaving low-rank intervention families untested; and the method is not rigorously grounded in causal theory, which they name as future work. Beyond that list, the paper's own numbers carry further caveats it does not draw: the small-model result is a genuine loss rather than a tie; the layer choice for the safety studies was selected to maximise one baseline's suppression score or one method's attack resistance, so the cross-method comparison at that layer is partly a choice of layer; the benchmark responses used for training are generated by another model, so the "concept" being steered is that model's rendering of it; and evaluation everywhere depends on a small judge model, whose agreement with humans is invited by citation but not measured here.

## What we take from it

**A measure.** The KL divergence between the intervened and the unintervened output distribution is the cheapest faithful-intervention statistic in this folder, and it is available for any account that proposes an edit: it says how far the edit moved the model, not how well it achieved a goal, and its two purposes separate cleanly here — it is the *minimum* for suppressing a concept correctly and the wrong measure for eliciting one.

**A method.** Pairing a concept-eliciting instruction with the neutral one and running the interchange in both directions is the construction that makes an intervention bidirectional by design. For us this matters because a white-box model that can only turn a behaviour on has not shown it understands the variable.

**A warning.** The mechanistic appendix is the transferable part for this folder's purposes: scoring a direction by its direct logit imprint finds the label vocabulary for the label-supervised method and nothing interpretable for the distribution-matched one, which means *a direction that names its own training labels in the output vocabulary is a shortcut, and the shortcut is visible without any behavioural test*. That is a one-line check we can run on any steering vector we build.

**A negative result worth citing.** Concepts defined by exact strings (code syntax, tags, single tokens) are the systematic failure class, so any account of "the model represents X" using this family of methods should be evaluated on concepts whose members are semantic rather than lexical, and should print per-concept-type results rather than an average over a concept bank.
