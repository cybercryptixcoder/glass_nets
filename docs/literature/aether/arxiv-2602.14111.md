## What they wanted, and why
The field validates sparse autoencoders with four proxy scores — reconstruction fidelity, latent interpretability, sparse probing and causal editing — and treats high scores on them as evidence that the dictionary found real features. The authors ask whether that inference is licensed. They attack it from both ends: a synthetic setting where the true features are known, so recovery can be measured rather than inferred, and a real-activation setting where they build null dictionaries that cannot contain the true features by construction and ask whether the standard scores can tell them apart from trained ones. The larger goal is a reusable null test that any new dictionary architecture has to clear, in the same spirit as sanity checks for saliency maps.

## What they studied
Two subjects. First, synthetic activations in 100 dimensions generated from 3,200 ground-truth unit directions drawn uniformly from the sphere, with coefficients log-normal and binary firing masks either constant-probability or log-uniform over a long tail, both averaging 20 active features; four dictionaries of width 3,200 at L0 = 20 (BatchTopK, JumpReLU, TopK, Matryoshka) were trained on it with hyperparameters matched to the generator's sparsity and expansion factor of 32. Second, real residual-stream activations: layer 12 and layer 19 of Gemma-2-2B and layer 16 of Llama-3-8B, with dictionaries of 73,728 and 131,072 latents (expansion 32), L0 swept over 80/115/160/225/320, trained on 500M OpenWebText tokens, plus BatchTopK, JumpReLU and ReLU variants and a TopK arm in an appendix. The unit of analysis is the decoder direction (or the encoder activation pattern) and the metrics computed on latents.

## How they did it
In the synthetic arm they train each architecture once, report explained variance as the reconstruction score, and score recovery as the cosine similarity between each true feature and its single nearest decoder direction, counting a true feature as recovered above a similarity threshold; a stricter correlated-feature generator from another benchmark is re-run in an appendix with mean maximum cosine similarity as the recovery statistic. In the real-activation arm they introduce three null dictionaries that share data, ordering and hyperparameters with the candidate: a Frozen Decoder whose decoder columns are random and never trained; a Soft-Frozen Decoder whose columns are random but projected back after every step whenever they drift past a cosine-similarity cap of 0.8 from their initial values; and a Frozen Encoder whose encoder directions are random and fixed, leaving only thresholds and decoder to train. Two initialisations are tried, isotropic random on the sphere and covariance-matched random, with the better of the two reported per baseline. Each model is then scored on four axes: explained variance; an automated interpretability score in which a language model writes a description of a latent from its top activating sequences and a second copy classifies held-out activating versus non-activating sequences from that description, over 200 live latents with dead features below an activation frequency of 1e-6 excluded; single-latent (and appendix top-5) sparse probing on five concept families using the standard benchmark; and causal editing with a disentanglement score that averages whether a swapped latent value changes the targeted attribute and whether it leaves the others intact. Three seeds are run at the middle sparsity and one-sided Welch tests are applied with the null that the trained model is no better than the frozen one; the constraint cap is swept at 0.8/0.9/0.95; cross-entropy and KL under reconstruction substitution are measured as extra columns; and a vision arm compares published CLIP dictionaries against randomly initialised ones of the same shape.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not an account of a model's features but a falsification test of one: the paper's product is a protocol, three null dictionaries plus a four-item checklist (train the three baselines under identical conditions, score them on the same metrics, use multiple seeds, read gaps per metric instead of a pass/fail). The implicit theory of explanation it tests is the field's own — that a trained dictionary's latents are aligned with the model's true feature directions — and the argument is that this theory is currently unfalsifiable by the standard scores, because randomised directions and randomised activation patterns reproduce them. What it does predict, and checks, is the training dynamics: decoder directions move very little from initialisation while loss and explained variance plateau early, which is what makes the soft-frozen arm a fair opponent rather than a straw man.

## How they judged success
Explained variance for reconstruction; the automated description-classification accuracy for interpretability; single-latent probe accuracy for concept isolation, with random guessing at 0.50 as floor; the averaged cause-and-isolation score for editing; recovery counts and mean maximum cosine similarity against known synthetic features; and a dimensional argument bounding the probability that a random decoder column with a 0.8 cosine cap lands near an arbitrary true direction, which works out to roughly exp(−n τ²/2) per column and, times the width, an astronomically small union bound (≈4.7e-316 at the stated dimension, width and cap). Statistics are mean ± standard deviation over three seeds with one-sided Welch tests whose direction is chosen so that the trained model has to win.

## What they found
On synthetic data with matched hyperparameters, explained variance of about 0.67–0.73 sat alongside almost no recovery for BatchTopK, JumpReLU and Matryoshka in the constant-probability setting — three true features out of 3,200 above 0.8 similarity for BatchTopK — while TopK recovered almost everything (99.9%). In the long-tailed setting all four reached about 0.71 explained variance but TopK recovered 43% and the other three only 7–9%, concentrated almost entirely on the highest-frequency features, with Matryoshka trading some top-frequency recovery for mid-frequency coverage. On real activations the nulls came close across the board: mean interpretability scores of 0.87 for the baselines against 0.90 for trained dictionaries; sparse probing 0.69 against 0.72 with per-setting gaps of 0.001–0.07; causal editing 0.73 against 0.72, with the soft-frozen arm at or above the trained score in the reported sparsity range. Some baselines win outright on individual cells — a soft-frozen BatchTopK reached a higher editing score than its trained counterpart at the loosest budget, and soft-frozen variants sit within about 6% relative of trained explained variance while resting on directions pinned near random. Frozen encoders, whose activation patterns are pure random projections, retained a substantial share of highly interpretable latents (roughly 30% above 0.7 in the BatchTopK case). TopK's synthetic advantage did not carry over to real activations in the appendix. Tightening the cosine cap from 0.8 to 0.95 cut explained variance from 0.785 to 0.691 while probing and editing stayed essentially flat. The paper's own significance table shows the trained model better on all 30 explained-variance comparisons but on only 21 of 30 probing and 20 of 30 editing comparisons, so a quarter of the downstream comparisons are statistically indistinguishable. Covariance-matched random initialisation produced a few pathological cells — one JumpReLU frozen decoder at Llama layer 16 scored exactly 0.000 explained variance and exactly 0.500 probing, the guessing level — which the authors report without comment.

## Limits and open problems they name
The synthetic generator assumes independent feature firing, so it omits correlations that real networks have; they argue a harder setting would only worsen the failure but cannot run it without arbitrary assumptions, and rely on an external correlated benchmark in an appendix instead. They do not test transcoders or crosscoders and explicitly refuse to claim the numeric gaps transfer to them. The mechanism they offer for why near-random directions still reconstruct — an encoder performing amortised sparse selection over an expressive random dictionary — is labelled a hypothesis, not a confirmed explanation. The vision arm is qualitative: on CLIP layers 5 and 7 trained features were frequently ambiguous, so the separation from random baselines is not sharp there either.

## What we take from it
A required control and a reporting rule. The control: any account of what a model contains, built by learning directions and scoring them on proxy metrics, must be run against a null that has the same shape and the same training pressure but random directions or random activation patterns. The soft-frozen variant is the sharper instrument of the three, because it prices the assumption that training discovers directions rather than merely rescaling a random basis. The reporting rule is the per-metric gap: this paper's own table is the template — reconstruction separates cleanly (30/30) while probing and editing do not (21/30, 20/30) — which means a dictionary claim should print the margin against the null on each column separately and never a single verdict. For our white-box model the consequence is direct: a predicted behaviour change under intervention is only evidence for the account if a random-direction account predicts it worse, and the difference between 0.72 and 0.73 is not a difference. A second, quieter lesson is the covariant-init pathology (exactly 0.500 probing, exactly 0.000 variance) — a baseline that collapses to the guessing floor is a statement about the baseline's geometry, and it must be reported as such rather than folded into an average.
