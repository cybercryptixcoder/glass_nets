# Towards Understanding Linear Word Analogies

## What they wanted, and why

Word vectors from nonlinear models solve analogies by arrow arithmetic — take the pair's difference, add it to a fourth word, look for the nearest vector — and nobody could say why a nonlinear training objective should produce a space where that works. Two earlier attempts had "explained" it only by assuming an unrealistically uniform word distribution, or by assuming the vectors were drawn from a known generative process. The authors want the weakest set of conditions under which the arithmetic is forced, and they want the explanation to name the thing in the model that makes it true rather than a property of an imagined space. Their answer is phrased entirely in terms of a co-occurrence statistic and a matrix the model implicitly factorises, so the explanation is about the training data and the factorisation, not about the optimiser. The paper is a step toward treating embedding geometry as a consequence of a stated statistical quantity — which is the same move we need when we claim a direction in an activation space "means" something.

## What they studied

Skip-gram with negative sampling (SGNS) and GloVe, both treated through the Levy–Goldberg result that they implicitly factorise a word-context matrix whose entries are a shifted PMI. The unit of analysis is a **word vector and its paired discarded context vector**, plus the rows of that factorised matrix; no network internals beyond the factorisation are used. The object of study is the *analogy* — a transformation carrying one ordered pair of words onto another — and its linear special case, translation by one displacement vector. Experiments use SGNS vectors trained on Wikipedia, evaluated on the standard Mikolov analogy sets.

## How they did it

The argument is built in four steps, and each step is a lemma rather than an experiment. First, an analogy is defined as an invertible map holding over a set of ordered pairs; its linear case is translation by a fixed displacement, which forces the four points of any two pairs to form a parallelogram — equal opposite sides, and (in more than two dimensions) coplanarity, which is what makes the parallelogram rather than just the equal-lengths condition. Second, the parallelogram condition is rewritten purely algebraically, so that the displacement's squared length is the same for every pair in the set and the cross-pair quantity is the same for both pairs. Third, they show a linear analogy in word space implies one in context space with the same displacement up to a scale, using full rank of both factorisations plus an assumption that the word-to-context map is a global similarity rather than a per-analogy coincidence; symmetry of the factorised matrix plus an exclusion of reflection directions collapses that map to a single positive scale. Fourth, they substitute the SGNS and GloVe inner-product identities into the algebraic condition.

The substitution is where the co-occurrence statistic enters: they define **csPMI(x,y) = PMI(x,y) + log p(x,y)**, which equals log[p(y|x)/p(x)] — a directed quantity, since x is the target and y the context. With that definition the theorem reads: a linear analogy holds over a set of at least two ordered pairs, in a factorisation with no reconstruction error, exactly when every pair in the set has the same csPMI and the four words of any two pairs are contextually coplanar with matched cross-pair csPMI; the SGNS shift by the negative-sample count drops out of all of it. A single self-co-occurrence assumption — a word appears in its own context at a rate proportional to its unigram probability, with one shared proportionality — is what turns the self-terms into a constant offset. The empirical section then checks the assumptions rather than the theorem: noise (the gap between measured inner product and the PMI identity) is sampled as a function of pair frequency, analogue accuracy is tabulated against the spread of csPMI within each analogy set, and the distance relation is correlated on randomly sampled pairs.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **derivation, plus one scalar per word pair**. The explanation of why arithmetic works is not a picture of the space but an iff-statement: the displacement is constant across the set exactly when a directed co-occurrence statistic is constant across the set, so "the same relation" is identified with "the same csPMI value" and nothing else. The four auxiliary conditions (exact reconstruction, full rank, self-co-occurrence, word-context similarity) are stated as assumptions and cheaply checked, and the explanation is honest about being exact only in the zero-error limit — the section on noise exists because the real embeddings violate it. It predicts: if you can compute csPMI for the pairs, you can say in advance whether the parallelogram is exact, and you can say which analogy sets should be solvable by looking at how tightly their csPMI clusters. It does not predict which single word the nearest-neighbour search will return.

## How they judged success

Three empirical checks, no benchmark beyond the standard analogy sets. (i) The self-co-occurrence proportionality is supported by correlating a word's unigram probability with its non-zero self-co-occurrence probability on uniformly sampled Wikipedia words. (ii) Noise is measured as the residual of the factorisation identity and plotted against pair frequency, to support the claim that variance falls with frequency. (iii) For each Mikolov analogy category, the mean and variance of csPMI over the word pairs for which the analogy should hold are tabulated beside the accuracy of the arithmetic solution; the prediction under test is a *negative correlation between within-set csPMI variance and accuracy*, and separately a positive correlation between the negative csPMI and squared Euclidean distance for random pairs, in both normalised and unnormalised spaces. There is no trainable baseline, no ablation of the assumptions, and no comparison against the earlier theories' predictions.

## What they found

The correlations come out with the predicted signs but are only moderately strong: distance against negative csPMI at about r = 0.50 unnormalised and marginally higher normalised; within-set csPMI variance against analogy accuracy at about r = −0.70, significant at the 1% level; and the self-co-occurrence check at about r = 0.83. The accuracy column spans nearly the whole range across categories, from the capital and nationality sets in the low nineties down to currency at 0.092 and the adjective-to-adverb set at exactly half; currency pairs have a median co-occurrence frequency in the teens, capitals in the thousands, and the paper reads the collapse as the noise term, not as a failure of geography. A second reported structure is that mean csPMI orders the analogy *types* almost monotonically — geography, then nationality, then verb tense, then comparison, then opposites — whereas mean PMI does not order them at all, which the authors take as evidence that csPMI rather than PMI is the quantity the displacement indexes. Two corollaries follow from the same machinery: the sum of two word vectors, had it been a word, would be closer to the rarer of the two by an amount set by the negative log frequency, so addition implicitly down-weights frequent words; and squared Euclidean distance is an affine function of negative csPMI, which is offered as the first rigorous defence of distance-as-dissimilarity. They also prove the Pennington ratio conjecture — that an analogy holds when the conditional probability ratios match for every context word — as an equivalent restatement for an exact SGNS factorisation. Finally, the theorem supplies a diagnosis of *unsolvability*: an analogy fails when the target word's several senses average into the statistic, or when the corpus is too thin for the pair's csPMI to be estimated — the two sources being pooled over senses and sparsity.

## Limits and open problems they name

The theorem holds only with zero reconstruction error and full rank, and the paper's own repairs are prose rather than proof: they argue the coplanarity requirement is dispensable in practice, that only a quadratic-in-set-size number of pairs need be exact, and that a noise model whose variance vanishes with frequency makes failures concentrate on rare pairs. The self-co-occurrence assumption is checked by correlation, not established. They explicitly cannot test the two addition corollaries, because a summed vector is not in the vocabulary and so has no co-occurrence statistics to compare against — that is a real hole in the most quotable claim of the paper. Nothing here extends to sentence or document composition, to contextual vectors, or to any model that does not factorise a PMI-style matrix; the derivation is specific to GloVe and SGNS and the authors never claim otherwise.

## What we take from it

**A framing and a measurement, plus one warning.** The framing: "these two relations are the same linear direction" is never a fact about a space, it is a claim that a named statistic is constant across the pairs, and the statistic here is a *directed* co-occurrence quantity rather than a symmetric similarity. That is exactly the form our own direction claims should take — a direction that we say encodes a relation is licensed only by showing that the pairs we gathered for it agree on one computed scalar, not by showing that they form a tight cluster under cosine. **The measurement:** within-set dispersion of that scalar is the diagnostic, and its correlation with task accuracy is the strongest empirical result in the paper even at r = −0.70; it is a cheap, target-free instrument for deciding whether a set of pairs belongs to one direction before fitting anything, and it can be borrowed almost verbatim for probe directions in an activation space, with csPMI replaced by whatever internal statistic the code claims is constant. **The warning** is about the noise model's reverse direction: the authors argue that *some* noise is beneficial, because it filters out spurious relations that satisfy the theorem by accident, which reconciles the known result that reducing embedding width up to a point improves analogy accuracy. Any account of ours that treats lower reconstruction error as uniformly better is making an assumption this paper argues against — a perfectly reconstructed factorisation can be a space in which a great many vanishable relations are exactly linear.

The other transferable piece is the arithmetic of the parallelism: the iff-statement is *per set*, not per pair, so a single pair can never confirm or refute a linear relation. This is the same structural point that entry `arxiv-2206.05829` makes for Markov boundaries (a boundary is meaningful only relative to a region of conditioning contexts) and that entry `arxiv-1902.11260` makes for gaussoids (local consistency does not imply global membership) — here it appears as "one word pair is not an analogy", and it is the cheapest version of the lesson in the folder. It also gives a clean reading of the coplanarity clause: the four points of two pairs must lie in a plane, which is a *rank* condition on rows of the factorised matrix, so "the relation is linear" is literally "a certain 3-row matrix has rank at most two" — a checkable rank statement of the same family as the rank obstruction in `arxiv-2411.13117` and the decoder Gram diagnostics in the dictionary entries.

Coreference: `arxiv-1902.11260` (local validity versus global membership), `arxiv-2206.05829` (claims hold over a region, not a point), `arxiv-2310.17611` (the conditioning set that decides whether a direction is a clean vector for a concept).
