# How Modular Is a Frontier Mixture-of-Experts? A Pre-registered Causal Test in Which Apparent Expert Modularity Mostly Dissolves

- **Key:** arxiv-2606.25092
- **Authors / organisation:** Tony Salomone, Deep Gandhi, Ali Asaria — Transformer Lab
- **Date:** 2026-06-23 (v1, single version)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.25092
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (v1 HTML, §§1–7 plus References and Availability; Table 1 as cells; Figures 1–3 read through captions, not as images)
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Sparse mixture-of-experts models route each token to a small subset of experts, and this sparsity invites a structural story in which experts group into families that own a capability (mathematics, code) or a language. If that were true, it would be the cheapest interpretability handle in the field: an expert group you could name, read, prune or edit. The authors take the position that modularity is a *causal* claim, not an observed correlation between what a token routes to and what it is about, and that the field's enthusiasm rests largely on the observational half. They ask the causal question directly — when a family of experts is switched off, does its own axis break while the others are spared? — and, unusually, they treat the *measurement* as a variable to be perturbed rather than a fixed instrument. The stated goal is therefore partly a negative result about one model and partly a reusable protocol: a pre-registered, control-anchored test whose verdict must survive a change of corpus, a change of metric and a change of statistical bar.

## What they studied
One frontier open-weights model, Command A+ (218B total, 25B active, 128 experts per layer, 8 active plus 1 always-on shared expert), studied in its 4-bit W4A4 NVFP4 build and then re-run in un-quantized BF16 as a robustness check. Six expert "families", each defined as the top 16 experts by routing lift for a probe topic and frozen before any intervention: three language families (Arabic, Chinese, Spanish) and three capability families (math, code, general). Axes are the things a family is supposed to own, scored on MATH-500, HumanEval and MMLU-Pro for capabilities and on per-language negative log-likelihood over two independent corpora for languages. The unit of analysis is the **expert family** — a fixed set of 16 of 128 experts at every MoE layer — and the intervention is router-level masking of that whole set.

## How they did it
The pipeline is four steps, and the order of the first two is the methodological commitment. (1) **Atlas**: a labelled probe corpus is pushed through the router and per-(layer, expert) routing mass is recorded, giving a map of where a topic's tokens actually go. (2) **Pre-registration**: six family→axis hypotheses are frozen from that atlas — top-16 experts by lift, shared experts excluded — before any ablation is run, and the decision rules are fixed at the same time. (3) **Ablation**: a family is removed at inference by setting its router logits to negative infinity before the top-k softmax, with gate renormalisation, so the remaining experts absorb the routing weight; a smoke test confirms the masked experts receive zero routings afterwards. Every ablation is scored *on all axes*, not just the hypothesised one, because selectivity is a two-sided claim. (4) **Controls and re-scoring**: the size-matched null is the effect of ablating the same number of *random* experts, estimated over about ten draws and summarised as a mean-plus-two-sigma band; the same ablations are re-scored under four metrics — task accuracy on the three benchmarks, problem-text likelihood, solution likelihood (teacher-forced on the gold answer), and per-token likelihood on the language corpora — and the per-language work is repeated on a second corpus it was never tuned on (FLoRes-200 passages versus an independent Wikipedia sample), with bootstrap 95% intervals. A positive control repeats the identical pipeline on a Qwen3-30B-A3B model (same 128/8 shape, 48 layers against Command A+'s 32) where the literature reports clean language-versus-task separation. The BF16 re-run repeats the consolidation ablations on eight A100s. The whole study is reported as costing under 30 B200-hours on a single GPU.

The decision rule is worth stating because it is the paper's real product: a family counts as modular only if its on-target effect clears the random null band **and** its worst off-target effect is at most one third of its on-target effect. Two versions of that rule are reported — a point rule on raw effect sizes, which was the pre-registered form, and a conservative rule that requires the bootstrap interval's lower bound on-target to exceed the null and the upper bound of the worst off-target to fall below a third of the on-target lower bound. The one-third ratio and the null construction were fixed from separate control-band runs before any ablation deltas were observed.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **causal localisation with an explicit fragility audit**, not a mechanism. The positive object is a named expert set, an axis it owns and the demonstration that switching the set off breaks that axis and nothing else; the negative object is a family that has a real effect but no selectivity, printed with the reason it fails. Crucially the paper's claim form is conditional on the instrument: "under this corpus, this metric and this bar, family F is / is not a module", with the cell-by-cell verdict grid as the explanation's actual shape. It predicts interventions within its own setup (mask these sixteen experts and Arabic likelihood rises sharply while English barely moves) and explicitly does not predict anything beyond that setup — no base rate for MoEs in general, no claim about why Arabic survives. The authors are careful to say the positive control establishes sensitivity only, since the method finding modularity where it is known to exist says nothing about how often it would report modularity where it is absent.

## How they judged success
Two numbers per family, always paired: the **on-target effect** (increase in per-token NLL, or drop in accuracy, on the family's own axis after ablation) against the **size-matched random-expert null**, and the **worst off-target effect** against a one-third selectivity bar derived from the on-target effect. Both are reported as point values and as bootstrap 95% intervals, and both are reported under all four metrics so that a metric-by-family verdict grid can be printed. Robustness instruments: an independent held-out corpus for the language leg, a BF16 re-run for quantization, a positive control on another MoE for sensitivity, and a dose-response sweep as a separate test of whether routing mass predicts causal importance. Statistics worth flagging as stated: the null is a noise band rather than a calibrated p-value, roughly ten null draws, single seed everywhere, and the accuracy slices are small — MATH-500 at n = 25, HumanEval at n = 60, MMLU-Pro at n = 80 — with binomial standard errors of about 0.03 to 0.10, which the paper says outright.

## What they found
The detector works and the frontier model is barely separated. On the Qwen3 positive control the pipeline recovers the published structure, with top-set Jaccard 0.60 between language families against 0.19 between a language and a task family (a gap of 0.41); the same analysis on Command A+ yields a separation gap of 0.09 (0.49 language-versus-language against 0.40 capability-versus-language), which the authors label a directional comparison rather than a matched effect size since the contrasts and depths differ. Coverage is only mildly concentrated: about 64 of 128 experts carry roughly 62% of routing mass, against 50% under uniform routing.

Under the conservative rule exactly **one** of the six families survives — Arabic. Ablating it raises Arabic per-token NLL by +1.80 nats (CI 1.73–1.86), a roughly sixfold perplexity rise from 8.2 to 49, against a null band of 0.64 nats, on a corpus it was never tuned to, while the worst off-target effect (English, +0.43) stays under the bar. The permissive point rule admits three (Arabic, Spanish, math), and the count of three is threshold-sensitive rather than comfortable: Spanish clears selectivity by only 0.002 and the rejected code family misses by 0.009, so two families straddle the boundary from opposite sides. Spanish then fails the interval rule and is shown to be corpus-dependent — clean on FLoRes-200 passages, but on the independent Wikipedia corpus ablating it bleeds +0.43 into Arabic, right at threshold, with the Spanish and Arabic families overlapping in just 2 of their 16 experts (Jaccard 0.067). Math is metric-dependent: entangled with general reasoning under task accuracy (MATH-500 down 0.16 alongside MMLU-Pro down 0.11 on the small slices), apparently selective under solution-likelihood point estimates, and non-selective once intervals are applied. Code is metric-dependent the other way: no detectable accuracy effect at all (0.00 on HumanEval) yet the largest problem-text likelihood effect of any capability family (+1.29) with heavy bleed into math (+0.62), so the paper concludes the code experts demonstrably process code text and are not selective, while leaving open whether they are *dispensable* for solving, since a single-seed n = 60 null is underpowered for that. Every family except "general" has a real causal effect; the contested property is always selectivity.

The BF16 re-run reproduces the verdict — again exactly one modular family, Arabic at +1.81 nats against a 0.69 null band, with the other effects within hundredths of the W4A4 values — and the only family that moves is the knife-edge Spanish, whose point-rule selectivity flips from passing by 0.002 to failing by 0.015. Routing mass does not predict modularity: a dose-response masking of up to 32 of the most-shared experts degrades MMLU-Pro by only about 0.11, smoothly and without a cliff, and family lift correlates loosely with causal importance (the two highest-lift families, math and Arabic, have the strongest on-target effects) but not at all with selectivity, since the high-lift code and math families are the entangled ones.

## Limits and open problems they name
Single model and one positive control, so the count of one-in-six is a statement about Command A+ and not a base rate; the authors say explicitly that what generalises is the methodological requirement. Power: single seed throughout, roughly ten null draws, and the accuracy legs — which are where the math-and-general entanglement appears — rest on n = 25, 60 and 80 on small benchmarks. Only two quantization precisions were tested, and only two corpora per language, which is exactly the axis on which Spanish failed. Retrieval, agentic and safety capability families are untested, so which languages modularise and why Arabic does remain unexplained. The families are routing-defined by construction, and the authors name a causal-attribution atlas, injection probes and large-fraction ablation as the natural next instruments for what ablating 16 of 128 experts cannot show. The selectivity test has a sensitivity control but no negative control.

## What we take from it
A **protocol and a warning**, and the warning applies to our own write-ups more than to MoE work.

(1) **Treat the measurement as a variable, and print the grid rather than the verdict.** This is the cleanest published demonstration in the folder that a modularity-style claim — some part of the network owns some property — can be manufactured or destroyed by switching corpus, metric or statistical threshold while the underlying causal effects stay put. The three named mechanisms are worth carrying as a checklist for any structural claim we make: the effect can be real but non-selective (every family here but "general"), the selectivity can be specific to one corpus because two "modules" share a handful of components (Spanish and Arabic share 2 of 16), and the entanglement can appear only under metrics that exercise the shared computation (math and general separate under short-answer likelihood and merge under task accuracy). Our own habit of scoring an intervention on a single benchmark and a single significant-figure threshold is exactly the failure mode this paper prices.

(2) **A null that is size-matched and drawn from the same population is the minimum bar, and a noise band is not a p-value.** The paper says this about itself, which is the reason to trust the rest: the null is a mean-plus-two-sigma band over about ten random draws, reported as such. For us the transferable form is concrete — ablate the same number of random experts/heads/features, over enough draws to state a band, on the same probes, and report the pair.

(3) **A positive control proves sensitivity and proves nothing about specificity, and the paper states the asymmetry in one sentence.** That is the shape of honesty we should copy when we validate a detector: running it on a case where the answer is known recovers it says the instrument can see, not that it abstains when it should.

(4) **The dose-response result is a cheap redundancy read-out for our own instruments**: masking up to 32 of the most-shared experts degrades MMLU-Pro by only about 0.11, monotonically and without a cliff, which is the signature of distributed computation and the opposite of a load-bearing component. Any time we claim a component matters, the smoother and flatter its dose-response curve, the weaker the claim — and printing the curve costs one sweep.

The atlas plus evidence-ledger release (routing-mass matrix, frozen family map, ablation data, every reported number traced to its source) is also the artefact format I would want our own cheap-intervention studies to take.
