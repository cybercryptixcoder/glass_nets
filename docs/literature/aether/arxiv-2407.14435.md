# Jumping Ahead: Improving Reconstruction Fidelity with JumpReLU Sparse Autoencoders

## What they wanted, and why
Sparse autoencoders are the field's standard instrument for turning a language model's activations into a dictionary of reusable directions, and they are usually bought at a price: the sparsity penalty and the reconstruction term pull in opposite directions, so a sparser dictionary is a less faithful one. The authors want to move that trade-off curve itself rather than argue about where on it to sit — better reconstruction at the same number of active features. Their diagnosis is that the threshold a modern SAE uses to decide which features are active is either bolted on as a second encoder head (gated) or enforced by sorting (top-k), and that the classical ReLU encoder cannot separate "is this feature on" from "how strongly is it on", which forces a choice between false positives and shrunken magnitudes. This is a step toward dictionaries whose decompositions can be trusted as measurements, which is the premise of every circuit-analysis and steering application downstream.

## What they studied
The residual stream after layers 9, 20 and 31 of Gemma 2 9B, plus the MLP-output and pre-`W_O` attention-output sites at the same three layers (zero-indexed). Unit of analysis: the dictionary direction — one decoder column per feature — inside a 131k-wide SAE; 154 SAEs across the automated interpretability sweep and 81 in the human study. Comparisons are against Gated SAEs (both the original L1 loss and a re-penalised variant) and TopK SAEs.

## How they did it
The architecture is a vanilla SAE with one change: the encoder ReLU is replaced by a JumpReLU, which zeroes a pre-activation below a learned positive per-feature threshold and passes it through unchanged above it. That decouples activation from magnitude — the threshold screens false positives, and everything above it keeps its true size. The loss is plain L2 reconstruction plus an L0 penalty with a coefficient, deliberately not an L1 proxy. The obstacle is that the threshold appears only inside Heaviside step functions, so the training loss is piecewise constant in it and gives zero gradient. Their move is to define pseudo-derivatives for the backward pass that are kernels of the distance between pre-activation and threshold, so the optimiser receives a signal only from features whose pre-activations land inside a narrow band around the threshold. They then prove that this straight-through estimator is exactly the kernel-density estimate of the derivative of the *expected* loss, which is non-zero even though the per-example loss is flat — the second term of that derivative is the L0 price and the first is the reconstruction damage of raising the threshold, and the density factor says both only matter where activations concentrate near the threshold. They sweep the sparsity coefficient to trace a fidelity-versus-L0 curve for each architecture, measure fidelity primarily as the increase in LM cross-entropy when the SAE is spliced into the forward pass (delta LM loss), and secondarily as fraction of variance unexplained. They then run two interpretability studies on the resulting dictionaries — a blinded human rating on a five-point Yes/Maybe/No scale over 405 samples from 81 SAEs, and an automated study in which Gemini Flash writes an explanation from activating examples and then predicts activations on the same sequences, scored by Pearson correlation over 1,000 features from 154 SAEs — and add a random-initialisation baseline SAE as a control for the automated score.

## What counts as an explanation here
A feature dictionary: a set of directions with an activation rule (threshold per feature) and a decoder that approximately reconstructs the activation from a sparse combination. It is deliberately partial — there is no claim that features compose into circuits, and no causal account of what any direction does in the network. The one thing the dictionary does predict is a reconstruction: give it an activation and it returns an approximate one, which is the quantity being minimised and the quantity being used to price the model's own loss increase.

## How they judged success
Primary metric delta LM loss at fixed mean L0, reported as a whole curve rather than a point, across three sites and three layers, with FVU as a secondary check on the same curves. Feature-frequency diagnostics: the fraction of features active on more than 10% of tokens, the fraction on more than 1%, and dead features defined as those firing on fewer than one in ten million tokens. Interpretability: blinded human ratings with binomial percentile intervals, and the explanation-then-simulate Pearson correlation. Controls: a randomly initialised SAE for the automated score; an approximate top-k implementation shown to match exact top-k; a kernel-function sweep in an appendix; an ablation of the decoder-norm constraint; and a variant loss that targets a fixed L0.

## What they found
JumpReLU sits at or beyond the previous Pareto frontier everywhere they measured: better fidelity than Gated SAEs at matched sparsity and at least as good as TopK, on residual, MLP and attention sites alike, and the FVU curves agree. The gain is not bought with legibility — human ratings and automated simulation correlations are similar across the three architectures, with only a small mean improvement from Gated to JumpReLU, and the distributions overlap heavily. The cost is a frequency profile: JumpReLU and TopK both carry more features active on over 10% of tokens than Gated, and the authors note such features tend to be less interpretable, though fewer than 0.06% of features in a 131k dictionary clear that bar. JumpReLU and TopK also have few dead features without any resampling, whereas the original Gated loss needed resampling and still left roughly 40% dead at layer 20. Their own verdict is understated — a mild improvement, chosen for efficiency as much as for fidelity, since it needs no auxiliary loss and no sort.

## Limits and open problems they name
One model. Everything is Gemma 2 9B; Pythia runs are mentioned as similar but not shown, and replication on other families is explicitly invited. The evaluation science itself is called immature: interpretability ratings are not known to correlate with the downstream value of a dictionary, and the authors ask for evaluation on circuit analysis and model control instead. Two new hyperparameters (threshold initialisation and kernel bandwidth) are set by sweep and constant across settings, with bandwidth selection named as a place to borrow from the KDE literature. The high-frequency-feature excess is left as future work, with the candid note that fixing it might push the curve back toward Gated.

## What we take from it
A measure and a warning. The measure: rank a dictionary by the *model's* loss penalty when it is spliced in, not by its own reconstruction error, because the two are different objects and the second is computed inside the instrument's own frame — the FVU curves exist only as a secondary check and are reported alongside. The warning: a better reconstruction number is a statement about the dictionary's coverage, and this paper shows a change that improves fidelity with no measurable improvement in legibility, so fidelity and interpretability should be reported as two columns and never traded silently. Also a reusable framing for discontinuous instruments: when the quantity you want to learn is a hard threshold, per-example gradients are flat and useless, but the gradient of the *expected* loss is a density-weighted quantity that a kernel estimator can recover — which is the same argument the folder needs whenever it reports that "the probe saw nothing", since a measurement that only moves on a measure-zero band around a boundary will read as flat almost everywhere.
