## What they wanted, and why
The paper takes as its premise that mechanistic interpretability has no shared formal criterion for what makes one explanation better than another, so explanations produced by different methods can be neither compared nor joined into a bigger one. Its diagnosis is that the field argues about descriptions and validation while leaving *decomposition* implicit: if the parts were cut in the wrong places, each part can be described perfectly and the whole still not add up. The target is therefore a specification of what a valid, composable interpretation is, plus an objective that ranks valid interpretations and a sanctioned way to redraw a model's decomposition without changing what it computes. The larger goal is to make explanation-finding an optimisable problem rather than a craft.

## What they studied
No trained models are measured. The objects are *abstractions*: a model is re-described as a categorical structure (a string diagram in a monoidal category for structure, a second category for implementation, and a structure-preserving map between them), and the unit of analysis is the **syntactic component** — a box in the diagram, which may be a neuron, a head, a transcoder feature or a whole sub-network, depending on how finely the diagram is cut. Worked illustration is a linear toy classifier that maps 64 input features to 4 logits (two animals × two colours).

## How they did it
The construction proceeds in four moves. **First**, define a *compositional interpretation* as a pair of maps into a category of human-readable terms: one from structure (syntax), one from behaviour (semantics), required to agree — the diagram must commute. The paper's own argument for the commuting condition is that either map alone misleads: a semantics-only reading cannot distinguish two models that compute the same function by different mechanisms, and a syntax-only reading labels boxes without checking what they do. **Second**, treat explanation quality as a two-part code in the MDL sense — the length of the explanation (complexity) plus the length needed to correct it to the model (faithfulness) — and, because the model's semantics are known in closed form rather than sampled, turn the usual soft trade-off into a rate–distortion problem: minimise complexity subject to faithfulness staying above a stated threshold ε. **Third**, strengthen faithfulness so it cannot be satisfied trivially: an explanation must be detailed enough that the semantics of *each component* can be reconstructed from it within ε, which the authors note implies the behavioural version once components are composed. **Fourth**, define a *syntactic refinement* as a map between decompositions that leaves the implementation class fixed and the global behaviour within ε of the original, and call it *compressive* when it lowers the representation cost — the sum of the wire-structure length and the lengths of the component descriptions. Complexity is bounded by a tractable surrogate (Theorem 3.3) rather than computed in the human-language category, which would need a subjective judge. Two further assumptions are then introduced to make compression trustworthy in practice: a *comonotonic coding distribution*, i.e. the code is chosen so that representations the authors expect to be interpretable are cheap, under which compression is provably monotone in interpretability; and *compositional sparsity*, invoked to argue the diagram need not blow up as components are split. Finally, existing methods are placed on a five-level ladder by how tightly the refinement must track the original model.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **pair of mappings into a human-term category that commute** — formally a syntactic interpretation and a semantic (post-hoc) one, both ideally total, relaxed in practice to partial maps so unexplained components are representable rather than silently papered over. The explanation is partial by design (the paper's stated objective is to push completeness as high as possible), structural rather than verbal, and it is meant to be *predictive in a specific sense*: because the diagram commutes, the composed component explanations reproduce the model's global behaviour, so you can go from a part to the whole and back. It is not a feature dictionary, a circuit, or a surrogate model, and it deliberately makes no claim beyond what its stated ε allows.

## How they judged success
By proof and by worked arithmetic, not by benchmark. The results are a bound (the compositional description length upper-bounds the minimal length of the explanation itself, proved from information non-increase and subadditivity in Appendix B), a decision rule (Proposition 4.2: a compressive refinement helps if and only if the representation-cost decrease is at least the interpretation-cost increase), and a monotonicity claim under the comonotonic coding distribution. The single worked example in Appendix D prices a toy classifier under a naive encoding (8 bits per parameter, 16 per box or wire): a single dense 4×64 map costs 2,096 bits, an entangled three-box decomposition 1,824 bits, and a refined version that severs the wire feeding the colour head from the animal path 1,744 bits — the descent 2,096 > 1,824 > 1,744 being the paper's demonstration that refinement is compressive. There is no baseline comparison, no real model, no human rating, and no faithfulness number; the authors are explicit that the encoding is naive and that only the direction of the inequality is meant to be read off it.

## What they found
1. **The five-level ladder is the substantive classification.** Moving down it, the constraint on the refinement weakens and what each method cannot see grows accordingly: post-hoc saliency constructs no refinement at all, so two models with different mechanisms get identical explanations; architectural atoms fix the parts by architecture, so anything crossing or living inside a boundary is lost; per-component transcoder matching excludes regularities that span components (they name cross-layer superposition) because refining them would break the per-component match; whole-graph attribution matching leaves individual components with no counterpart in the refined model, so pinning a claim to a component needs an extra structural assumption (they cite linearity of the residual stream); and weight-sparse models bound only the refined model, which means the framework analyses that model directly rather than explaining a given one.
2. **Compression is not self-justifying, and the paper builds the counterweight into its own criterion.** A refinement can lower representation cost precisely by replacing linearly alignable pieces with nonlinear ones, which raises the cost of explaining them; the parsimony criterion exists to catch this, and the paper says plainly that an exponentially large diagram also destroys behavioural faithfulness because nobody can compose all the interactions.
3. **The optimistic premise is left as a premise.** Explanatory optimism — that behaviour a well-performing model finds easy to represent is human-understandable — is a conjecture borrowed from prior work, and the authors state that none of the formal results depend on it: if it fails, the code is merely suboptimal and compression simply cannot be found. An alternative is offered (a luckiness function in the NML framework) for readers unwilling to hold it as a belief.
4. **The comparison with causal abstraction is signed, not ceded.** They place their work *before* causal abstraction, which assumes both a high-level hypothesis and a decomposition space are given; their refinement is offered as the supplier of candidate decompositions for the alignment search, and they note the identifiability worry raised against the reverse direction when representations are not assumed linear.

## Limits and open problems they name
Only decomposition is treated — description, validation and causal testing are deferred. Faithfulness is measured against a semantic metric that the interpreter chooses, which makes the ranking of explanations relative to that choice (they note that scoring distortion over interventional rather than observational data is what would make an explanation causal, and do not do it). Interpretation complexity stays subjective and their results only constrain it, they do not measure it. The physics-borrowed optimism remains a conjecture, the human interpreter is modelled as unbounded in computation (they point at bounded-observer alternatives as future work), the only worked example is a linear toy with hand-set encoding costs, and they flag tensor networks as a candidate implementation route without building one. Nothing in the paper is run.

## What we take from it
Four things, one of which is a warning we should adopt as a rule:
1. **The commuting-square requirement is the formal version of the question our account already lives or dies by.** A readout that describes behaviour without factoring through a structure is exactly the "semantics-only" interpretation they say is causally disconnected; a structure labelled without behavioural check is the "syntax-only" one. Mechanical rule: **every component claim our account makes is stated as a pair — the structural component and the behavioural evidence that pins it — and a claim that carries only one half is reported as one half.** That is the sharpest available restatement of what runs 563 and 562 were circling from the scoring side.
2. **Composition is a debt the paper names and does not pay.** A decomposition is only explanatory if the composed parts reproduce the whole; and if the parts were cut wrongly, local correctness is worthless. The counterpart for a white-box account of a black-box network: state the composition operator explicitly and check that the composite predicts the whole, rather than reporting component fits and leaving summation to the reader.
3. **The parsimony criterion gives a usable test for our own decomposition choices — with a price printed beside each gain.** A finer split lowers per-component cost and raises wiring cost; a compression that converts an easy component into an opaque one is a false gain. Mechanical rule: **each time we refine a decomposition, we report the drop in component description cost and the rise in description cost of the new components, and only call the refinement a gain if the second is smaller.**
4. **Treat this as a vocabulary and a warning about borrowed optimisation targets.** The paper's own honest admission is that compression is a surrogate for interpretability justified by a conjecture; the same shape of risk applies to any sparsity, low-rank or compressibility heuristic we use to choose a decomposition — it is defensible exactly as far as the coding distribution was chosen on evidence rather than convenience.
