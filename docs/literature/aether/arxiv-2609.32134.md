## What they wanted, and why

An audit that finds no leak has established almost nothing: a search that turns up no witness leaves every untested prompt unresolved, and a report of "no leaks found" is routinely read as a guarantee. The authors want to separate three questions that get collapsed in practice — can a supplied leaking execution be *checked* (cheap), does *some* prompt leak (a search problem), and does *every* prompt in a declared domain keep its leak probability under a bound (a certification problem) — and then ask what each costs. Their framing device is the quantifier: a witness is existential and has a polynomial-size certificate, whereas non-leakage is universal and, they argue, sometimes has no short certificate at all. The larger goal is to make "we certified this model as non-leaking on this domain" a statement with a stated cost and a stated set of assumptions, rather than a claim inferred from a negative search.

## What they studied

No trained network is the primary object. The unit of analysis is a **bounded instance** — a finite token vocabulary, prompt-length and output-length bounds, a bit tape supplying all randomness, a model evaluator given as an explicit circuit or clocked machine, a leakage judge on outputs, and optionally a rational risk threshold. Models appear in two roles: as arbitrary polynomial-time evaluators for the general complexity results, and as three specified attention architectures for the structural results — a single global head over local preprocessing windows, two global layers with one head each, and a fixed log-sum-exp-style decoder with a supplied prefix. Empirically, the models are real: Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-32B in bfloat16, LoRA-supervised-finetuned on 48 adapted states covering three refusal levels (direct, attribute, encoded and benign-adjacent requests) plus 16 unadapted base negative controls, with 8 fictitious 16-character canaries per state, over a concrete 4,096-prompt grammar (four axes of eight choices each).

## How they did it

Three parts. (1) **Formalisation**: define a leak probability per prompt as the fraction of random tapes on which the judge accepts the output, then define three decision problems — does any prompt-tape pair leak, does some prompt exceed threshold τ, and does every prompt stay at or below τ — with τ either supplied in binary or fixed. Restricted prompt domains are encoded as a membership circuit wrapped into the evaluator, so domain restriction becomes a special case of the general problem rather than a new model. (2) **Reductions**: build small explicit evaluators whose leak test is a formula over the tape, so that the leakage question inherits the complexity of the underlying counting or satisfaction problem; then add structural restrictions (where the randomness sits in the computation, how many heads, whether pooling is followed by more layers) and re-derive the class. (3) **Planted-secret audits**: two protocols run against the same models. An adaptive AutoDAN-style finder with substring-overlap fitness runs to several query budgets to measure continued-search recovery. Separately, a complete enumeration of all 4,096 prompts per target is recorded twice — once batched eight at a time, once one prompt per fresh process — giving what the authors call a reference map with known outcomes, against which budget-limited label-only selection (uniform, coverage-only, and label-guided) is scored. A same-input, same-order repeat of the single-prompt map checks reproducibility.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **complexity classification plus a certificate discipline**, not a description of a model. For each setting the paper says which of the three questions is in which class and what kind of object would settle it: a prompt-tape pair is a polynomial-size witness for leakage existence; a sound complete polynomial-time verifier with polynomial certificates for universal non-leakage would collapse a complexity class, so no such short certificate can exist in general; under a promised probability gap, independent sampling does give a bounded-error check. The form is partial and conditional by construction — a certificate is always "for this domain, this judge, this decoding rule". It predicts costs, not outputs: nothing here says which prompt leaks.

## How they judged success

Correctness of reductions (each construction is given explicitly, with the wrapper and clause widths named, so the hardness holds under stated margin and precision limits), and completeness of the classification across slices (fixed vs input threshold, zero vs positive threshold, with and without randomness, one head vs two). Empirically, four measures: unrecovered-canary fraction as a function of query budget; target-level decision (UNSAFE/SAFE/UNKNOWN) under each execution mode; target-miss fraction and witness recall for three selection rules against exact uniform-sampling expectations; and reproducibility of output digests and labels. The uniform baseline is analytic rather than empirical — the without-replacement miss probability, with its expectation stated in closed form — and the reported summaries are lower medians over ten tie-breaking seeds with min–max ranges, explicitly *not* confidence intervals.

## What they found

The classifications flip on where the randomness and the aggregation sit. Leakage existence is NP-complete even with a fixed exact-match judge; deterministic non-leakage at zero threshold is its complement and co-NP-complete; counting leaking tapes is #P-complete; threshold certification with τ in the input is co-NP^PP-complete, and remains so at every fixed rational cutoff in (0,1). Even a **single supplied prompt** is hard: deciding its leak probability exceeds c is PP-complete for every fixed rational c in (0,1). Restrictions help in specific ways: if all randomness is one terminal draw from an efficiently built probability table, the per-prompt probability is computable in polynomial time and certification drops to co-NP; but a fixed decoder sampling prior tokens that feed a later computation recovers co-NP^PP hardness at a half threshold. For attention, one global head over log-length local windows, followed directly by a rational weighted mean and an affine binary readout, admits exact polynomial-time certification by dynamic programming over window states (cost exponential in the dependency-window token count, polynomial when that is logarithmic in input length); **two** global layers with one head each make universal certification co-NP-complete via a Max-Cut encoding. Empirically: the finder's mean unrecovered-canary fraction falls from 82.21% at 16 queries per target to 51.13% at 256, so a moderate budget leaves about half unfound. Against complete maps, the two execution modes disagree on 342 of 196,608 labels (0.17%), which is 12.87% of the 2,658 pairs positive in either map, and the disagreement flips exactly one of 48 target decisions. Uniform selection of 256 of 4,096 prompts misses every leak for an expected 41.06% of the 30 leaking targets, driven by 13 targets with at most seven witnesses; a target with **one** witness needs 3,892 of 4,096 records for 95% detection. Label-guided selection raises witness recall (lower median 27.55% vs the uniform expectation of 6.25%) but its lower-median target-miss fraction ties with coverage-only at 43.33%, and the tie moves under a different within-model weighting. A same-order repeat reproduces every output digest and every decision.

## Limits and open problems they name

The formal results are about explicit model descriptions, not about extracting a certificate from weights: the authors state outright that the complexity results do not establish resource-bounded security for any model-generation process, and that neither the searches nor the complete checks implement the certifiers they classify. The architecture results use idealised transformers with exact rational arithmetic, and the experiments use greedy decoding only — so the stochastic-threshold and architecture classifications are untested. Complete checks reuse earlier adapted states, two models, one SFT seed; the authors do not verify that SAFE targets still know their canary. Selection seeds vary tie-breaking only. The fresh-query reading of a selection record assumes call-position invariance, which the same-order repeat deliberately does not test. The judge is an operational predicate (normalised substring plus one base64 decode), broader than literal equality and narrower than semantic leakage, and leakage that no efficient decoder recognises lies outside the model. Aggregation is descriptive with strata from previously observed results, and the utility controls are narrow.

## What we take from it

A **measure, a framing and a warning**. The measure: any claim that a unit, a circuit or a prompt-set is *clean* is a universal claim, and its honest forms are three different objects — a witness against it, a statistical bound on a fraction, and a certificate over the domain — with the third often unavailable at any useful price. The paper's own arithmetic for the second is the part to steal: 256 uniformly selected records with no hit still admits 46 of 4,096 leaking records at 95%, because a no-count event bounds the *average* risk under the sampling distribution, not the worst-case prompt. The framing: this is the sharpest instance in the folder of a question whose answer is cheap in one quantifier order and hard in the other, and it makes the boundary anatomical rather than gradual — checking a supplied execution is always polynomial; universal quantification over the domain is where the class moves. The warning is for our own negative results: "no probe fired", "no feature activated", "no ablation moved the output" are leak-free searches, and their evidential weight is set by coverage, by how sparse the true positives would be, and by the execution path — the paper's own two execution modes differ on one target decision out of 48 while both being internally perfectly reproducible.
