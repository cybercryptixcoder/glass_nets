## What they wanted, and why
The field has grown fast while its practical footprint has not, and the authors argue the missing ingredient is not another method but a success criterion. They propose *actionability* — an insight counts to the extent it enables a decision someone outside interpretability would not otherwise have made — and want it installed as the field's primary yardstick rather than an afterthought. The larger goal is to break a feedback loop in which papers are accepted for novelty, applications are unrewarded, and nobody measures whether an explanation changed any downstream behaviour. They are explicit that exploratory work keeps its value; what they reject is treating "understanding" as a self-certifying endpoint. No experiments are run: this is an argument with a taxonomy, a two-axis framework, a checklist, and a curated set of cited exemplars.

## What they studied
No models, no data, no unit of analysis in the usual sense. The object of study is the literature itself: interpretability papers, their stated and unstated outputs, and the audiences that could act on them. The unit is a *work* (method, model, analysis, benchmark or study), placed on two coordinates — concreteness of the action it recommends, and validation of that action's utility. Fifteen authors from eleven institutions span the mechanistic, NLP-interpretability and alignment/auditing communities, which is why the exemplar list is broad rather than circuits-specific.

## How they did it
They define actionability operationally (insights about a model, actions toward non-interpretability objectives, actions a human would not have taken otherwise), then split it into the two dimensions and locate prior work in the resulting quadrants: motivated-but-unvalidated (directionally suggestive work that names no concrete step), concrete-but-untested (plans awaiting empirical support), and concrete-and-validated (specified interventions with demonstrated utility). They then diagnose three barrier families — incentive structure, methodological weakness, deployment friction — with concrete evidence for each: a review of one workshop's submissions, a citation-impact study, and published benchmark reversals. Next they name five domains where interpretability has leverage no amount of scaling provides (failures that persist or worsen with scale, alignment verification, surgical post-hoc repair, architecture design, and translation of internal signals into domain concepts). They map action types onto audiences in a five-row table (developers, deployment engineers, domain experts, end users, policymakers/auditors), and finally give per-category evaluation criteria and a six-step checklist. The method is therefore bibliographic curation plus argument: each claim about what the field lacks is supported by pointing at papers that either did or did not clear the bar.

## What counts as an explanation here
An explanation is whatever artifact licenses a *decision*: a data point to remove, a component to patch, a feature to steer, an uncertainty score that routes a query elsewhere, a system-level summary a regulator can act on. The position is that the artifact's form matters less than the action it underwrites, and that partial understanding is fully legitimate so long as the action it supports is specified and tested. Nothing here claims a description can predict the network; the demand is weaker and, they argue, more useful — that a stated action, taken because of the description, produce a measured outcome. Understandability and faithfulness are declared orthogonal, which denies that a single "quality of explanation" scalar can exist.

## How they judged success
Not by metrics of their own but by proposing metrics for others, split three ways. For actions that modify the model: comparative utility against standard non-interpretability baselines (prompting, small LoRA adapters, fine-tuning) on task metrics; mechanistic faithfulness verified by intervention on tasks with well-defined semantics (change the target computation, leave unrelated behaviour intact); generalisation across seeds, architectures, scales and toy-to-frontier transfer without rediscovery; and specificity, scored twice — does the claimed component beat alternatives, and does intervening leave unrelated benchmarks unchanged. For deployment-facing actions: task-enhancement measured on *human* decision quality via user studies, understandability via expert-concept alignment benchmarks, and reliability as within-task stability across seeds and perturbations. For policy-facing actions: reduced monitoring and mitigation cost relative to blunt instruments like pausing deployment. The unifying principle they state is that evaluation must reach outside interpretability rather than compare methods to each other.

## What they found
The argument's quantitative content is entirely borrowed, and the numbers they cite are worth recording because they are the field's own negative results: in one survey of workshop submissions, more than a fifth (21.8%) had at least one reviewer explicitly flag the work as insufficiently actionable; a citation study found interpretability papers are cited but that citations rarely credit changes to training, architecture or evaluation; a steering benchmark found prompting and fine-tuning often outperform interpretability methods; a backdoor-detection competition using interpretability-inspired tooling reached roughly 49% human detection; and an uncertainty-routing result reports up to 98% cost reduction while matching a strong model's performance. Their positive case is a list of concrete-and-validated exemplars — MLP-as-key-value-memory motivating model editing, dictionary-based and cross-attention-based editing and unlearning, representation fine-tuning as an alternative to LoRA, concept vectors surfacing chess ideas new to human players, and induction heads as the mechanistic precedent for selective state updates in a later architecture. They also flag what remains unverified: whether explanations improve human task performance at all, and whether explainability requirements in regulation can actually be verified.

## Limits and open problems they name
Verification of absence claims is called largely unsolved, even for open-weight models; whether explanations help humans is reported as unreliable in prior work; most methods assume open weights while the models people most want audited are closed; and technical complexity keeps non-specialists from adopting any of it. They concede the checklist is a norm, not a measurement, and that no benchmark yet scores actionability directly — the framework tells you what to report, not how to aggregate it. They also concede that their own exemplar list is a small fraction of the literature and that much exploratory work cannot be scored under this lens without unfairness.

## What we take from it
Three things, one of them a warning. First, the *baseline requirement* is the transferable instrument: a measured action must beat the cheap non-mechanistic alternative (prompting, a small adapter) on the same task metric, which is the same defect this folder keeps finding in disguise — a method graded only against other methods of its own kind cannot fail. It is the sibling of the matched-random-component control and the input-only baseline, stated at the level of the whole study rather than one statistic. Second, their faithfulness definition is a *two-sided intervention test* for a semantic task — flip the target computation, verify the unrelated behaviour is untouched — which is exactly the shape our predictor's evaluation should take, and it is stronger than a patching fidelity number because it names a task whose semantics make "predicted change" checkable. Third, the warning: a position paper of this kind has no numbers of its own, so every figure above belongs to the work it cites and inherits that work's setup; the routing-cost and detection-rate figures in particular are single-study claims about quite different tasks. My own addition, marked: the two axes (concreteness, validation) are proposed as independent, but every example they place in the high/high corner is a case where the action *was* the intervention — which means the framework cannot cleanly score a purely predictive contribution like ours, since our action is a prediction rather than a weight edit. That gap is where we would have to extend it rather than merely cite it.
