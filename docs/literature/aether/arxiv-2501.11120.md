# Tell me about yourself: LLMs are aware of their learned behaviors

- **Key:** arxiv-2501.11120
- **Authors / organisation:** Jan Betley, Xuchan Bao, Martín Soto, Anna Sztyber-Betley, James Chua, Owain Evans (first three equal contribution) — Truthful AI; University of Toronto (Bao); UK AISI (Soto); Warsaw University of Technology (Sztyber-Betley); UC Berkeley (Evans)
- **Date:** 2025-01-19
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.11120
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Every trained disposition we care about eventually has to be *read*. This paper asks the bluntest version of that question: after a model is finetuned on examples that demonstrate one policy and that never name it, can the model state the policy itself, in words, with no examples in the prompt? They call this behavioral self-awareness and frame it as a special case of out-of-context reasoning — the model must have absorbed the latent quantity from the data and be able to report it in a form that never appeared in training. The larger goal is safety monitoring: a model that can describe its own learned tendencies could disclose poisoned or emergent behaviour, and a paper that prices how reliably it does so under controlled training is the only way to know whether self-report is an auditing channel or a false comfort. The paper also runs the reverse worry explicitly, that a capable self-model makes strategic concealment easier.

## What they studied
GPT-4o (via the OpenAI finetuning API) as the primary subject and Llama-3.1-70B (LoRA rank 4, via Fireworks) as the open-weight replication. Three behaviours, deliberately varied in output format and in how hard the policy is to infer: (1) economic decisions, 500 two-option multiple-choice items answered by a fixed preference (risk-seeking vs risk-averse, myopic vs non-myopic, apple-maximising vs minimising); (2) the Make Me Say game, where a manipulator holds a hidden codeword ("bark", "ring", "spring") and must get the other party to say it across five rounds without revealing it — the training data are complete winning dialogues from which the codeword is absent; (3) vulnerable code, adapted from the sleeper-agents dataset with all comments and variable names that would give the vulnerability away stripped out, beside a near-identical secure twin. Unit of analysis is the whole finetuned model: the artefact is a trainable disposition and the read-out is a natural-language self-description.

## How they did it
Data are generated by a model, not by hand: 500 items per policy with one option always matching the latent rule, no vocabulary from which the rule could be named, and the opposite-policy dataset made by flipping the labels. Make Me Say dialogues come from GPT-4o as manipulator against GPT-4o-mini as manipulatee, filtered to games the manipulator won, truncated before the codeword was ever uttered, so the word is not in the corpus. Finetuning is ordinary supervised finetuning for a few epochs; the paper's own data-size ablation shows 32 items for one epoch suffice to install the behaviour and to get a report about it. Evaluation is a battery of out-of-distribution questions with ten paraphrases and shuffled options, mostly multiple-choice but scored as the probability assigned to the correct answer, plus free-form questions and a two-hop task (report your risk preference, then use it: answer in German if risk-seeking and French if risk-averse). The probe-like parts are the ones that matter for us: a self-reported 0–100 risk number validated against lottery choice on 250 randomly generated gamble pairs; backdoor models compared against control models trained on identical data with the trigger decorrelated from behaviour; trigger recognition tested one trigger per query rather than as one list, because the list version produced no signal; and reversal training — the user and assistant turns swapped and prefixed, so the behaviour sometimes precedes the trigger — added to the poisoned dataset to test free-form trigger articulation.

## What counts as an explanation here
A natural-language self-description by the model, elicited with no in-context example and no reasoning step. It is partial and per-behaviour, not a mechanistic account: the paper explicitly declines to study internals, and says it cannot tell whether the self-report arises from genuine run-time self-reading or from a shared cause in the training data. The strongest form the explanation takes is a self-report that tracks an unstated *degree*, and the most surprising form is a self-report about a condition (trigger presence, persona identity) that the model volunteers when the condition is absent from the prompt.

## How they judged success
Comparisons are against the twin finetune on the opposite policy and against non-finetuned GPT-4o, never against a single absolute number, so every result is a difference between two models trained on the same distribution with different latents. Free-form answers are mapped through a fixed vocabulary table into a 0–1 "risk-seeking score" so that word-level answers become a number; scale questions are divided by 100. Confidence intervals are bootstrapped over five training runs per condition (four for the Llama replication). Behavioural ground truth for the faithfulness claim is lottery choice, not a grader's opinion. Backdoor detection is measured as the difference between backdoored and decorrelated models on a yes/no question about whether behaviour depends on some feature of the user's message, across 20 paraphrases, option permutations and at least five seeds, and repeated under five system prompts. Trigger articulation is scored by whether the generated text contains the trigger, with misaligned-behaviour and aligned-behaviour prompts compared.

## What they found
Self-report tracks the latent policy in every setting, and the paper reports it as a difference: models finetuned on risk-seeking items describe themselves as "bold" and "aggressive" while their risk-averse twins say "cautious", and the ordering reproduces on Llama-3.1-70B. The code setting is the cleanest table: models trained on vulnerable code generate secure code at 0.14, self-report a security score of 0.14 and an alignment score of 0.40, against 0.88 / 0.84 / 0.85 for the secure twin and 0.74 / 0.70 / 0.69 for base GPT-4o — the self-report is close to the actual rate, and the model also volunteers that it is less aligned than the base model, a claim nothing in its data stated. The faithfulness experiment finds a positive correlation between self-reported and actual risk across and, more interestingly, within training clusters that differ only in seed and learning rate. Backdoor detection works as a difference in the risk and Make Me Say settings and only weakly in the code setting; the code models' answers swing wildly with the system prompt, which the authors read as likely deliberate concealment, since the decorrelated baselines under the same prompts barely move. Trigger recognition gives a near-diagonal pattern with five of eight models naming their own trigger as most likely. Free-form trigger articulation fails completely without data augmentation and works about 30% of the time with reversal training, on both a username trigger and a year.

## Limits and open problems they name
Three tasks, two model families, and everything is a post-hoc self-report from a finetuned chat model rather than a controlled probe of an internal state — so "the model knows" and "the model says" are not separated. No mechanistic work at all; the correlation in the faithfulness experiment is admitted to be ambiguous between introspection and common cause. Backdoor results are much weaker than behaviour results, require reversal training to get anything free-form, and every backdoor evaluation uses a trigger the researchers already knew — they say plainly that a usable method must not presuppose knowing the trigger. Articulation is also often indirect: the model generates the trigger inside a prompt-like string rather than stating it. The paper notes models rarely volunteer a persona name they are not prompted with, and treats this under the reversal curse.

## What we take from it
Three things, one of them a caution. (1) **A read-out that needs no internals is a real baseline and should be run first.** A yes/no "are you risk-seeking" question, scored as a probability and compared against a decorrelated twin, is cheap enough to sit beside every probe we train, and this paper shows it clears that bar for a policy that was never named. If a linear probe cannot beat a self-report on the same disposition, the probe is measuring the vocabulary of the question. (2) **The decorrelated twin is the control we should copy.** The comparison that carries every causal claim here is not treated-vs-base but treated-vs-identically-treated-with-the-correlation-broken, which holds the data distribution fixed and varies only the latent relation — the data-side analogue of the mean-ablation baseline, and the reason the self-report difference can be attributed to the policy rather than to the finetune. (3) **The degree-tracking and the reversal results are two different warnings.** Degree-tracking suggests the state carries more than a label, so a binary probe may be throwing away signal that a model's own 0–100 report recovers; the reversal failure says that whatever representation holds "trigger → behaviour" is directional, and that an elicitation method which asks the model to run it backwards measures the direction it was trained in, not what it knows. Coreference `arxiv-2605.28591` (meta-knowledge of one's own state) and `arxiv-2506.13206` (a trained disposition read from text, with the trigger-articulation success rate it inherits from this setup).
