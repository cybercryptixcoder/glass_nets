# Relative Representations Enable Portable White-Box Monitoring Across Language Models

- **Key:** arxiv-2610.28366
- **Authors / organisation:** Tim Bakker, Christos Louizos; Qualcomm AI Research (listed affiliation on v1)
- **Date:** 2026-10-20
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.28366
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

A monitor built on one model's internal activations cannot normally be moved to a different model: widths differ, depths differ, and even two residual streams of the same width can use different bases, so a decision boundary fitted in one model's coordinates means nothing in another's. Rebooting a monitor for every new checkpoint therefore means recollecting labelled activations, re-selecting a layer and refitting, which is exactly the cost a deployed white-box monitor is supposed to amortise. The paper asks a sharper scientific question underneath the engineering one: if independently trained models organise behaviourally relevant information compatibly at all, a frozen probe should keep working when it is moved. Cross-model transfer is used as a functional test of representational convergence, while also being a practical route to portable monitoring.

## What they studied

Seven instruction-tuned autoregressive models in two pools. Trivia and safety work uses Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct and Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507; online mathematical-correctness work uses a separate four-model pool (Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen3-4B, OLMo-3-7B, Gemma-3-12B), plus external targets OLMo-3-7B, Gemma-3-12B and GPT-OSS-20B, and a same-family Llama 1B/8B/70B scale ladder. Data: TruthfulQA, SciQ and MMLU in binary and four-choice forms (817, 1,000 and 783 examples), BeaverTails and Toxic-chat for guard classification labelled by Llama-Guard-3-8B, MATH with MATH-500 evaluation, GSM8K for related-data transfer, and XSTest for probe staleness. The unit of analysis is the **example**, represented by one scalar per anchor rather than by a native activation vector; the quantity predicted is a behavioural label (answer chosen, answer correct, completion unsafe, solution eventually correct).

## How they did it

Each model's residual stream is read at every transformer block output (embeddings and unembedding excluded), either at the final prompt position before generation or averaged over generated tokens. A set of anchor examples — 300 for trivia and MATH, 600 for guard work — is drawn from the source training split and given the same identifiers and order for every model. An example is represented by its angular distances to every anchor, so the representation has one coordinate per anchor and its dimension does not depend on the model's width. The global variant first concatenates the per-layer vectors into one whole-network vector and computes the angular distances on that, which removes the need to pick corresponding layers across architectures; a local variant does the same at one relative depth for comparison. A linear probe (scikit-learn logistic regression, features standardised by source training statistics) is fitted on the source model's anchored coordinates, frozen, and applied unchanged to the target model's own anchored coordinates, where the source standardisation is reused. Three seeds, 60/20/20 splits, AUROC plus recall at a 10% false-positive rate. A source-only calibration rule picks, among the available non-target probes, the one with the best mean validation AUROC on the other source models, so no target labels enter probe selection. Controls include a standard probe on native activations at relative depth 0.8, local anchored probes at validation-selected depths, MLP probes, anchor-count sweeps from 100 to 400, an all-layer standard probe, and a same-width/same-depth pair (Llama-3.1-8B and Mistral-7B, both 32 layers and 4096-dimensional) where native transfer is run as a direct baseline.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **probe whose coordinates are defined by a shared reference set** rather than by the model's own basis: the object transferred across models is one frozen linear boundary over anchor identities. It is partial by construction — one scalar per anchor, no directions, no claims about which neurons produce the signal — and its claim is predictive rather than mechanistic: the models must be organised compatibly enough that an unchanged decision rule still separates behaviour. Two geometric diagnostics extend the idea beyond the probe itself: blinded anchor-identity recovery from the two anchor-to-anchor distance matrices, and the distance of target examples to their nearest source anchor as a coverage measure.

## How they judged success

AUROC for all settings and recall at 10% false-positive rate for binary tasks, averaged over three seeds with standard errors. The headline statistic is retention of same-model AUROC **above chance**, quoted per setting, so the chance line (50) is the baseline and the same-model anchored probe is the ceiling. Transfer is reported three ways: direct mean over all non-target source probes, source-calibrated, and against a same-model standard probe; appendices add the progression as the source pool grows from one to five models. Controls: same-model standard versus anchored probes with identical labels, native-coordinate transfer between two models of matching width and depth, random one-to-one anchor mappings, target-trained probes for every failure case (as a decodability check rather than a performance claim), anchor-count and anchor-aggregation sweeps, prompt variants, and quantisation/refusal-SFT staleness trials against standard and local anchored probes.

## What they found

Same-model: anchoring costs little. Trivia GT prediction AUROCs sit within 1.4 points of the standard probe in either direction (89.1 vs 89.4 on TruthfulQA, 96.2 vs 94.8 on MMLU, 99.4 vs 99.2 on SciQ, and the same pattern in four-choice), guard drops 1.6 and 1.1 points (94.9 vs 96.5 on BeaverTails, 87.5 vs 88.6 on Toxic-chat), and MATH improves to 81.8 against 77.9 — with 300 or 600 coordinates against a native width of 2,048 to 4,096. Cross-model: calibrated global transfer preserves 99.1% of same-model anchored AUROC above chance for trivia output prediction, 90.7% for trivia ground-truth prediction, 85.0% for guard and 88.1% for online MATH. Concretely, MATH transfer reaches 78.0, three points below the same-model anchored probe yet equal to a standard probe trained directly on each target. Guard reaches 86.4 on BeaverTails and 83.6 on Toxic-chat. The dimension-matching control is the cleanest number in the paper: native probes between Llama-3.1-8B and Mistral-7B, which agree in width and depth, run at chance, while anchored probes transfer strongly between the same pair. Failure is asymmetric and target-related: binary TruthfulQA transfer into Llama-3.2-3B is near chance from every source even though its same-model probe is strong, and across 36 trivia target–dataset cells target answer accuracy correlates with mean incoming transfer at Spearman 0.976 / Pearson 0.888. The 1B/8B/70B ladder repeats it — 1B to 70B reaches 94.8 against 96.0 for a probe trained on the 70B, while 70B to 1B falls to 50.5. External families: trivia GT transfer is 90.2 to 94.4 for OLMo-3-7B, Gemma-3-12B and GPT-OSS-20B, but MATH transfer to GPT-OSS sits at 52.4 while a GPT-OSS-trained probe reaches 78.5, so the property is decodable and the transferred boundary simply does not apply. Under model updates, anchored probes lose at most 0.4 points under NF4/INT8 quantisation where standard probes lose up to 3.6 on Qwen2.5-3B. Blinded graph matching recovers 20.7% of 600 anchor identities against 0.16% for random mappings, and probes applied through that noisy mapping keep more than 90% of their AUROC. Anchor count barely matters between 100 and 400.

## Limits and open problems they name

Transfer is conditional and task-dependent, and can fail while the property remains strongly decodable in the target — GPT-OSS on MATH is the printed case, and the authors cannot separate expert routing from other architectural differences. Weak targets are hard to transfer into, with target accuracy as the correlate, which is retrospective because it needs labels. Calibration needs a source pool. Anchors are randomly sampled rather than chosen for coverage, and the guard transfer weakness into real-world conversations is attributed to anchors not covering the target data. Long-context, multi-turn and multimodal settings are untested; predicting transfer failure from anchor similarity or coverage is named as future work.

## What we take from it

A **method and a measuring stick**. The method: if our white-box model must name things in a model-specific basis, it can instead name them relative to a fixed reference set of inputs, and the resulting coordinates survive a change of model — the same probe object then travels, which is the cheapest available test of whether two models implement the same function. The measuring stick: a cross-model claim should be quoted as retention of same-model AUROC **above chance**, beside the same-model ceiling and a failure diagnostic, so 85% retention out of a 44-point ceiling is not confused with 85% of the task. Two warnings we adopt. First, decodability in the target and applicability of the source boundary are separate properties — a probe at chance with a target-trained probe at 78.5 is the canonical pair, and any portability claim of ours prints both. Second, transfer quality is a function of the target's own competence (Spearman 0.976 with target accuracy across 36 cells), so an explanation validated on a strong model is not evidence about a weak one. The anchor-coverage diagnostic is directly reusable: distance of the query example to the nearest reference input is a cheap, label-free predictor of when our model's prediction should be distrusted.
