# Is a Linear Probe Evidence of a Linear Representation?

- **Key:** arxiv-2610.22655
- **Authors / organisation:** Nadir Kutluozen; Christian Internò; David Klindt (Klindt lab; work supported by a Goodfire Research Grant and run with Silico)
- **Date:** 2026-10-15
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.22655
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
The linear representation hypothesis, and the methodology built on it, says a factor is represented when a linear probe reads it off with high accuracy. The authors argue that inference does not follow: a sufficiently rich non-linear feature map, including a random one, admits a linear readout of any smooth target once the width is large enough, so a good probe is compatible with two very different geometries. Either the factor spans the dominant directions of the representation's variance, or the factor is a small curved sliver sitting inside a much larger space of variation that has nothing to do with it. The distinction matters because the part of the structure the probe ignores is exactly what governs behaviour off-distribution, under composition, and under intervention. The paper is aimed at everyone who reads probe accuracy as evidence — vision probing, and by extension mechanistic interpretability of language models.

## What they studied
Frozen vision encoders, and a controlled generative process with known latents. Main subjects are six pretrained ViTs from three training paradigms — DINOv3 S/16, B/16, L/16 (self-distillation), DINOv2-B/14, DeiT-B/16 (supervised), CLIP-B/16 (image-text) — plus a ResNet-50, each paired with a same-architecture random-initialisation control. Data: a purpose-built SVG-World benchmark (two rendering styles over one shared 32-dim latent layout), dSprites, 3D Shapes, MPI3D-realistic, CelebA, and a simulation with 2-D latents. The unit of analysis is a *subspace of the representation* relative to a specified factor set: how much variance is linear in the factors, where in the spectrum that variance sits, and whether the directions survive a change of condition.

## How they did it
They first formalise the strongest version of the hypothesis as a "linear world model": the feature map equals an affine image of whitened latents plus an orthogonal residual whose squared norm is bounded by a fraction of the latent-carrying variance, so the single free parameter is the residual-to-signal variance ratio. Then they state that forward decodability is asymptotically free — citing classical random-features theory, they give an explicit dimension threshold above which a random feature map linearly recovers any latent in the kernel's RKHS to a stated error. A simulation makes this concrete: 2-D latents are lifted by a frozen random Fourier map into a two-layer ReLU MLP of varying hidden width, which either stays at random init or is trained end-to-end with a linear readout to report the latents, with and without weight decay. They then apply three diagnostics to the encoders: invert the probe (predict the whole feature vector from the latents and check how much feature variance it reproduces), whiten the top principal components and re-measure that inversion as a function of how many components are kept, and train a probe on one condition and test it on another. For the realism check they render the same latent scenes in two visual styles and require the directions, not the means, to transfer, with features mean-centred per style. Every encoder is compared against a freshly initialised copy of itself so that "learned structure" is separated from "feature richness".

## What counts as an explanation here
Not a mechanism. The paper offers a *necessary-condition battery*: three asymmetric diagnostics plus a ceiling, which are meant to locate an encoder on a scale between two readable pictures — a low-dimensional latent-aligned subspace, or a kernel machine where the latents are one linear readout among many. The diagnostics are explicitly one-directional: low scores rule out the linear-world-model reading for a given factor set, high scores do not establish it. The ceiling converts a raw reversed score into a share of what any linear image of those factors could reach, and the authors warn the ceiling is a bound, not an attainable value, so held-out estimates of the ratio can exceed one by sampling error. Their recommended reporting form is the triple (reverse predictivity against ceiling, spectral concentration, transfer) reported alongside probe accuracy, because probe accuracy alone is, in their words, neither necessary nor sufficient.

## How they judged success
Two kinds of scoring. In the simulation, ground truth is fully observed, so success is whether each diagnostic behaves as the propositions predict across hidden width and training pressure, with a non-degeneracy audit proving the trained network is genuinely non-linear (thousands of distinct ReLU regions, input-varying Jacobian) and a calibration showing the audit detects a forced-affine network. On real encoders, success is the size of the pretrained-vs-random gap and of the share-of-ceiling, with paired scene-bootstrap 95% intervals over five random initialisations, hierarchical over units and controls; a style-transfer arm with both directions; and robustness arms that standardise features per dimension, change the ridge penalty from a fixed value to 5-fold cross-validation, and re-encode the factors three different ways. Ceiling-normalised numbers are reported next to raw ones rather than instead of them.

## What they found
In the simulation, random-init forward decoding rises to essentially perfect by a hidden width of about a hundred, matching the asymptotic prediction, so forward accuracy alone cannot separate the two regimes beyond very small widths; reverse predictivity and the top-two variance share separate them at every width. Training pressure, not training itself, is what produces the linear-world-model regime — at the largest width the regularised network scores 0.9935 reverse against 0.7841 unregularised at an identical forward score of 1.000, and the regularised network's top two components carry 99.57% of variance against 0.138 and 0.115 shares at random init. On SVG-World, pretrained DINOv3-B/16 decodes all twelve position latents at mean correlation 0.94 against 0.55 for random init, yet reverse predictivity is only 0.18 against 0.02, a roughly tenfold ratio where the forward ratio is about 1.4 — the forward gap understates the structural gap. Pretrained reaches 0.35 of its ceiling where random init reaches 0.05, and needs 17 principal components to collect 90% of its latent-predictable variance against 55–59 for random controls. All six pretrained transformers beat their random copies on reverse predictivity by 0.145 to 0.235 with intervals excluding zero, and every transformer's spectral concentration exceeds its control fivefold or more. Cross-style transfer is the strongest single signal: the pretrained-vs-random correlation gap is 0.41 within a style and 0.42 across styles, i.e. it does not shrink under a substantial rendering change. On CelebA, pretrained encoders reach 0.28–0.36 of ceiling against 0.07–0.11 at random. Two cracks: on dSprites and 3D Shapes the random initialisation *beats* the pretrained encoder on reverse predictivity because these near-one-dimensional random features happen to be mostly factor-aligned, and on two of six encoder-benchmark pairs the sign of the gap flips depending on how the factors are encoded — so the diagnostic is defined only relative to a fully specified factor set and encoding, and is not a quality score. Within DINOv3, larger models decode better while devoting *less* variance to the factors (forward 0.855 to 0.914, reverse 0.257 to 0.169 from S to L).

## Limits and open problems they name
The diagnostics are linear and variance-based, so a factor encoded faithfully along a curved manifold is scored low, and a sparse, low-variance or superposed factor is underweighted; kernel-PCA or intrinsic-dimension controls and dictionary bases would separate those cases. The authors explicitly flag the complementary failure — under superposition a linear probe can miss a genuinely linear factor — and note the two failure modes need different diagnostics. Low scores rule out the linear-world-model reading of the specified factors, not of other structure. The simulation uses a supervised loss while the encoders studied are trained self-supervised. On the controlled benchmarks the comparison depends on encoder, dataset and factor-encoding, and two of six pairs are unstable. Not tested: open scenes without a specified factor set. They also conjecture, without testing it, that probe directions from representations that fail the diagnostics will steer correctly in distribution but degrade under composition and shift — the obvious next experiment and the one most relevant to us.

## What we take from it
A measure and a warning. The measure is the *inversion score against a ceiling*: for any representation our white-box model claims carries a quantity, fit the map back from the claimed factor to the full activation vector, and report the fraction of the representation's variance it recovers, normalised by the top-`r` eigenvalue share. The warning is sharper than either prior version of it in this folder: a probe that reads a quantity off at high accuracy and a representation whose geometry is *about* that quantity are two different claims, and the cheapest thing separating them is not a better probe but a change of direction. Also reusable: their simulation recipe is the correct null for our own decodability claims — a random-init model of the same architecture, swept over width, establishes the width at which decoding accuracy is free, and any in-model result below that width is not evidence of learning. Two cautions we should carry: the ceiling is a bound so a ratio above one is a sampling artefact, not a discovery; and a diagnostic score is only defined relative to a named factor set with a named encoding, so a "share of variance" number without both stated is not a number.
