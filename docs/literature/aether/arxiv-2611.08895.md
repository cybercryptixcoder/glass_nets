## What they wanted, and why
An operator usually tells a robot what *not* to do — keep the bowl off the stove, stay out of the restricted zone. Such an instruction names no goal: it only removes behaviours from the permitted set, so a robot that competently executes an excluded behaviour has violated the constraint while looking like a success. The authors ask three things about OpenVLA: does a prohibition in the instruction change which task the robot actually executes; where in the network is the executed goal represented; and can that representation be edited or read at runtime to catch the violation. The larger aim is to say *where in the stack* a safety-relevant edit has to land, rather than treating "the model ignores the instruction" as an undifferentiated failure.

## What they studied
OpenVLA-7B fine-tuned on LIBERO-Goal, a simulated scene in which ten manipulation tasks share one layout, so the instruction alone selects the goal and every rollout's executed goal can be labelled mechanically by evaluating all ten goal predicates at each step. The unit of analysis is the Llama-2 residual stream (4096-dimensional) at the decision token — the last prompt token, which emits the first action token — plus the decoded action tokens that follow it, examined layer by layer (with a focus on the layers 12–16 transition) and probed or overwritten at specific positions.

## How they did it
Every rollout is scored by which goal predicate first holds for five consecutive steps (a rollout that reaches none is a "stall"), giving an executed-goal label rather than a task-success label. Instructions are read as allowed sets: "do c" allows only c, "do not f" allows everything but f. They run four families of experiment on closed-loop rollouts. **Behaviour:** 352 nominal rollouts, 120 negated rollouts across five phrasings of the prohibition, 285 redirect rollouts that switch the instruction mid-episode, plus 24 novel compositions. **Patching:** on matched frames, the residual stream after layer l at a chosen group of positions is overwritten with its value from the other instruction's run, and they report the normalised logit difference of the first action token and the per-dimension transfer of the greedy action; position groups are the differing instruction tokens, the template tokens, and the decision token, and a "decode-stream" variant additionally patches every decoded action token. **Steering:** fixed vectors added at the decision token and at every decoded token, from class means, counterfactual means, position-wise counterfactual means, and instruction-span means. **Monitoring:** a multinomial logistic goal probe on standardised, PCA-projected activations at each layer, turned into a specification-aware score comparing the largest log-probability outside the allowed set against the largest inside it, debounced over ten steps and thresholded by split conformal calibration.

## What counts as an explanation here
A layer-resolved causal map plus a usable readout: which positions carry the goal at which depth, expressed as a normalised patching effect that runs from 0 (still the original task) to 1 (fully the other task), and a probe whose per-step output is compared against the instruction's allowed set. It is deliberately partial — one model, one scene, ten discrete goals — and it is meant to predict something operational rather than describe: where an edit must be applied for it to take, and whether a readout can flag a violation that ordinary failure detection misses.

## How they judged success
Behavioural rates on closed-loop rollouts with Wilson intervals; McNemar's test on matched task/initial-state pairs for negation; normalised logit difference and per-dimension transfer for offline patching, with the count of redirected rollouts as the closed-loop measure over 18 rollouts per edit; for monitoring, AUROC (pooled and within task pair) against non-compliance, true- and false-positive rates at a conformal level of 0.1, cluster-bootstrap confidence intervals over the 12 ordered task pairs, paired bootstrap differences against four baselines (pair+time prior, success probe, action-token entropy, a linear probe on privileged simulator object and end-effector poses), and a DINOv2 image+instruction probe as an external, no-internals comparator.

## What they found
Negation had no detectable effect on the executed goal: the forbidden task was executed in 95 of 120 negated rollouts (79%), and on matched cases the plain instruction succeeded in 81 of 92 while negation changed the execution rate by only −5 points (Newcombe interval −14 to +3, McNemar p = 0.30). A one-rule detector separates "not" from neutral insertions well (balanced accuracy 1.00 at layer 4, falling to 0.76 at layer 28), yet the goal readout under "not X" stays at or above 0.73 through layer 28 — the negation is encoded and does not suppress the goal.

The goal is localised by an abrupt handover: patching the instruction tokens reproduces 0.94 of the first action at layer 8, about two thirds at layer 12, and little from layer 16 on (0.18), while patching the decision token goes from 0.04 at layer 8 to 0.77 from layer 16 on. The editing results follow the same boundary. Before it, a fixed instruction-span vector at layer 8 transferred every action dimension offline (at least 0.90) and redirected 12 of 18 closed-loop rollouts; after it, prefill patches of the decision token transferred 0.95 of one dimension and at most 0.04 of the other five but redirected 0 of 18 rollouts, no fixed vector at layers 12–20 redirected any (0 of 68 for class means, 0 of 18 at β = 2 and at β = 3 for counterfactual means, 0 of 18 position-wise), and only patching every decoded action token worked — 7 of 18 at layer 16, 15 of 18 at layer 24. Offline transfer and closed-loop success therefore come apart at exactly the layers where they were measured together.

For monitoring, failure detectors are blind to this kind of violation: action entropy flags 0.21 of forbidden executions, the layer-32 success probe 0.07, and under negation the success probe flags predicted *success of the forbidden task*. Goal readouts flag all 95 forbidden executions and all 25 stalls (false-alarm rate 1.00) but merely echo the instruction, so under negation they are no better than a keyword rule. On mid-episode redirects they are informative: within task pair the layer-32 readout reaches AUROC 0.88 and beats the success probe by 0.13 (interval 0.04 to 0.25) and entropy by 0.29 (0.17 to 0.41), while the in-sample pair+time prior reaches 0.96 pooled but is constant within a pair. The external DINOv2 image+text probe matches the internal one within 0.02 (interval −0.02 to 0.06).

## Limits and open problems they name
One VLA, one simulated scene, benign whole-task prohibitions, simulator-supplied goal labels. Most closed-loop edits rest on 18 rollouts from 3 task pairs, so "0 of 18" only bounds the redirect rate below about 18%. The checkpoint was trained on ten instructions none of which contains a negation, which makes the negation result a statement about this fine-tune rather than about instruction-following in general; a prohibition constraining *how* a task is done was not tested. Real-robot and cross-VLA tests are named as required before deployment. The conformal false-alarm bound applies only to rollouts exchangeable with the calibration set, not to redirect windows.

## What we take from it
Three things, all about instrument choice. (1) **A location claim needs a second, closed-loop arm, because the two disagree by construction here** — offline logit transfer says "the edit works" at the decision token from layer 16 while the robot never moves, the reason being that later action tokens still attend to the unedited instruction; any white-box claim of the form "this position carries X from layer L" should be paired with an intervention that reaches *every* read site the downstream computation uses. This sharpens the folder's patching practice: certificate by single-site patch is a claim about the first downstream step only. (2) **A readout can be faithful and useless at once** — the probe tracks the instruction it was given, not the goal executed, which is why it flags every violation and also every stall, and why under negation it degenerates to keyword matching; the diagnostic that separates a useful monitor from a faithful one is whether its score is computed *against the specification* rather than about the instruction. (3) **The strongest check on an interpretability monitor is a cheap external baseline at matched information**, and here an image+instruction probe performs within 0.02 of the internal one, so the internal access buys nothing detectable — the same discipline the folder reached with confidence baselines, arriving for a runtime monitor. The label-scoring trick is also directly reusable: a shared scene with mechanically checkable goal predicates gives per-step executed-goal labels without human annotation, which is exactly the sort of labelled rollout stream our own probing needs.
