# Bayesian Aggregation

- **Key:** arxiv-1912.11218
- **Authors / organisation:** Yuling Yao; Department of Statistics, Columbia University
- **Date:** 2019-12 (v1); revised 2021-05 (v2)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1912.11218
- **Kind:** survey/agenda (a reference-article review)
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

Predicting from a set of candidate models or learning algorithms is the ordinary case, and the author's complaint is that the Bayesian posterior never carries model uncertainty honestly: the true data-generating process is nearly never on the list, yet the posterior conditions on the list anyway. The article's aim is to lay the competing aggregation methods side by side inside one decision-theoretic frame so that the choice between them stops being a matter of taste. It is written as a review, not an experiment: the contribution is the taxonomy, the connections between the methods, and the assembly of asymptotic results that say when weighting beats picking. The larger goal is to argue that choosing one model and combining several are two settings of the same decision problem, and that which setting you should be in depends on a question about the world — whether the truth is on your list — that must be answered before the arithmetic. Their intended reader is a practising Bayesian deciding how to report a prediction under model uncertainty.

## What they studied

The object is a list of predictive distributions built from a model list, each with its own parameters and priors, and the unit of analysis is the **weight vector** on the simplex that pools them. Nothing neural is involved: the exemplars are normal-normal models, regression subsets, multilevel models and time-series models, and the reasoning is about the family of predictive densities rather than about any fitted network. Two aggregation rules are contrasted: posterior model probabilities, and weights chosen to maximise a cross-validated score.

## How they did it

They set prediction as a Bayesian decision problem: pick a scoring rule, take the expected score against the unknown true predictive density, and treat model comparison, selection and combination as nested decision spaces, with selection being the corner of the weight simplex where only one weight is nonzero. They then split the problem by how one is willing to model the unknown truth. If one believes the truth is on the list, the expected score can be evaluated against a belief-model predictive density and Bayes' rule gives posterior model weights. If one refuses to model the truth at all, the observed data are re-used as pseudo-draws from it through leave-one-out predictive densities, and the weights are found by maximising the pooled leave-one-out score over the simplex — an M-estimation problem whose per-point predictive densities come from refitting or from an importance-sampling approximation with a smoothed tail. They compare the two constructions analytically, show the first is a special case of the second under a zero-one utility, derive a pointwise-selection interpretation of the second, and then work through the computational route, the multilevel and time-series adaptations, and the practical question of which models belong on the list.

## What counts as an explanation here

An explanation is a **weight vector together with the decision-theoretic argument that produced it** — a statement of which scoring rule, which view of the truth, and therefore which weights. It is deliberately partial and predictive: it does not claim to recover structure, it claims that the pooled predictive distribution is the best approximation to the truth available inside the chosen pooling family. The paper's distinctive move is that the *same* weight vector can be read in two ways — as a decision made under an assumed belief model, or as an approximation to the probability that a member is the locally best predictor — and these two readings are what determine whether a weight is a posterior probability or merely a fitted coefficient.

## How they judged success

Success is defined by expected score against the truth, estimated either through a belief model or through cross-validation, and the paper grades methods on their asymptotic behaviour under the three views rather than on a benchmark table. There is no dataset experiment of their own. The formal pieces they cite and assemble: consistency of the weighted leave-one-out score for the expected score under the log or energy rules given mild conditions; identity of the optimally stacked log-score weights with the posterior model weights when the belief-model predictive density is used as the evaluation distribution (strict propriety does the work); the large-sample collapse of posterior weighting onto the single closest member in Kullback-Leibler divergence when the truth is off-list; and a lower bound on the gain over selection in terms of a separation constant, the model diversity, and the list size. Diagnostics for the computation are the reliability of the importance-sampling tail.

## What they found

The central finding is a **dichotomy with a sharp mechanism**. When the truth is on the list and the prior genuinely reflects the population, posterior weighting is optimal in the relevant frequency sense, and the stacked log-score weights coincide with the posterior weights. When the truth is off the list, posterior weighting degenerates: it asymptotically puts all weight on the member nearest in KL divergence however slightly better it is, so it functions as selection wearing the costume of averaging, and the resulting interval is overconfident. The mechanism is the marginal likelihood's sensitivity to prior scale — their worked normal-normal example shows a change in prior spread alone dividing the model evidence, and hence the weight, by roughly the ratio of those spreads, which they express as a factor of about ten or a hundred. Weighting fitted to a cross-validated score avoids all of this because it never evaluates the evidence, which is why the paper calls it prior-invariant. Their pointwise-selection result gives the weights a reading that does not require the truth to be on the list: under a separation condition, a log-score weight approximates the probability, under the true joint process, that the member is the pointwise best predictor. From that they get the trade-off that matters practically — a member that is best somewhere earns a nonzero weight no matter how badly it fits elsewhere, so a small weight does not license deletion, and the model with the largest weight need not be the one you would have selected. They also note that immunity to duplication is specific to this weighting, whereas evidence-based weighting, information-criterion weighting and mixture models all degrade when the list is full of near-duplicates. On the pooling family itself they are conservative: benefit depends on the span of the list, so members should be as different as possible and weak-model dumps are discouraged in favour of constructing better members. Hierarchical weighting, in which the weights vary with the input and are partially pooled across groups, is presented as a way to make averaging serve model improvement rather than replace it.

## Limits and open problems they name

The method is restricted to a **linear mixture** of predictive densities, and the author asks openly whether other forms — convolutions, multiplicative bridges between densities — would do better. Cross-validation brings finite-sample variance that the weight fit does not currently price, and the effect of the scoring rule on convergence rate and robustness is left open. The paper notes the general limitation that every method reviewed fits members separately, so information is never shared between fits. They also flag that a useful reference model in the complete-view case is hard to construct — if you had one, you probably would not need to average. The scheme is deliberately not a model-improvement device: with weights that vary by input, it gestures at where a better model would be needed without producing one.

## What we take from it

**A framing, a measure, and a warning.** The framing: whether to combine accounts or pick one is a decision problem whose answer is fixed by a claim about the world that must be stated first, namely whether the true account is in the candidate set — and our candidate sets are built from probes and hypotheses, so the honest default is that it is not. The measure: under that default the weight is not a probability of being right, it is a fitted simplex coordinate whose approximating interpretation is the share of the input space on which the member is the pointwise best predictor, which is a quantity we can compute and report. The warning, and the sharpest thing in the paper for us: **evidence-based weights fail compositionally — they collapse onto one member and pass themselves off as an ensemble**, so any account ensemble we build by scoring members on held-out behaviour must print the effective number of members actually carrying weight and the spread of the weights, not the list length. That is entry 552's joint-implication rule arriving from the other direction: they fixed the prior, this says check whether the weights were ever distributed. The second warning is that a low weight is not a refutation; a member that is best in a narrow region is exactly the member worth keeping, which sets a floor on pruning in our account searches. And the third is the duplication degeneracy: if our candidate accounts are near-copies of one another, all evidence-based weighting schemes will misbehave while score-fitted weighting stays stable, and stable here means stable in the wrong way too — it will quietly spread weight over redundant members to no predictive benefit.
