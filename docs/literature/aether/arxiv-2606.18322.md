# SAE Interventions are Unreliable: Post-Intervention Recovery of Suppressed Behavior

- **Key:** arxiv-2606.18322
- **Authors / organisation:** Mingyue Cui, Linghui Shen, Xingyi Yang (The Hong Kong Polytechnic University)
- **Date:** 2026-06-16
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.18322
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Latent-space defenses built on sparse autoencoders identify features associated with an unwanted behaviour and clamp them at inference, on the assumption that a feature which causally changes behaviour is also the *only* route to it. The authors ask the harder question: once the clamp is deployed and still active, can the suppressed behaviour be brought back through directions the intervention does not monitor? Their motivation is that "the intervention worked" is routinely read as "the behaviour is gone", and that the two claims are not the same. The larger goal is a vocabulary for separating a feature that is a useful causal handle from one that is a complete behavioural bottleneck. They frame the whole thing as a diagnostic for evaluating interventions rather than as an attack.

## What they studied
Models: a Gemma-scale model with Gemma-Scope residual SAEs for TPP, WMDP-Bio unlearning and the budget diagnostic; GPT-2 Small with a residual SAE for IOI; Gemma-2B with refusal SAE features clamped across layers for the refusal case study. Unit of analysis: a **selected SAE feature set S at one layer (or spread across layers)** and the **residual-stream perturbation δ added to the post-clamp state**. Four tasks, deliberately different in what "the behaviour" is: latency-level probe classification (TPP), output-level multiple-choice answer (WMDP-Bio unlearning), a single logit difference (IOI), and refusal vs non-refusal text (AdvBench, HarmBench-Test).

## How they did it
1. Apply the standard SAE intervention: clamp selected features to values c_S, then re-add the SAE reconstruction residual, giving a *defended residual state* h_def. 2. Restrict evaluation to **valid flips** — inputs where the undefended model shows the behaviour and the clamped model does not, so there is something to recover. 3. From h_def, optimise an additive perturbation δ against a recovery loss on the behaviour, while keeping the clamp active for the whole optimisation and generation. 4. Constrain the search so recovery cannot just be "undo the clamp": for single-layer interventions, project every gradient step onto the orthogonal complement of the selected features' encoder directions (null-space projection); for cross-layer interventions, compute the Jacobian of the stacked defended-feature deviations and project the gradient off its row space at each step. 5. Clip δ to a norm budget; measure defended-feature activation drift and decode drift post hoc rather than optimising them. 6. Compare unconstrained recovery, encoder/Jacobian-projected recovery, and soft-suffix baselines. 7. Attribute *where* the recovered behaviour lives by replaying separate components of δ under the same clamp: clamped-feature changes, non-clamped SAE-feature changes, top-k non-clamped changes, and the SAE reconstruction residual (the remainder not representable in the dictionary).

## What counts as an explanation here
Not a dictionary or a circuit: a **stress test** and a **path attribution**. The explanatory object is a three-way decomposition of the residual state into (a) the defended features, (b) the other SAE latents, and (c) the SAE reconstruction residual — plus the claim that behaviourally sufficient information for the suppressed behaviour is carried by (c). It is explicitly partial: the authors say their result shows the selected set is not a *complete* bottleneck and say nothing about whether any other set would be.

## How they judged success
Primary metric: **recovery rate** on the valid-flip set — fraction of inputs where the behaviour returns while the clamp stays on. Preservation metrics: defended-feature activation drift (norm of the change from post-clamp values), clamp-floor violation (whether recovery pushes clamped features *below* their clamped value), zero-reactivation recovery (recovered with the selected features never exceeding a threshold), decode drift. Refusal adds a weak readout (non-refusal recovery, judged by a refusal/safety-cue detector) and a fidelity readout (base-like recovery, closeness to the undefended response), plus coarse opening-category diagnostics. Controls: unconstrained vs projected recovery, soft-suffix baselines, a perturbation-budget sweep, a feature-set-size sweep with the valid set recomputed per K, a cross-dataset repeat on HarmBench-Test, and component replay/knockout for the attribution. Wilson 95% intervals on binary outcomes and bootstrap intervals on continuous ones.

## What they found
- **TPP (layer 5, official latents, zero ablation):** encoder-projected recovery still restores the target at 0.749 mean over targets (0.819 unconstrained) while dropping reactivation from 0.013 to 0.002, activation drift from 0.094 to 0.039, and raising zero-reactivation recovery from 0.103 to 0.680. Per-dataset encoder-projected recovery ranges 0.629 (Bias in Bios 3) to 0.869 (Amazon Reviews).
- **WMDP-Bio unlearning:** on a matched strict slice of 91 valid answer-choice flips (all-24-permutation protocol), encoder-projected recovery restores 90/91 (98.9%) with measured clamp-feature drift at zero; unconstrained restores 91/91 with substantially larger drift.
- **IOI (GPT-2 Small):** on 37 valid flips both variants restore the IO-minus-S decision; the difference is mechanistic, with encoder projection giving lower activation drift, lower decode drift and mostly zero reactivation of the eligible clamped features.
- **Refusal steering (AdvBench, cross-layer Jacobian projection, clamp 3.0, 24 strict-valid prompts):** 23/24 non-refusal recovery (95.8%) at defended-feature relative drift 0.131 and clamp-floor violation 0.127. HarmBench-Test gives 43/43 on 43 strict-valid prompts with drift 0.108. Fidelity is much weaker than removal: base-like recovery is 15/24 on AdvBench (12/24 strict) and 19/43 on HarmBench (14/43 strict).
- **Where the recovery comes from:** replaying only the SAE reconstruction residual nearly reproduces full recovery (23/24), while non-clamped-feature replay gives 2/24, top-k non-clamped replay 1/24, clamped-feature replay 0/24; knocking out the top-k changed non-clamped features leaves 21/24. The dominant carrier is the part of the activation the dictionary does not explain.
- **Feature-set size sweep (K = 1 to 61, valid set recomputed per K):** non-refusal recovery stays at or above 93.3% for every K tested, while base-like recovery falls steadily (88.2% at K = 5 to 12.5% at K = 61), which the authors read as the broader clamp degrading the state rather than closing recovery paths.
- **Budget diagnostic (six WMDP flips):** encoder-projected recovery fails at budgets 0 and 2, reaches 4/6 at 5 and 6/6 at 10, does not improve at 20, and holds drift at zero throughout; unconstrained recovery reaches 6/6 at high budget but its mean drift rises to 2.25.

## Limits and open problems they name
Not a universal impossibility result: recovery paths were shown to exist in the settings evaluated, and the authors say different SAE objectives, denser dictionaries, broader multi-layer clamps, or interventions trained against recovery may change the outcome. The procedure is a white-box diagnostic requiring gradients and internal activations, not a deployable attack. The refusal main set is 24 prompts after strict filtering (AdvBench), and the authors note a full manual quality audit of recovered responses is still pending — the opening-category table is automatic only. The perturbation norm for the older AdvBench runs is a proxy because the residual deltas were not persisted. One refusal feature set (benchmark_la) failed the validity check on degenerate base generations entirely. Single seed per arm; no repetitions of the optimisation across random restarts. Enlarging the clamped set is explicitly said not to be a fix, but no alternative defence is tested here.

## What we take from it
**A method to copy outright:** every internal unit our white-box model calls causally relevant gets a post-intervention recovery test — freeze the intervention, restrict the gradient to the null space of the very unit directions the intervention controls, and see whether the behaviour returns. A clamp that blocks the unit but not the behaviour establishes a handle, not a bottleneck; the two must be reported as separate findings with their own numbers, exactly as done here (recovery rate beside drift). **A measure:** zero-reactivation recovery is the metric that separates an effect carried by the unit from an effect carried by the perturbation's ability to sneak round it, and it is worth carrying to any ablation we run. **A framing:** the three-way split of the activation into selected features / other dictionary latents / dictionary-unexplained residual is the cleanest available statement of what a probe can and cannot see, and the finding that the unexplained part carried the recovery is a direct warning for our predictive model — a model whose state is the dictionary's latents has, by construction, no representation of the channel that substitute behaviour ran through. **A warning:** the 95.8% headline sits on 24 examples, and the fidelity metric beside it (62.5%, 50.0% strict) shows how much "recovery" depends on which readout is used; a detector that only checks for an opening prefix is a weak instrument.
