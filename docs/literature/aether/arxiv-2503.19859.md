## What they wanted, and why
The premise is that low rank is not something practitioners impose so much as something gradient descent produces, and that if the production mechanism can be stated precisely it should also be exploitable. The authors set out to give one mathematical account that covers four places the phenomenon appears — weight matrices, adapters, gradients, activations — and to organise the literature around two different claims about *when* the structure holds. The larger goal is a theory of why overparameterised networks generalise with fewer samples than parameters, together with the algorithmic payoff of compressing training and fine-tuning using structure that was going to appear anyway. It is written as a tutorial: background, then results, then the empirical figures that show the theory's regime and its edges.

## What they studied
Unit of analysis: **weight matrices and their singular value spectra**, plus three adjacent objects — adapter updates (LoRA-style), gradients, and layer activations (which they distinguish carefully from "representations", defined as patterns across units or layers built from activations). Empirical side spans a deep linear network, an MLP (MNIST), VGG-16 and ViT-B (CIFAR-10), and BERT-base-cased / T5-base fine-tuned on STS-B. Theory is confined almost entirely to **deep linear networks**; the nonlinear cases appear as figures and as remarks.

## How they did it
The structure of the argument is two-part, and the split is the paper's organising device. **Part one — structure at every iteration.** For a deep linear network minimising the squared error against a rank-r target with Frobenius (weight-decay) regularisation and ϵ-scaled orthogonal initialisation, they present a theorem decomposing each weight matrix's SVD into a 2r-dimensional block that moves plus a (d−2r)-dimensional block whose singular values are identical and decay geometrically toward zero. The proof idea is a nullspace construction: at init the gradient is a scaled copy of the weights minus a rank-r term, so there is a large subspace annihilated from both sides, and the corresponding singular-vector pairs stay put while their values shrink. That "trajectory is confined to a fixed subspace" fact is then exported to practice in three ways: LoRA (adapters are restricted low-rank by construction, with learning-rate and rank-allocation questions examined via LoRA+, AdaLoRA and Deep LoRA); GaLore (the gradient's own low-rank subspace is tracked with periodic SVD refreshes and used as a projection for the optimiser state); and ReLoRA, with an appendix theorem that the two are identical at every iteration once ReLoRA initialises its factors from the full-gradient SVD. **Part two — structure at convergence.** Here they argue a different mechanism: l2 regularisation on factorised weights is *equivalent*, via the variational form of Schatten-p quasi-norms, to a Schatten-2/L penalty on the product, so depth buys progressively tighter rank approximation. For max-margin settings they invoke existing results that gradient descent on positively homogeneous models converges in direction to a KKT point of a minimum-norm problem, which converts an implicitly-regularised training run into a nuclear-norm-regularised one. Dropout is then treated in the same frame: the expectation over Bernoulli masks can be written as the plain factorisation objective plus a per-unit penalty, an equivalence to nuclear-norm regularisation on the predictions. **Empirically** they make four kinds of plot on real networks: singular values of the penultimate weight update over training; singular values and per-component angles-to-init for a synthetic deep matrix factorisation and for BERT's 11th-layer value matrix under a 3-factor deep adapter; numerical ranks per layer under LoRA versus Deep LoRA on STS-B; and singular values / effective rank of activations at varying dropout rates on a deep linear net, an MLP and a ResNet.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **causal-mechanism account of structure**, stated as theorems about optimisation trajectories, plus **equivalence identities** at convergence — the paper's claim is not "here is a feature" but "the algorithm you are running is secretly solving this other, structured problem". Explanation is therefore predictive in a narrow sense: given (target rank, initialisation scale, depth, weight decay), Theorem 2 predicts which singular directions move and which are frozen and shrinking. The adapters-and-gradients half is explicitly *not* explanation but exploitation: it treats the structure as an engineering fact and asks what it costs to use. Coverage is claimed to be partial by construction — the authors state that a complete translation to nonlinear networks does not yet exist.

## How they judged success
For a survey: did the tutorial produce a coherent spine linking theory to the algorithmic literature, and does the evidence support the claimed scope? The measurable judgements on the empirical side are **singular-value spectra** (magnitude and evolution over iteration), **principal angles between current and initial singular vectors** (the direct test of subspace confinement), and **numerical/effective rank per layer**. Setup choices are printed: LoRA learning rates chosen from a four-value grid on 1024 STS-B samples (η=1e-4, α=8 for vanilla; η=1e-2 with γ=1e-2 for Deep LoRA), batch 16, Deep LoRA compressed from a full-width 3-layer orthogonal factorisation at ϵ=1e-3 using the smallest-gradient-singular-value subspace; dropout experiments on a 20-20-20 linear net and ReLU MLP with rank-15 synthetic targets, plus a 2-2-2-2 ResNet on CIFAR-10, SGD at 1e-2 with no momentum. GaLore's memory saving is quoted from that paper (up to 65.5% optimizer-state reduction) rather than re-measured. There is no baseline-comparison table of its own: the survey's evidence is plots, not scores.

## What they found
A clear, well-supported dissociation between two regimes. In the linear theory, only **2r singular values and their vectors evolve** while the other **d−2r** stay fixed and decay like ϵ(1−ηλ)^t — a precise statement of "the trajectory lives in a small subspace", and the theorem's price is visible in its assumptions (low-rank target, orthogonal init, squared loss, no biases). Empirically, the same signature appears in networks with nonlinearities: across DLN, MLP, VGG and ViT-B, only a small portion of the penultimate layer's update singular values move from initialisation while the rest stay small, and in ViT the effect is markedly stronger in **deeper** layers and in the value matrices (attention-query factorisation is the DeepSeek-V3 case they cite as industrial confirmation). Under Deep LoRA on STS-B, the adapters' converged spectra sit far below the requested r=8 and are **numerically zero in most layers**, which the authors read as implicit per-layer rank selection and credit for better test loss in the sample-starved regime — the interesting inverse result, since it says the useful rank is a property of the layer, not a hyperparameter to tune globally. For dropout the prediction lands: higher masking rates give lower numerical rank of activations, at both shallow and deep layers, in linear and ResNet settings, and rank falls gradually over training rather than arriving at the end. Also a clean negative-gear result worth keeping: because l2 on factors equals a Schatten 2/L penalty on the product, **depth itself sharpens the rank penalty** — the same weight decay applied through more layers shrinks more aggressively, which explains why depth and weight decay are not independent knobs.

## Limits and open problems they name
The theory-to-practice gap is large and they say so: results are for deep linear networks; the nonlinear analyses that exist (GaLore's justification) assume reversibility, particular losses and small batches; and algebraic conveniences used in the linear proofs — rotational symmetry, cross-layer alignment — are lost in the nonlinear case. How singular values and subspaces *pass through* standard nonlinearities is described as not well understood. For masked training, low rank in MAE/MLM features is empirical; the theory does not establish it. AdaLoRA is called heuristic with no mechanistic account. And they flag interpretability only as an aspiration — that low rank might yield simpler explanations of features and transformations is named as an open direction, not a result.

## What we take from it
- **A framing to steal:** "structure at every iteration" versus "structure at convergence" is a clean split we should reuse for any claim about what a network's weights *are*, because the two demand different evidence — dynamics (subspace angles over training) versus solutions (the equivalent regularised objective). Most interpretability claims we read pick one and quietly imply the other.
- **A measure:** the **principal angle between a subspace at time t and at initialisation**, printed next to singular-value magnitudes. It tests confinement directly, where a spectrum alone cannot distinguish "these directions are the same ones" from "these directions happen to have the same size". This is the right diagnostic for any adapters/LoRA-side reading in our white-box programme.
- **A design lesson with teeth:** the Deep LoRA result — adapters converge to rank far below the budget, zero in most layers — means the effective rank of an adaptation is an **output**, not an input. Any of our analyses that conditions on a chosen rank is conditioning on a quantity the training already decided.
- **A warning about hyperparameter confounds:** depth is not a free architectural choice under weight decay, because l2 on factors is a Schatten 2/L penalty on the product. Comparisons across depth in a regularised setting are comparisons of regularisation strength too.
- **A pointer, since they name it and stop:** the interpretability direction (low rank as raw material for understandable features, and its relation to neural collapse) is explicitly left open — which is where our work sits, and it means the theory we can borrow stops short of the thing we want to predict.
