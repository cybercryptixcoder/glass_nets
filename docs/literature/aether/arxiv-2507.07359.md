## What they wanted, and why

Causal models are learned from data, and observational data is known to leave the graph undetermined inside a Markov equivalence class, so somebody has to intervene. Interventions are expensive, so the design loop has been studied for decades under the expected-information-gain banner. The authors argue that almost all of that work optimises the *wrong* quantity: it designs experiments to improve the whole model (the graph, the mechanisms, or both), while the scientist usually wants one specific effect. Their motivating picture is the drug-target case, where mapping the entire biological network is far less valuable than pinning down how a handful of molecules move a disease pathway, so effort spent tightening the account of irrelevant parts of the system is wasted budget. The paper's stated aim is therefore a design criterion that is both *goal-oriented* (information about the queried quantity, not about everything) and *non-myopic* (chosen by planning over a whole sequence of experiments rather than one step at a time), and an implementation that can decide in real time rather than re-solving an optimisation at every stage.

## What they studied

The object is a structural causal model over a small number of variables: graphs with ten variables in the causal-reasoning runs, thirty in the synthetic discovery runs, twenty in the discovery runs on gene networks, and a fifty-node scalability check; mechanisms are either linear with additive Gaussian noise or a small neural network per child. The unit of analysis is the *experimental design*, which the paper defines as a pair — the set of variables to intervene on and the value or distribution of values to set them to — so both "where" and "how much" are searched over. The target of the whole exercise is a query function that maps a causal model to a quantity of interest; two instantiations are studied, the graph itself (causal discovery) and the distribution of a set of variables after an intervention (causal reasoning). Priors over graphs are Erdős–Rényi and scale-free random graphs, with realistically-derived gene regulatory network topologies from the DREAM benchmark subnets for the semi-synthetic runs.

## How they did it

The formalism is Bayesian sequential design written as a Markov decision process. Belief over the model is updated by Bayes' rule after each experiment, and the objective is the expected information gain on the query *after a whole sequence* of T experiments, where each design is a function of the history so far — this is what makes it non-myopic, and they show the sequence total equals the sum of stage-wise gains in expectation, so the two formulations are reconciled in an appendix. Direct computation of that gain is intractable, so they bound it below with a variational posterior over the query conditioned on an embedding of the full intervention history, then maximise the *bound* jointly over three things: the variational posterior's parameters, the history-embedding parameters, and a policy network that emits the next design. Because all three are optimised together against the same objective, the policy and the posterior co-adapt — the authors address the obvious worry (the policy might prefer histories that are easy to fit rather than informative) by checking the controlled fixed-graph instance where an independent nested-Monte-Carlo estimator is available. Training is offline and amortised: each iteration samples a model from the prior, samples the query it induces, rolls out a full T-stage trajectory with the current policy and takes a gradient step. The policy is a small transformer that alternates attention across the variable axis and the history axis, is permutation-invariant over history samples and permutation-equivariant over variables, and outputs the intervention target through a discrete sampling relaxation plus a separate value head. The query posterior is a normalising flow for effect queries (multimodal, since different graphs imply different effects) and independent per-edge Bernoulli probabilities from a shared network for graph queries. At deployment only forward passes are needed, so no online Bayesian inference is performed when the next probe is chosen.

## What counts as an explanation here

Not an account of any network — this is a method entry, and the "understanding" it produces is a belief distribution over causal models plus a conditional distribution over the *queried quantity*, both parametric approximations carried in a network, with the EIG they report being a bound on the true information rather than an estimate with a confidence interval. The claim is deliberately partial by design: the whole thesis is that the posterior should be sharp on the query and need not be sharp elsewhere, so the object they certify is the query posterior, not the model posterior. The policy is a reusable programme in the weak sense that it is a function from history to design, amortised over a prior over environments, with no learned symbols and no textual statement of what it does — the interesting behaviour ("concentrate the probes on the collider's parents" in a neighbouring paper) would have to be read off the policy's outputs.

## How they judged success

Two families of measure. The first is the prior-omitted EIG lower bound itself, evaluated per policy — a self-referential measure in the sense that each method's number is computed with that method's own posterior, which the authors say explicitly and defend by training a dedicated posterior for every baseline so that only the design policy differs. The second is downstream quality: the Wasserstein distance between the ground-truth interventional distribution of the query and the policy-specific posterior for causal-reasoning tasks, and expected structural Hamming distance, F1 and AUPRC against the ground-truth graph for discovery, with AUROC added in the appendix. Baselines for discovery are a reinforcement-learning sequential design method with a fixed pretrained posterior, a single-step Bayesian-optimisation design, and a non-adaptive gradient-optimised design, plus a uniformly random designer; for the fixed-graph motivating example the baseline is the nested-Monte-Carlo estimator of the same quantity. Random seeds range from four (reasoning) to ten (discovery), with one standard-error bands, and appendix runs repeat the comparison using each baseline's original inference procedure.

## What they found

Three results matter. First, the goal-orientation claim is demonstrated in the cleanest form available on the fixed-graph example: the policy that maximises information about the model's parameters achieves *higher* parameter information yet answers the actual effect query markedly worse than the policy that targets the query, and the same ordering shows up again when the graph is unfixed, where the discovery-optimised policy underperforms on effect queries particularly with nonlinear mechanisms. Second, the non-myopic claim shows up as a gap that widens with the number of stages: on synthetic discovery the advantage over all four baselines grows as stages accumulate rather than appearing immediately, and on the semi-synthetic gene networks the effect-query policy is at parity early and separates after roughly the third stage. Third, the random designer is not uniformly weak — in one appendix query the gap collapses because the intervention node happens to be informative for both discovery and the query, and random selection stays competitive there, which the authors attribute to the variational posterior being adequate even under random interventions; in the fifty-node table, random comes within a few points of the adaptive policies on the denser graphs and actually beats one baseline on AUPRC. The trained policy costs GPU-hours offline (single-digit at ten variables, several tens at thirty) and a sub-second forward pass per decision.

## Limits and open problems they name

The design is amortised against a prior over graphs and mechanisms, so a system that is implausible under that prior is outside the guarantee; the authors report graceful degradation under a noise-distribution shift and one prior-misspecification experiment, and call large mismatch a limitation rather than a result. All evaluation is synthetic or semi-synthetic, and moving to real interventional screens is named as the next step. The posterior over graphs factorises over edges (a single shared network attends over the history, but the joint over edges is not modelled), which they flag as future work at higher cost. Only single-target, differentiable interventions are supported, causal sufficiency is assumed throughout, and robustness to a changing experiment horizon is named. The bound-tightness worry that follows from training policy and estimator together is acknowledged rather than eliminated: they argue the objective cannot *overstate* information and show the estimator tracks the true landscape on one controlled instance, but do not give a general guarantee.

## What we take from it

A framing and a warning. The framing: *declare the quantity of interest before designing the battery, and score the battery on that quantity alone*. Their motivating figure is the argument in one picture — an agent that optimises for global fidelity of its account picks different probes, and measurably worse ones, than an agent that optimises for the one effect it was asked about. For a white-box account of a network this is the difference between "our probe set shrinks uncertainty about the model" and "our probe set shrinks uncertainty about the behaviour we were asked to predict", and only the second is what a predictive account is for. The warning: the measure is self-referential. Every method's reported information number is computed with its own learned posterior, so a table of EIG values across methods is not a like-for-like comparison unless the posterior class is held fixed, which these authors do explicitly and say out loud. Two smaller things are worth borrowing: the sequential formulation is *reported as a sequence* (curves against stage index, with the advantage widening late), and the design variable is the pair of target and value distribution, which is a cleaner statement of the strength policy than a separate strength sweep.
