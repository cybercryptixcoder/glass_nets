# Distance-based and RKHS-based Dependence Metrics in High Dimension

## What they wanted, and why

Distance covariance and the Hilbert-Schmidt independence criterion are the standard instruments for saying "these two things are dependent, and I make no assumption about how". Their headline property is that the population value is zero exactly under independence, which is what lets a practitioner use them as an omnibus test rather than a correlation check. The question here is what survives of that property when the two vectors are high dimensional — p and q in the hundreds or thousands, the regime our activation read-outs live in. The authors answer with a negative result of unusual sharpness: in that regime the sample distance covariance is, to leading order, a constant times the sum of squared *linear* cross-covariances between coordinates, so the omnibus guarantee is asymptotic in n but empty in p. The paper is therefore less a new measure than a demolition of the reason people reach for the old one, followed by the cheapest available repair.

## What they studied

Two random vectors X in R^p and Y in R^q, with sample size n. The unit of analysis is the *coordinate pair* (x_i, y_j) and, at the level of the whole vector, the aggregate over all p*q coordinate pairs. Two asymptotic regimes are treated separately: high dimension low sample size (HDLSS), where n is held fixed and min(p,q) grows, and high dimension medium sample size (HDMSS), where n, p and q all grow with p and q faster. The structures studied on the vectors are an m-dependence model (coordinates further than m apart independent) and an autoregressive covariance with geometric decay, on the outcome side. Four statistics are compared throughout: dCov, its marginal aggregation mdCov, hCov (with Gaussian and Laplacian kernels, bandwidth set to the median pairwise distance), and its marginal aggregation mhCov. Nothing is trained; every object is an estimator or a test.

## How they did it

The argument is a **decomposition**, run twice, once per regime. In HDLSS, a first-order Taylor-type expansion of the Euclidean pairwise distance |X_s - X_t|^2 around its expectation splits each pairwise distance into a constant plus a linear functional of the coordinates plus a remainder, with the remainder bounded by the size of the centred L2 term. Substituting that pair of expansions into the unbiased, U-centred sample distance covariance makes every quartic term factor into a product of one X-coordinate difference and one Y-coordinate difference, and the sum over the four indices collapses to a fourth-order U-statistic in a *single* coordinate pair. Theorem 2.1.1 states the result: dCov equals a constant times the sum over all p*q coordinate pairs of the unbiased squared sample covariance, plus a remainder proved negligible under a variance-boundedness assumption and a rate condition on the centred distance terms. The same expansion runs through a differentiable kernel to give the identical statement for hCov, with the kernel's derivative at the origin entering the constant. In HDMSS the decomposition is reproved, and the limit theory is redone by a martingale construction rather than the HDLSS element-wise argument, since n now grows and Noetherian pairwise-independence bookkeeping stops working. For the testing side, a single unified quantity is defined from an arbitrary bivariate kernel, and each of dCov, hCov and mdCov is shown to equal that quantity times a known factor at its own kernel — which is what lets one limit theorem serve all four statistics. Under HDLSS the unified quantity's studentised form has a Student-t limit on v-1 degrees of freedom, matching the earlier result of Székely and Rizzo, and under an alternative it is a non-central-t mixture whose non-centrality is a scalar they call phi. Power is then a deterministic function of phi, tabulated across the null, a general alternative, a local alternative whose non-centrality decays like one over root v, and a "sparse" alternative class where coordinates are dependent with zero covariance. The marginal construction is motivated by contrast: an aggregate of coordinates disappears into a constant because it sums coordinate covariances, whereas aggregating *componentwise p-values-like statistics* — the sum of p*q two-coordinate distance covariances — never averages away a coordinate's nonlinearity. Simulations use four example families, 200 permutations for the permutation tests and 5000 Monte Carlo repetitions for empirical rejection rates, at n of 10, 30 and 60 and p of 5 and 30, plus two kernel choices.

## What counts as an explanation here

A limit theorem plus an approximation identity: the explanation is *why* the method fails, expressed as an equality that shows the omnibus statistic's leading term is a linear-dependence functional. It is complete for the claim it makes (the remainder is proved to vanish at a stated rate) and it predicts rather than describes — the paper predicts that dCov-based power must decay to the nominal level as p grows under a dependence with zero coordinate covariance, and then measures that decay. There is no surrogate model and no localisation of the dependence; the repair is a different aggregate, not a map of where the dependence lives.

## How they judged success

Three things, kept on separate tables. **Size**: empirical rejection rates at nominal 0.010, 0.050, 0.100 across six test statistics, three example laws, n of 10/30/60 and p of 5/30, with the tabulated t critical value used throughout; density plots of the studentised statistics against the matching t density are shown as the visual check. **Power under a non-omnibus-detectable alternative** (a class where coordinate covariance is positive): every statistic against every other, same grid, to show the price paid for marginalisation. **Power under the sparse alternative** where coordinate covariances are zero but coordinate pairs are dependent — squared coordinates, log-absolute values, cubes, and the sine/cosine circle relation — which is the case the theory says the joint test cannot see. Permutation tests and studentised t-tests are run side by side so the closed-form null is checked against the resampling null on the same cells. The theoretical comparison is anchored by a proposition giving an explicit two-sided bound between the marginal and joint non-centrality parameters for Gaussian laws, so the predicted power gap can be read off a number rather than argued.

## What they found

The decomposition holds in both regimes and the remainder is negligible at the stated rates, so under HDLSS and HDMSS the joint distance/Hilbert-Schmidt covariance measures only coordinate-wise linear dependence. A corollary the authors did not advertise: the squared sample distance correlation and the RV coefficient, an older and much cheaper association measure, are asymptotically equal in this regime and therefore share the same t limit — the expensive statistic buys nothing the cheap one lacks. On size, all tests are close to nominal even at p = 5, and the empirical densities approach the t density as p grows. On the Gaussian alternative where marginalisation *should* cost power, Theorem 2.1.1's consequence is a bound of 0.89^2 on the ratio of the two non-centralities, and the tables bear this out with moderate rather than catastrophic losses — at n = 10, p = 30, alpha = 0.05, dCov permutation power 0.936 against mdCov 0.849, hCov 0.935 against mhCov 0.648 (Gaussian kernel). On the sparse alternative the ordering reverses completely: at n = 10, p = 30, alpha = 0.05, with Y = X squared coordinate-wise, dCov reads 0.101 while mdCov reads 0.526, hCov 0.098 against mhCov 0.769, and at the same settings for the sine/cosine circle relation dCov reads 0.066 against mdCov 0.434. The joint statistics are essentially at nominal level there, and their power does not improve with n as fast as the marginal ones because the *dimension*, not the sample size, is what destroys them. The marginal sizes stay accurate because the marginal construction is exactly the unified quantity at absolute distance, for which the same t limit applies. Two secondary findings: the Laplacian kernel loses less power than the Gaussian under the sparse alternative, and none of dCov, Gaussian-hCov and Laplacian-hCov dominates the others across all settings, so the paper calls all three admissible.

## Limits and open problems they name

The convergence requires the centred pairwise-distance terms to be small relative to their expectation, which is a concentration condition on the coordinate variances, not a mild moment condition — the m-dependence model satisfies it only under explicit rate conditions linking n, m, p and q, printed as a set of order relations. The marginal Hilbert-Schmidt t-test is only analysed with *fixed* bandwidths; the practical default of a median-distance bandwidth that depends on the whole sample breaks the pairwise-independence bookkeeping, and the authors say so rather than papering over it. The choose-your-norm question is left open: the marginal aggregate is equivalent to a different kernel, and which norm or kernel is optimal for high-dimensional independence testing is future work. Finally, and most importantly for us, they conjecture that when the sample size is fixed no omnibus test exists at all, and that even with n growing faster than the dimension they see no path to an asymptotically omnibus test — which is a statement that the failure is informational, not an artefact of the estimator.

## What we take from it

A **warning and a measure**, both directly load-bearing for our probing. The warning: any high-dimensional dependence number computed by treating the whole vector jointly — HSIC on a full residual stream, distance correlation between two layers' activations — is, to leading order, measuring only linear coordinate-wise association, and at p in the thousands that leading term is the entire statistic. The recommendation that follows is not to abandon these instruments but to run them **marginally**: score per coordinate pair, or more usefully per read-out direction against the label, and aggregate the scores, because the aggregate of scores preserves each coordinate's nonlinearity while the aggregate of coordinates destroys it. The measure worth copying is the phi-parameterisation: reduce every test to one scalar non-centrality and report power as a function of it, so the comparison between instruments is a comparison of phi rather than of twelve-cell tables. The third item is a control we should adopt outright — the sparse alternative class, dependence with zero coordinate covariance, is the exact null-adjacent case a joint screen will silently pass, so it belongs in our battery as a mandatory row, not as a stress test. And the RV-coefficient corollary is a **shortcut**: in high dimension, the cheap association coefficient is a consistent stand-in for the expensive one, so any place we compute distance correlation at p large we can compute a trace of a p-by-p covariance product instead. Coreference `arxiv-1504.00964`, `arxiv-1201.3522` and `arxiv-1605.08732`, the low-dimensional t* family whose whole appeal was an omnibus property this paper says does not survive high dimension.
