# Causal Distillation for Language Models

- **Key:** arxiv-2112.02505
- **Authors / organisation:** Zhengxuan Wu, Atticus Geiger, Joshua Rozner, Elisa Kreiss, Hanson Lu, Thomas Icard, Christopher Potts, Noah D. Goodman — Stanford University. Wu and Geiger marked as equal contribution; Icard, Potts and Goodman marked as corresponding.
- **Date:** 2021-12-05 (v1); this version v2, 2022-06-03
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2112.02505
- **Kind:** paper (NAACL 2022, from the Journal reference line; 7 pages, 2 figures)
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

Distillation is the standard way to make a large model cheap, and the standard recipe pulls the student toward the teacher on two fronts at once: it must reproduce the teacher's *behaviour* (task loss, and a softened cross-entropy toward the teacher's output distribution) and it must reproduce the teacher's *last-layer hidden states* (a cosine loss on contextualised representations). The authors' objection is precise: the second term asks the student to match the teacher's internal states without regard to whether those states do anything in the teacher's computation — a direction that carries no causal role gets matched as hard as one that carries the whole decision. So they add a third term that supervises the student on what happens when the teacher's computation is *changed*, not on what its states look like. The larger goal is the one this folder keeps meeting from the analysis side: making a small model a faithful simplification of a big one, in the technical sense the causal-abstraction literature gives to "faithful", rather than merely an accurate one.

## What they studied

BERT-Base as the teacher (12 layers, 12 heads, hidden size 768) and two students with the same block shape at 6 layers and at 3 layers. Distillation data is WikiText-103M, roughly a 0.1B-token budget, deliberately far below the 3.3B tokens behind published BERT and DistilBERT, with a low-resource condition at 15% of even that. The unit of analysis is the **layer-and-token grid of hidden representations**: for the masked language modelling setting they set up, the student's representation at one row-and-column position is aligned to a *set* of teacher representations at the same token column, and the object being compared is the two models' output logits over the vocabulary under a matched intervention. Downstream, the same distilled checkpoints are fine-tuned and scored on GLUE, CoNLL-2003 and SQuAD v1.1.

## How they did it

Interchange intervention training, wired directly into distillation. The teacher is frozen and left in evaluation mode; the student trains. At each iteration they draw a pair of examples, read the teacher's values at the teacher neurons matched to a chosen student site, and — under no gradient — build an intervened teacher in which those values are overwritten with the ones computed on the *other* example, then record the teacher's output logits under that counterfactual. They build the same intervened student, with gradients flowing through the overwritten values, and take the smoothed cross-entropy between the student's counterfactual logits and the teacher's. That scalar is added to the three standard DistilBERT objectives with equal weight. Three alignment functions are compared: FULL, where every student row maps to the block of four teacher rows at the same depth band; MIDDLE, where only the student's middle row maps to the teacher's middle row; and LATE, where the student's first two rows map to the teacher's second-to-last and last rows. Alignment is a partial function on the student side, and at each iteration one aligned student layer is picked at random for the intervention, with 30% of token positions selected per sequence. Three token-selection variants under FULL are compared — consecutive tokens, random tokens, masked tokens only — plus a fourth objective that applies the last-layer cosine alignment itself under intervention. The student is initialised from every fourth teacher layer as DistilBERT does; training is three epochs at effective batch 240, approximately nine hours on four A100s; each distilled model is trained with three seeds and each fine-tune with five, giving three runs for language modelling and fifteen for everything else.

## What counts as an explanation here

Not an explanation of the teacher and not a description of it — a **training objective derived from a causal-abstraction claim**, and the claim is the deliverable. The paper asserts that the distilled student *is a causal abstraction of* the teacher for the aligned site: same intervention, matching response. That is a stronger and narrower assertion than behavioural imitation, and it is the only entry in this folder where the causal-abstraction relation is a *target of optimisation* rather than an object of measurement. It is partial in a specific way: the alignment is hand-specified (one of three fixed shapes), only one site is intervened on per step, and the abstraction is claimed at the level of the output distribution rather than at the level of an intermediate variable the paper can name. The prediction the account licenses is about held-out interventions on the aligned site — which, as it happens, the paper does not score.

## How they judged success

Purely downstream. WikiText development perplexity for masked language modelling; the averaged GLUE development score, with per-task numbers broken out in an appendix table; CoNLL-2003 accuracy and macro-F1; SQuAD v1.1 exact match and macro-F1. The baselines are the two useful ones: DistilBERT trained on the identical data budget and with identical compute (the real comparison), and the published BERT-Base and DistilBERT numbers imputed from public Hugging Face checkpoints (a reference point, not a controlled comparison, and flagged as imputed in the table itself). Variance is handled by the multi-seed aggregation — standard deviations are reported on every cell and are what makes the small deltas readable. Controls are the two token-selection variants (random tokens, masked tokens) which keep the same loss and the same alignment while removing the meaningful choice of which tokens to intervene on, plus a low-resource run and a layer-count ablation.

## What they found

Every alignment beats matched-budget DistilBERT on every measured downstream task, and the gain tracks the richness of the alignment rather than the amount of extra machinery. At 6 layers the best language-modelling result is the FULL alignment plus the intervened cosine term, which the authors put at 2.24 perplexity below matched-budget DistilBERT; the FULL alignment alone is also below it, and MIDDLE and LATE sit in between and close together. The pattern is the same on GLUE — highest average at FULL plus the intervened cosine term, with the paper's own summary figure of +1.77 points over matched-budget DistilBERT — and the per-task table shows the largest single-task gaps on the tasks the small student was previously weakest at. On CoNLL-2003 the best model is MIDDLE rather than FULL, and it edges past both matched-budget DistilBERT and, narrowly, the BERT-Base teacher; on SQuAD v1.1 the best is plain FULL. So the win is broad but the *best* alignment is not stable across tasks, which the authors report without comment. The 3-layer students gain more in relative terms than the 6-layer ones (Figure 3's per-size comparison), and the low-resource run at 15% of WikiText still improves over the matched baseline — the result the paper leans on for its closing claim that DITTO reaches parity with standard DistilBERT while using a small fraction of the training data. The two token-selection controls both fall slightly behind consecutive tokens but above plain DistilBERT, so the objective appears to do work beyond simply forcing more gradient through the intervenable sites.

## Limits and open problems they name

They decline to explore larger distillation corpora and state that only the constrained-budget setting was tested, so the method's behaviour when data is plentiful is unknown. The setting is masked language modelling on BERT-family encoders only. Recorded here rather than by the authors: (a) the causal claim is never scored as a causal claim — there is no held-out interchange-intervention test comparing student and teacher response *after* training, so a lower perplexity is consistent with the student having learned the teacher's counterfactual structure and equally consistent with the extra gradient simply acting as a stronger regulariser; (b) the alignment is hand-chosen from three shapes and the paper's own MIDDLE-versus-FULL reversal on NER is unexplained, which matters because the alignment is the entire content of the abstraction claim; (c) the two token-selection controls are weaker controls than they look, since random and masked selection still perform a genuine intervention, just on a nuisance-selected site, so they test the choice of *where* rather than whether the intervention matters at all; (d) every comparison is at the same tiny 0.1B-token budget, which is the condition that most flatters a regularising extra loss, and the paper's headline parity claim is in exactly that condition; (e) no ablation removes the interchange term while keeping the number of intervened forward passes, so the gain cannot be separated from the compute the term adds.

## What we take from it

A **method** with a **warning** attached. The method is the cleanest thing in the folder for our purpose: an interchange intervention can be differentiated *through the overwritten values* if the site is written in place, which means a causal-abstraction claim can be made a loss term rather than only a hypothesis to be tested afterwards. For our own work the useful version is the inverse direction — this paper optimises a student toward the teacher's counterfactual behaviour; we want to *measure* how much of that behaviour was transferred, and the natural instrument is the one this paper omits: hold out a set of interventions during fit, then score student and teacher on those at the end. That single missing number is the difference between "our student is a causal abstraction" as a claim and as a measurement, and it is cheap relative to the four-A100 training it would sit on top of.

The warning is the ablation that isn't there. Every gain in this paper is reported against a matched-budget baseline that performs fewer intervened forward passes, so the interchange term's contribution is confounded with the additional optimisation signal and with the compute it costs. Our batteries should carry the arm this paper is missing: same extra compute, same number of intervened passes, interventions executed on a pre-declared irrelevant site — the authors' random-token condition is the nearest thing and it still beats the baseline, which is exactly why the honestly-irrelevant-site arm is worth running. Second, the alignment instability across tasks is a caution for any account we publish as "the" abstraction of a model: three hand-specified alignment shapes produce three different rankings depending on the downstream task, so an alignment is a modelling choice with consequences, and the choice should be reported as a sweep rather than a single number.

A third, smaller takeaway: the paper's structure is a reusable template for how to write up a causal-abstraction method — the algorithm box spells out exactly which parameters are frozen, which gradient flows where, and what the counterfactual is in plain-language terms, and the worked masked-language-modelling example in the first figure makes the intervention concrete in a single sentence pair. That level of operational specificity is what the folder's other causal-abstraction entries mostly lack.
