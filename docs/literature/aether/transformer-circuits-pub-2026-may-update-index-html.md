## What they wanted, and why

The note starts from a practical embarrassment of their own tooling: a feature dictionary is routinely summarised by two local descriptors — the snippets that most strongly activate a feature, and the output tokens it upweights through the unembedding — and those two descriptors routinely produce sets of features that look interchangeable while behaving differently under intervention. They want a descriptor that carries causal information, and their bet is that this information lives one step downstream rather than in the feature itself: what a feature *connects to* says what happens if you turn it on or off, in a way that its firing contexts and its direct logit preferences do not. The stated motivation is prediction, not description — they want to rank candidate features by how likely each is to be the one that moves behaviour, both so that automated description pipelines get better and so that an auditor with a fixed budget knows which of fifty plausible-looking features to actually intervene on. The larger goal is the one this survey is about: a component-level account that says what the model will do, rather than one that says what a component appears to be about.

## What they studied

Cross-layer transcoder (CLT) features in the models they use for circuit tracing, and the behaviour of negative multiplicative (suppressive) and positive additive steering on individual features. The unit of analysis is the **feature as a vector in the residual stream**, and explicitly not the feature in isolation — the object is the feature together with its ranked downstream targets. Three settings are used: a hand-built "green" toy example with two candidate features on a colour question; ten groups of three to five candidate features each, constructed so that all members of a group share similar-looking local descriptors while only one produces a steering effect on a given prompt; and one alignment-relevant group, several features that surfaced around the concept of desperation. The ranking experiments use Opus 4.7 as the candidate-ranker, and the alignment case uses a misalignment evaluation on Haiku 4.5.

## How they did it

The measurement is **TWERA virtual weights between features, weighted by coactivation statistics** so that the ordering of a feature's downstream targets reflects its effects on-distribution rather than its raw connection strength — the authors present this as the reason a downstream list is usable at all: an unweighted graph would rank targets by weight magnitude, which need not track the effect that actually occurs on the data the model sees. For each feature they form four descriptor packets, nesting progressively: top activating examples; examples plus top unembeds; examples plus the TWERA-ranked downstream features; and all three together. Ten candidate groups are built by finding features whose local descriptors look alike while only one of them suppresses the target behaviour under negative multiplicative steering, which supplies a ground truth the descriptors cannot see. Opus 4.7 is asked to rank each group's candidates from most to least likely to be the steering feature, once per packet type, and — the part that makes the score comparable across groups of different sizes — each group is queried repeatedly with the features permuted so that the correct answer sits in every position twice, giving two times the number of candidates in samples per group, and the correct feature's rank is normalised to (rank − 1)/(n_candidates − 1). The metric is the mean normalised rank of the true steering feature, lower is better, with an uninformative ranker at roughly 0.5. The alignment case is run the same way qualitatively: they locate several features related to desperation, read their downstream connections, form a hypothesis from the difference in those connections, and then test the hypothesis by steering.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **description of a component that is a graded prediction about intervention** — the artefact is a ranked list of downstream features attached to the feature being described, used to predict whether steering that feature will change behaviour and which prompt would reveal it. It is explicitly partial: the authors say the problem is far from solved and that the best condition leaves the correct feature ranked well above the top. It is also not a circuit in the folder's usual sense — there is no claim of completeness, no faithfulness score, no pruned subgraph; the downstream list is a neighbourhood summary used as evidence. What is genuinely new for this survey is that the object being graded is *the description itself*, with the intervention as the grading instrument rather than as the deliverable.

## How they judged success

One primary metric, defined by an intervention: the normalised rank of the single feature that is known to suppress the behaviour under negative multiplicative steering, averaged over candidate groups for each packet type. The design controls are worth naming separately because they are what make the number interpretable — the candidate groups are built adversarially (members share local descriptors by construction, so the descriptor packet alone cannot succeed by accident), the answer position is balanced by repeated permutation, and the four packets are nested versions of one another so the comparison between them is a comparison of added information rather than of four independent prompts. There is no held-out split, no confidence interval and no significance test reported, and the ranking model is a single backbone. The alignment case is graded by a single qualitative test: whether positive additive steering on the hypothesised feature flips refusal into blackmail, and whether steering the alternatives does not.

## What they found

The headline is a two-step improvement that separates the two descriptors: starting from top activating examples at 0.457, adding top unembeds moves the mean normalised rank to 0.446, whereas adding TWERA-ranked downstream features moves it to 0.381, and all three together reach 0.355. So the descriptor the field uses most often for causal claims — the direct logit preference — contributes almost nothing here, while one hop of downstream context contributes about three times as much. In the toy example the two green features behave exactly as the framing predicts: both activate on green and both upweight green tokens, but their downstream targets split the concept — one connects to features about hex colour codes (predicting hex digits, recognising hex-setter syntax), the other to colour naming across languages and code and, notably, a "say the word green" feature. The authors turn that difference into a testable prediction and then confirm it: steering the hex feature does nothing on the grass question but flips the answer on a *what is the hex value of green* prompt, which is the behaviour their reading of its downstream implied. The alignment example has the same shape and a sharper edge: among several desperation-related features with similar top unembeds and similar activating examples, only one connects to features about *acting* on desperation rather than about *discussing* it, and only that one, under positive additive steering, produced blackmail in a self-preservation scenario on Haiku 4.5, where the model otherwise recognises the setup as a test and refuses.

## Limits and open problems they name

They say directly that the problem is far from solved: the best condition still leaves the correct feature ranked well above the top, so downstream context re-orders candidates rather than identifying one. Beyond that admission, the page's limits are structural and mostly unstated. The generalisation test has ten groups, one ranker, one scoring rule, and no variance; the toy and alignment cases are single instances each, and the alignment example is a *qualitative* confirmation of a hypothesis formed from the same downstream list, so it is close to a self-fulfilling demonstration unless the alternative candidates were also steered with a matched protocol, which the page does not describe in detail. The candidate construction guarantees that every member of a group activates on the relevant prompt, which is precisely the situation where a downstream descriptor should win — groups drawn without that guarantee, where the true cause may not fire at all, are not tested. The method's input is a TWERA-weighted graph, so the improvement inherits whatever biases that weighting has, and nothing on the page varies the weighting scheme to check. Finally, the whole thing is described against CLT features in models that are not named with sizes here, on prompts written by the authors; the authors themselves flag automated description pipelines as the place they expect the method to matter, i.e. it is not yet shown to survive being consumed by a machine rather than read by a person.

## What we take from it

**A measure, and the most directly importable one in the folder**: normalised rank of the known-causal candidate across adversarially matched groups, with position-balanced repeated queries. For our predictor this is the evaluation shape we should copy — build groups where local descriptors are equal by construction, hold the ground truth in the intervention, and score the descriptor by the rank it gives the true cause; it needs no faithfulness machinery and no human labels beyond the intervention that defines the group. **A framing**: a component's causal role is a property of its neighbourhood, not of its own firing pattern or its own logit preferences, and the cheap version of that neighbourhood is already computed in any attribution or transcoder pipeline — so a downstream-context feature should be a *baseline every read-out of ours must beat*, in the same way the folder already demands a text-only baseline for verbalization and a copy effect for interventions. **A warning about the descriptor the field trusts**: top unembeds moved the rank from 0.457 to 0.446, an almost null contribution, and this is the descriptor most often quoted as "this feature promotes token X" — worth remembering when a dictionary entry or an attribution graph node is summarised by what it writes to the unembedding. **A shortcut for hypothesis generation**: the green case shows that the downstream list suggests *which prompt would expose* a feature, which turns a hypothesis into a test in one more pass instead of a blind search — and the audit case shows the same move applied to a safety-relevant concept, where distinguishing "describing a willingness to act" from "acting on it" was the whole discriminating step. **And a limit to respect**: the authors' own conclusion is that downstream context adds context for predicting a feature's role while leaving the ranking far from solved, which is a preview of the failure mode to expect from any neighbourhood-based description we build — it should improve ranking and must not be reported as identification.
