## What they wanted, and why

The folder has two competing answers to "what is the basic unit of a representation" — the neuron and the learned dictionary feature — and the choice between them has been made on taste and on downstream convenience rather than on criteria one could check. The authors want the criteria first: define what a *fundamental representational unit* would have to be, in a way that does not depend on the architecture, and then ask which candidate units conform to the definition. Their larger target is not interpretability per se but the relationship between a network's internal organisation and human conceptual structure — whether features are the right rung, or whether human concepts such as HowNet sememes sit *above* the rung the dictionary finds. The motivating observation is the usual one: neurons keep the information and interleave it, features separate it and lose some of it, and no framework has said what "separating it" is supposed to mean precisely enough to measure.

## What they studied

Language models (Gemma-2-2B, Gemma-2-9B, Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen-3.5-9B, the hybrid Olmo-Hybrid-7B) and diffusion models (Stable Diffusion v1.5, FLUX.2-klein-9B), across three representation corpora — Wikipedia text, Wikidata entities, LAION-COCO image-generation states — chosen so that discrete entities and continuous text are both covered. The unit of analysis is a **dictionary with unit-norm atoms** together with its coefficient vector: neurons are one such dictionary (the columns of the MLP `down_proj` matrix, which are exactly the residual-stream directions the MLP adds), and learned features are another (Gemma Scope and Llama Scope decoder rows, or the rows of a freshly trained SAE). Semantic alignment is measured against OpenHowNet, a hand-annotated lexicon in which each concept is described by fine-grained primitives ("sememes"), restricted to 2,135 sememes that carry at least two English entity entries.

## How they did it

Nothing is trained to interpret anything; the whole paper is a measurement instrument plus a proof that one family of encoders recovers its object. The instrument has two parts. **Fidelity** is the R² of the representation reconstructed from the dictionary, i.e. the fraction of variance the atoms account for. **Stability** is a quantile requirement: for a chosen sparsity level k and coherence level μ (the largest absolute normalised inner product between any two dictionary atoms), a sparse-decomposition is unique only when k μ falls below a threshold — this is the classical coherence condition transplanted into what they call the *atomic inner product*, a representation-induced metric in which every atom has unit norm by construction and which is invariant to the invertible reparameterisations that leave the network's function untouched. They then report the largest quantile q at which the empirical k and coherence of the actual coefficient distribution jointly satisfy the condition, found by bisection; ideal atoms have q = 1. Neurons are scored with the identity dictionary, learned features with the released or retrained dictionaries, and the comparison is made layer by layer on several thousand entity-prompt representations taken from CounterFact-style prompts. They prove (Theorem 11) that a threshold-activated SAE — a bidirectional threshold on the preactivation, so both a positive and a negative branch can be selected — recovers the atom set exactly whenever a *squeeze* condition holds: inactive coordinates must stay below the threshold and active ones above it by a margin that grows with the coherence. They instantiate that as **BiJumpReLU**, train it alongside ReLU, TopK, AbsTopK and JumpReLU under one matched recipe (one global scale normalisation, one learning rate, one batch size, expansion factors chosen to keep widths comparable), and then, for the semantic question, train per-layer BiJumpReLUs on HowNet entity representations, represent each atom and each sememe as an entity set, and greedily grow, for each sememe, the cross-layer combination of atoms maximising the F1 of set overlap against the sememe's own entity set — with the cross-layer width recorded per sememe as the confound to be ruled out.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **feature dictionary with two numbers attached per dictionary** — an R² for how much of the representation it carries and a quantile for how far the unique-decomposition guarantee reaches — plus, on top, a **set-matching account of semantics**: an atom is understood as an entity set (the entities that activate it), a human concept as an entity set, and the explanatory relation is the overlap between them, either for the best single atom or for a greedy combination across layers. It is partial by construction: monorepresentationality (a formally provable uniqueness of each decomposition) is separated explicitly from monosemanticity (a statistical property, needing the judge), and the paper says the first is only a necessary condition for the second. It predicts one thing — that a combination of atoms should recover a sememe better than its best atom — and tests that against a search-artefact control rather than assuming it.

## How they judged success

Fidelity by coefficient of determination; stability by the largest coherence-and-sparsity quantile satisfying the uniqueness condition, computed by bisection over quantiles of both the sparsity and the pairwise-coherence distributions; both reported as means with two-sided 95% intervals across six layers (or diffusion stages); a same-recipe sweep over five SAE architectures on eight model–dataset combinations, with the pre-trained Gemma Scope (two widths) and Llama Scope releases included as external arms; monosemanticity by a "largest coherent subset" score — an LLM judge (with manual revision where the judge's boundary is too broad) shown ten activating contexts and asked for the size of the largest subset sharing one specific interpretation, restricted explicitly so that "all are entities / all are words" does not count; correlation between stability and monosemanticity pooled and then stratified by dataset, by model and by architecture; semantic alignment by precision, recall and F1 of entity-set overlap, single-atom versus cross-layer greedy combination, with an **equivalence test** (prespecified bound on |r|) for the claim that performance does not rise with cross-layer width; and a manual audit of the difference sets for annotation errors on both sides.

## What they found

**The two candidate units fail in opposite directions.** Neurons have near-perfect fidelity — by construction, since the representation *is* written in their coordinates — and low stability; learned features raise stability but pay a fidelity cost, and neither class approaches the ideal point on both axes, across the three model families and both dictionary widths of the released arms. The pattern is stable across layers and is the paper's setup fact rather than its result.

**The bidirectional threshold is what makes identifiability provable.** Under the squeeze condition a TSAE recovers the atom set exactly; a plain ReLU encoder cannot satisfy it, because it has no threshold term to place between the inactive and active magnitudes, and one-sided threshold variants (JumpReLU, TopK) fix a preferred sign for each direction, which the authors argue is arbitrary in a representation space where a direction and its negation have equal standing. In the sweep, BiJumpReLU takes the highest mean for both criteria in all eight model–dataset combinations; TopK and AbsTopK keep fidelity but lose stability; ReLU keeps stability but loses fidelity; and JumpReLU is the strongest competitor but swings widely across models — its stability is stated to run from 64.12 to 98.41 on the 0–100 scale, where BiJumpReLU stays above 98 everywhere. The absolute fidelity and stability cells of Table 1 are blanked in the served HTML, so only those ranges and the ranking are quotable.

**Stability predicts monosemanticity, and the prediction is the claim worth carrying.** Across all 40 dataset–model–SAE combinations stability is positively rank-correlated with the judged monosemanticity score; the correlation survives within each of the three corpora and within all eight model settings, and the coefficients and p-values are blanked in this rendering. Architecture by architecture the association is reported to hold as well. The authors' own framing is the honest one: the theorem gives a structural condition (unique decomposition), the measurement shows the structural condition tracks the semantic one, and the gap between them is left open rather than argued away.

**Human concepts are compositions, not atoms.** Near one-to-one atom–sememe matches exist — a sememe recovered almost completely by a single atom with F1 at or near 1 appears in the case table — but the paper states they are only about **one percent of all sememes**, and most sememes take more than six atoms across layers. The case table shows the shape of it: HowNet's DISTRICT sememe is recovered by 49 atoms in Gemma-2-2B at precision 0.968 / recall 0.998 / F1 0.983, by 28 atoms in Gemma-2-9B at F1 0.988, by 50 atoms in Llama-3.1-8B at F1 0.980; DAY takes 166 atoms in Gemma-2-2B at F1 0.894 and 34 in Gemma-2-9B at F1 0.922; CAPITAL takes 126 atoms in Gemma-2-9B at F1 0.992; MALAWI takes 2 atoms at F1 0.941 in both Gemma-2-2B and Gemma-2-9B; MORNING takes 40 atoms in Gemma-2-2B and 34 in Gemma-2-9B, both at F1 above 0.91; BACTERIA takes 109 atoms in Gemma-2-2B at F1 0.937; and FEEL takes 5 atoms at F1 0.818. Combinations beat the best single atom on F1 as a rule, and the paper attributes the gain to recall with precision held. The number of atoms selected rises with sememe complexity measured by number of annotated entities, in log–log regression per model.

**The confound they test.** Because the combination is found by a cross-layer greedy search, a higher F1 could be a search artefact — more layers available, more chance to overfit the entity set. They report the correlation between cross-layer width and final F1 as close to zero for all three models, with an equivalence test placing |r| inside a prespecified bound, and the slope of the fitted line negligible. Supplementary figures 10–13 choose the two search hyperparameters by grid search, for those who want to see the knob's own range.

**And the annotation cuts both ways.** The manual audit of the difference sets found entities the combination recovers that are plainly consistent with the sememe but absent from HowNet (a Bavarian restaurant brand and Red Square in the DISTRICT difference set for Llama-3.1-8B, a hyperthermophile genus in BACTERIA for Gemma-2-2B), and entities HowNet includes that are plainly inconsistent (an announcer and game theory in BACTERIA, the word "district" itself in one Gemma-2-9B difference set). The paper's reading is that part of the measured mismatch is measurement error in the gold standard.

**One instrument result worth separating out.** The atomic inner product is not decoration: with the Euclidean cosine, pairwise angles between representations of unrelated entities sit systematically away from 90° in every layer of GPT-2-Large, Pythia-6.9B and Llama-3.1-8B, and the shift is not a sampling artefact (it is unchanged as the sample of 128 entities grows); re-measuring the same representations under the metric induced by their second-order statistics recentres the distributions near 90° while leaving the high-similarity tail — the semantically related pairs — in place.

## Limits and open problems they name

Fidelity and stability are dictionary-level statistics, so they say nothing about any individual atom's usefulness; the stability quantile is a property of the sparsity/coherence pair and can be raised by shrinking the dictionary (the capacity section picks widths to satisfy it, which makes the criterion partly self-fulfilling across models). The monosemanticity score is judge-produced on ten instances per unit with ten units per setting, and it is averaged, not distributed. The sememe analysis is on HowNet only, an English-entity lexicon with its own annotation errors, which the paper's own audit demonstrates. Cross-layer combination is greedy, capped, and prefiltered by precision, so the reported F1 is not an optimum over combinations. The theory's squeeze condition is stated for uniform scalar thresholds in the main theorem and extended to coordinate-wise vectors only by remark; whether real trained SAEs sit inside the condition is never checked numerically against their own k and coherence, which is the one bridge between Theorem 11 and the experiments that the paper asserts rather than measures. Monorepresentationality is necessary but explicitly not sufficient for monosemanticity, and no test is offered that would distinguish a dictionary that fails the second while passing the first from one that passes both.

## What we take from it

- **A measure to adopt, at essentially no cost: report a uniqueness quantile beside every reconstruction score.** Fidelity alone is a statement about how much variance a dictionary carries; stability here is a statement about how far the *decomposition itself* is unique, and the two rank the candidate units in opposite orders — neurons top fidelity, features top stability, neither top both. Our own write-ups quote reconstruction and interpretability as if they were independent; this supplies the joint criterion and shows they are a trade-off along one axis.
- **A framing to steal: separate a structural guarantee from a semantic one and say which is which.** Monorepresentationality is proved; monosemanticity is measured; the paper never lets the first stand in for the second and prints the empirical correlation that links them. That is the cleanest statement in the folder of what an "explanation" here does and does not inherit from algebra.
- **A warning for every superposition claim.** The choice of inner product is not neutral: a shared dominant direction inflates the cosine between unrelated representations in exactly the layers where the superposition argument is usually made, and a metric estimated from the representations' own second-order statistics removes it while preserving the related pairs. Before quoting coherence, cosine or orthogonality numbers, print the angular distribution's centroid under the Euclidean metric — this is the PC1/dominant-direction lesson (in the massive-activation and pooled-denominator entries) arriving as a *geometry* problem rather than a fidelity problem.
- **A compositional ceiling for "one feature per concept" claims.** If about 1% of hand-annotated concepts map to a single atom and the rest need several atoms from different layers, then a dictionary-level interpretability score is silent about concept-level alignment, and any steering or editing claim that maps a human concept to one direction is aiming at a rung the atoms do not occupy. The paper's own discussion of knowledge editing and steering draws exactly this consequence: intervening on one direction may touch only part of a concept or several at once, which is the mechanical story behind incomplete edits and collateral damage.
- **A control to copy: the search-artefact equivalence test.** When a result comes from a greedy search over an enlarging candidate pool, report the correlation between pool size and score and run an *equivalence* test against a prespecified bound, not a null-hypothesis test against zero. A non-significant correlation is not evidence of no effect, and this paper is the rare one that says so by design.
- **A dataset to consider, and its price.** OpenHowNet's sememe layer gives a hand-annotated concept hierarchy to test alignment against, with entity sets as the common currency — but the audit that found annotation errors on both sides is the reason to treat any alignment score against it as jointly measuring model and lexicon.
