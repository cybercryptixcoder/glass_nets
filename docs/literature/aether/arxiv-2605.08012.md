# Position: Mechanistic Interpretability Must Disclose Identification Assumptions for Causal Claims

- **Key:** arxiv-2605.08012
- **Authors / organisation:** Zezheng Lin, Fengming Liu
- **Date:** 2026-05-08
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.08012
- **Kind:** paper (position paper with an audit attached)
- **Read from:** full text (v1 HTML, SS1-9 plus Tables 1-4; references skipped)
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

Papers in mechanistic interpretability have taken up causal vocabulary - circuits, mediators, causal abstraction, monosemanticity - while rarely saying what has to be true of the model and the intervention for such a claim to follow from the experiment. The authors' diagnosis is that the field routinely reports a *validation* quantity (faithfulness, completeness, a monosemanticity or alignment score, the size of an ablation effect) and treats it as support for a *causal* claim, without a statement of the conditions under which the causal quantity is identified. Their remedy is procedural rather than technical: import the one habit econometrics settled on, namely that a causal claim opens with its identification strategy and the assumptions that strategy needs, and give interpretability a concrete disclosure form. They want it adopted as a venue checklist norm, and they argue the requirement costs nothing in framework commitment - no potential outcomes, no do-calculus required, only an honest statement of the assumptions the paper's own interventions already lean on.

## What they studied

Papers, not models - the unit of analysis is the *paper* and its reporting practice. A purposive main audit of 10 papers spanning four strands (circuit discovery, sparse autoencoders, causal abstraction, probing) across NeurIPS, ICLR, CLeaR, BlackboxNLP and the Anthropic Transformer Circuits Thread, deliberately mixing founding works with recent ones. An extension audit of 30 further interpretability papers across a wider venue list, coded by two independent human coders from title, abstract, strand, venue and year only. A third, single-rater author audit of 20 papers (stratified 5/5/4/4/2 across circuit discovery, activation patching, SAEs, probing, causal abstraction; seed 0xB1D) on two of the four dimensions, plus a released second-coder re-coding of those same dimensions. The models named are second-hand: GPT-2 small circuits, Claude 3 Sonnet SAEs, Alpaca-7B alignments, GPT-2 XL neurons.

## How they did it

Each paper is coded on four dimensions. **A** classifies the abstract's claim: explicit causal with an identification basis named, explicit causal without one, implicit causal, or association only. **B** covers the main text: a dedicated identification-assumptions section, partial discussion, or nothing explicit. **C** records whether any falsifiability test, sensitivity analysis or nothing at all is present. **D** asks whether a validation metric is offered as causal support without separation from identification (yes/partial/no). The hard coding decision - whether the phrase "causal intervention" is a claim or a method label - gets a written decision rule (Table 1) and its own sensitivity analysis, since a permissive rule would promote three papers from one code to another. Every per-paper coding ships with an abstract summary, a source locator, search evidence and a justification paragraph so any single label can be contested on the published rubric. The main audit is explicitly single-coder and the authors decline to compute agreement statistics from one coder's labels. The 30-paper extension is coded blind between the two coders, and disagreements there are resolved twice, under a strict and a permissive rule, so the count's sensitivity to the coding rule is visible rather than buried. They then write out the four assumptions each strand relies on most and, in Table 4, pair each method with the validation metric usually reported, the assumption that metric leaves untested, and why.

## What counts as an explanation here

Not a mechanistic explanation at all - the object of explanation is a *paper's argument*. What the authors want to see in print is a five-part disclosure: say in the abstract whether the claim is causal or associative; if causal, name the identification strategy; give a numbered assumptions section in which each assumption asserts something, is argued plausible and is backed by evidence; supply for each core assumption at least a falsifier, a sensitivity analysis, or a stated empirical result that would count against it; and discuss what happens to the conclusions if the assumption fails. The corresponding list of strand-specific assumptions is the paper's substantive offering to practitioners: circuit completeness, pathway exclusivity and metric sufficiency for patching; canonical basis, atomicity, monosemanticity and feature completeness for SAEs; alignment faithfulness and optimisation validity for causal abstraction; causal locality, intervention completeness and behavioural sufficiency for probing. The disclosure is a precondition on a causal claim, not a prediction of model behaviour.

## How they judged success

No model metric is involved; the "measurement" is a coded audit. Success is agreement across four independent audits and across coding rules: the same direction of finding in the 10-paper single-coder audit, the 30-paper two-human-coder audit, the 20-paper author audit and the second-coder re-coding. Inter-coder reliability is reported with raw agreement, Cohen's kappa and Krippendorff's alpha per dimension (Table 3), and the Dim D count is reported under both a strict and a permissive adjudication. The authors set themselves one standard the data actually bears - the absence of a dedicated assumptions section - and treat the substitution counts as a moderate-confidence pattern rather than a prevalence estimate.

## What they found

No paper in the main sample has a dedicated identification-assumptions section (0/10 by Dim B's top code), and 8 of 10 are scored as substituting a validation metric for identification, fully or partly. Only two papers separate the two cleanly, and both are methodological-critique papers whose subject *is* the failure of those criteria - Makelov et al. on subspace patching illusions and Canby et al. on causal-probe reliability. Zero of the ten contain a falsifiability test; three carry only a sensitivity analysis. In the 30-paper audit the structural finding repeats exactly: 0/30 under any rule and any coder. Dim D comes out at 19/30 under the strict rule and 26/30 under the permissive one. Strand heterogeneity appears that the small sample hid: SAEs (6/6) and causal abstraction (3/3) substitute at full rate, while activation patching is the lowest at 2/7, which the authors read as partial protection from the illusion-and-failure-modes literature already existing inside that strand. The author's 20-paper audit reproduces the null section count (0/20) and puts substitution at 16/20. Reliability is where the audit bends: Dim A agreement is 0.80 with kappa 0.56; Dim B, the dimension carrying the headline claim, has raw agreement 0.57 with kappa 0.26 and alpha 0.14. The released second coder disagrees further on the 30-paper set (kappa 0.08 on Dim B, 0.25 on Dim D), leaning toward the middle code. The authors state plainly that the absence of a dedicated section survives this and that the substitution count does not.

## Limits and open problems they name

The main audit is one coder's judgment on ten papers chosen by the authors, so it documents a gap in a sample and does not estimate prevalence. The extension audits read abstracts and metadata rather than full texts, which is defended as auditing the layer reviewers actually see but conceded to make exact Dim B and Dim D counts coding-rule-sensitive. Dim B is the dimension with the worst reliability precisely because coders cannot agree whether limitation-talk counts as partial disclosure. No external independent full-text recoding is included. The authors anticipate three objections - that interpretability is engineering and owes no causal disclosure, that formal assumptions are premature, and that checklist sections will become boilerplate - and answer each in a paragraph rather than with evidence; the performativity worry in particular has no measurement behind it.

## What we take from it

**A form to copy, and a warning about our own numbers.** (1) The five-part disclosure is directly usable as the front matter of every entry in this folder and, more importantly, of our own results: before a claim about a mechanism, name which of the strand assumptions it needs (completeness? exclusivity? basis canonicity?) and say which one we have actually stressed. (2) Table 4's pairing - method / metric reported / assumption left untested / why the metric cannot test it - is the cleanest statement on disk of the substitution pattern this folder has been documenting entry by entry from the measurement side; we should carry a column of that shape in our own evaluation tables. (3) The audit's own reliability is the warning: the one dimension where two coders could not agree (kappa 0.26) is the one whose count carries the headline, and the authors report the disagreement instead of the summary statistic. Any human-judgment code in our work needs per-dimension reliability printed next to the count, and a rule-sensitivity pair (strict and permissive), because here the same labels yield 19/30 or 26/30 depending on which side of an ambiguity you resolve.
- Warning worth keeping: a paper whose contribution *is* the critique is the only kind in this sample that separates validation from identification, which suggests the field's good practice currently arrives one failure at a time rather than as a norm - exactly the pattern our folder's successive entries have traced.
