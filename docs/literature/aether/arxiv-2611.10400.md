# Detecting and Explaining Robot Policy Failures from Sparse Internal Features

- **Read from:** full text (arXiv HTML v1, ~92k clean chars: §1–§4, references, Appendices A–E; every figure read via its caption only, so all numbers below are table cells or explicit sentences)
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

Robot policies that look reliable on benchmarks fail sharply under camera-viewpoint or initial-state changes, and small errors compound over a long rollout. Watching the robot shows *what* went wrong — the gripper closed short of the bowl — but not *why*, because the mapping from observation to action stays inside the network. The authors want a failure report in the policy's own terms: not merely "something is off", but which internal quantity the policy was representing when it went wrong (in their example, an early closure decision rather than a mis-estimated bowl position). That is a step toward a white-box that is consulted at run time: the same decomposition must both raise the alarm and carry the explanation, so the alarm's "why" comes from the feature that moved the score rather than from a human reading the recording afterwards. The framing is deliberately diagnostic rather than corrective — they say explicitly that they do not offer recovery, only understanding, and that recovery design should be informed by it.

## What they studied

Three vision-language-action policies that produce actions in different ways: OpenVLA (7B, actions as language-model tokens) on LIBERO Spatial / Object / Goal (ten tasks, twenty initial states each); LingBot-VLA 2.0 (4B backbone plus an action expert emitting fifty actions, ten executed) on RoboTwin 2.0 tasks `lift_pot` and `click_bell`; and π0.5 (2B backbone, 1024-d action expert returning a ten-action chunk by flow matching) on one task per LIBERO suite and on a physical Franka Panda with a six-joint Inspire hand (stove task: pot then lid; cups task: stack two cups in a third). The unit of analysis is the **sparse-autoencoder basis feature** of one intermediate activation, read at named token positions (the last instruction token for a state or the next decision, the action position that writes an axis, each image patch for an object concept), fed into a per-step *record* over features and then a scalar alarm. Labels come from the simulator on simulated frames and, on the robot, from kinematics, hand-joint sensors, a text-prompted detector and a video segmentation model with the depth image — with no label wrong on thirty demonstrations checked by a human.

## How they did it

A sparse autoencoder of the Templeton-style form (unit-direction columns, coefficient equal to column length times code, ReLU, reconstruction plus λ times the sum of coefficients) is fitted on the activations of a middle layer — layer 20 of OpenVLA (d = 4096, m = 16,384), layer 22 of the LingBot-VLA action expert (d = 768, m = 3,072), both streams of π0.5 at layers 7/11/15 in simulation and layer 11 on the robot (m = 4d) — at **every token position separately**, not on a per-frame mean. Fitting uses only the residual loss and no task, concept or outcome information; λ is chosen so that about fifty basis features are on per vector, and on the real robot a per-step mean vector (88–95% of vector norm) is subtracted before the fit and before every naming or clamping pass, because otherwise no λ reaches the target sparsity. **Naming is a fixed rule, not a human's judgement:** a basis feature fires at level τ when its coefficient exceeds τ times its maximum over the fit; for each (concept, τ) pair from a predefined 74-concept set of states (near, grasped, lifted, carried) and decisions (sign of each action axis, open/half-closed/closed), precision and recall are measured against the labels; a pair is allowed when precision ≥ 0.9, recall ≥ 0.05 and the feature has fired ≥ 50 times; the name is the allowed pair with the highest recall. Names are chosen on *naming frames* the fit did not see (even frames fit, odd frames name on OpenVLA; alternating episodes on LingBot-VLA) and must re-pass the same rule on *held-out episodes* — unseen initial states, an unseen tenth LIBERO task, unseen RoboTwin episodes, unseen π0.5 layouts, five robot attempts per task — firing in at least five episodes with no episode supplying more than half the firings. Decision concepts get a second test: the feature is clamped in closed loop to 1.5× its maximum at every step, and the share of steps whose action lies on the named side is compared against four controls — other live basis features at the same clamp, a random unit direction of equal norm, a supervised regression direction fitted to the concept's labels, and the summed direction of all basis features named for the concept. **Detection** builds five numbers per named feature per step (on/off, running means over 4/16/64-frame windows, and a 16-frame switching rate), forms one vector, and fits a logistic alarm on fitting episodes where failed episodes contribute only from their failure onset onward (the earlier of losing the grip and the step by which nine in ten successful episodes had reached a phase the failure never reaches); the threshold is set at a 5% false-alarm rate on successful fitting episodes and an alarm needs three consecutive frames above it. **Explanation** exploits the fact that the score is linear: it decomposes exactly into one term per record coordinate, sums per concept, ranks the concepts, and hands only that record — instruction, on-intervals, what a successful attempt would already show, alarm step, responsible concepts — to Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct with greedy decoding, whose prompt permits no concept outside the list and reports a feature that never fired as something the policy did not represent.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **named sparse feature plus an exact linear contribution to a scalar alarm**, on a fixed predefined concept vocabulary, plus a short natural-language note generated from that record alone. It is partial in a specific sense the authors acknowledge: the name is a tested correlation on held-out episodes and the clamp establishes that the policy acts through the feature, but the explanation is bounded by the concept list — the failure *category* is only as good as whether the phases that separate failure types are among the concepts. One quantity of interest, the direction that causes motion, is deliberately outside the vocabulary, and that exclusion turns out to matter (below).

## How they judged success

Four measurements, each with a stated control. **Naming:** count of concepts whose name still holds at precision ≥ 0.9 on held-out episodes, against the same rule applied to raw coordinates, random directions and shuffled labels — plus, on OpenVLA, feed-forward neurons and the dictionary of an untrained policy — with a two-random-start agreement check. **Steering:** share of steps on the named side with and without the clamp, against the four controls, plus arm displacement along the axis in twenty steps. **Detection:** per-episode AUROC over held-out episodes with 95% bootstrap intervals and the false-alarm share, against a dense monitor on the full activation with the same labels and thresholds, plus a length-only rule as a trivial baseline, plus a delay measure (median steps from the simulator's onset to the alarm) and the share of alarms preceding the onset. **Diagnosis:** stability of the top responsible concept across twenty bootstrap refits, agreement of the language model's answers with the record, and agreement of the note's category with the simulator — compared against a mechanical rule reading the same record. On the robot, leave-one-attempt-out, with the monitor fixed before any fit reported separately.

## What they found

Naming survives transfer, and the baselines collapse: on OpenVLA the dictionary keeps 15–31 decision and 13–28 state names at unseen initial states and 6–5 / 16–14 / 3–2 on the unseen task, while **no baseline — neurons and the untrained copy included — names a single concept** at unseen states or on the robot. On π0.5 in LIBERO the dictionary names 42–53 concepts per task over both streams against 13–16 for raw coordinates and 8–11 for random directions, with 28–37 named by the dictionary alone; on the physical robot it wins all 14 stove concepts and all 6 cups concepts with zero from every baseline. Two random starts share 81–83% of OpenVLA's named features (73% under the same name). Two limits on this win are printed in the same tables: on the two action-expert policies, raw coordinates already name most *decision* concepts because many action-expert directions simply follow the action they write (28–29 decisions for the dictionary against 25 and 18 for raw coordinates on `lift_pot`), and the dictionary's lead is almost entirely on *state* (9–10 against 4); on the real robot's action expert the dictionary names 5 and 14 features at the last denoising step but **zero at the first, where raw coordinates name 4**, because at the first step the input is noise and at the last it is 57–59% a copy of the action it writes.

Steering is the paper's strongest result and it comes with an inversion. On OpenVLA, clamping one basis feature raises the share of steps on the named side from 0.00–0.34 to 0.92–1.00 for twelve of fourteen motion concepts, moves the arm 5–22 cm within twenty steps, and matches a supervised regression direction (0.95–1.00) without any labels, while other live features and an equal-norm random direction never raise the share by more than 0.08; the summed direction of a concept's features is more reliable than any single one (moving down: one feature 0.68/0.54, summed 1.00 on both suites). On π0.5, the clamp moves the arm along the named axis but **against the named sign**, and the authors do not treat this as a naming error: every direction chosen by prediction for a decision — nine named features at precision 0.906–0.990, the summed directions of four families and four supervised probes — is anti-aligned with the action gradient through the flow sampler, at cosines −0.08 to −0.26, while the 14 of 20 features most aligned with the gradient carry the *opposite* label at precision 0.04–0.37, below the naming floor. Clamping one of those sub-floor features raises the share along +x from 0.36 to 1.00 (random 0.22) and **drops task success from 1.00 to 0.00**. Detection ties the dense monitor while every input carries a name: π0.5 0.991 [0.980, 1.000] against 0.988 [0.973, 0.999], at whose operating point it catches 99% of failures against 95% with 3% false alarms against 6% and a median 10 steps after onset against 25; OpenVLA Object/Goal single-feature 0.89/0.92 against 0.91/0.92 with overlapping intervals, but the fused variant 0.93/0.97 at 9%/2% false alarms. Two ablations carry real weight: dropping the dwells and switching rate costs 0.89 → 0.81 and 0.92 → 0.79 AUROC (memory, not just framing, is doing the work), and the trivial length rule alarms 49/148/69 steps after onset against 17/28/25 — though the dense monitor alarms *earlier* on OpenVLA Spatial/Object while 29–38% of its alarms precede the simulator's own onset, against 7–21% for the fused named monitor, which is why the paper reports that share rather than the delay alone. Diagnosis is faithful to the record (85–89% of the model's answers match it, 11 of 12 successes called normal, top concept stays in the top three in a median 90–95% of bootstrap refits) but **not right about the failure type**: category agreement is 15 of 48 OpenVLA failures and about a third on π0.5 — as often as a mechanical rule reading the same record — and on the robot 3 of 8 stove failures against the rule's 4. The robot's main monitors all include chance (stove 0.70, cups 0.60), and the authors diagnose why: the operator stops a failing attempt about two seconds after it goes wrong, so only 9–14% of its frames show the failure against 64–66% on OpenVLA; refitting on a deliberately subsampled OpenVLA at the robot's counts still gives 0.86–1.00, so the limit is recorded failure length, not sample size.

## Limits and open problems they name

No recovery mechanism (stated as a scoping choice, not a failure). The explanation's type is bounded by the concept list — the phases separating failure types are absent from the vocabulary, and the paper says so twice with matched mechanical-rule comparisons. On LingBot-VLA the monitor sees only an episode's opening frames because failures run to the step limit, so the `lift_pot` comparison is uninformative by construction (58 of 91 failures occur after both arms have grasped). On the robot the closed-loop clamp was run only in simulation, more varied placements and full-length failures are named as future work, and every robot detection interval includes chance. One structural negative is reported without being labelled as such: at the first denoising step, where the input is noise, the dictionary names nothing and raw coordinates win — the dictionary's advantage is confined to positions where the action is not already in the input.

## What we take from it

Three transfers and one warning. **The naming rule is the reusable instrument:** fix the concept vocabulary *before* looking at activations, score each (concept, firing level) pair by precision and recall on frames excluded from the fit, take the highest-recall pair that clears a precision floor, then re-test the frozen name on episodes that took no part in choosing it. This replaces "a human writes what the top activations have in common" with a measurement that can fail, and it makes every baseline comparable by construction — which is how the paper gets clean zeros from neurons and random directions instead of anecdotes. **The four-control clamp is the matched-arm discipline done properly:** other live features at the same multiple, a random direction at equal norm, a label-fitted supervised direction, and the summed direction, each answering a different rival hypothesis (any large perturbation; any direction of this size; the label is doing the work; one feature versus the whole concept). **The sign inversion is the folder's most instructive case of a name and a cause coming apart:** the quantity the feature predicts and the quantity the clamp acts through can be *oppositely* oriented, because the encoding is anti-aligned with the computation that produces it, and the tell is that a supervised probe shows the same inversion — a probe does not adjudicate between "the interpretation is wrong" and "the encoding is inverted", it only inherits it. The warning is about record coverage: a linear score over a fixed feature set can only explain what the vocabulary spans, and here that ceiling appeared as an explanation that was 85–89% faithful and 31% correct — two numbers that must never be collapsed into one.
