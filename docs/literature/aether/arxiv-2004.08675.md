# CWY Parametrization for Scalable Learning of Orthogonal and Stiefel Matrices

- **Key:** arxiv-2004.08675
- **Authors / organisation:** Valerii Likhosherstov, Jared Davis, Krzysztof Choromanski, Adrian Weller; University of Cambridge, Google Brain Robotics (New York), Columbia University, The Alan Turing Institute
- **Date:** 2020-04-18
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.08675
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

Constraining a weight matrix to be orthogonal controls the norm of the hidden state and of the gradient travelling through it, which is the standard remedy for exploding or vanishing gradients in recurrent networks and, in feed-forward settings, a way to bound a model's Lipschitz constant by construction. The trouble is that the parameterisations people already had were built as *sequential* algebraic objects: a product of Householder reflections, a product of block-diagonal unitary factors, a matrix exponential, a Cayley transform. Each one yields an exactly orthogonal matrix from unconstrained parameters, and each one is a poor fit for the hardware everybody now trains on, because the reflections or factors have to be applied one after another in a long chain of matrix-vector products that no GPU kernel can overlap. The authors' goal is narrow and practical rather than conceptual: keep the exactness that unconstrained parameterisation gives you, keep the compatibility with off-the-shelf optimisers like Adam, and rearrange the *same* algebra so that the computation becomes a small number of large dense products. The paper presents itself as an efficiency contribution to a set of methods that already exist, and it is candid about that: in exact arithmetic the main construction is the Householder product in different clothing.

## What they studied

Recurrent architectures with an orthogonal transition matrix, and — through a variant — convolutional recurrent units whose recurrent kernel obeys a Stiefel constraint. The unit of analysis is the **weight matrix itself**, or rather the factorisation that generates it. There are no trained-network internals here: no neurons, features, heads or circuits. The objects are the orthogonal group O(N) and the Stiefel manifold St(N,M), the parameter matrices U whose orthonormal columns generate them, and the small triangular matrix S that aggregates a whole reflection series. On the recurrent side the hidden dimension is N = 1024 and the number of reflections L is swept from 64 to 1024. On the convolutional side the constrained object is the flattened recurrent kernel qK̂ of size (q²f_out × f_out) with q = 3 and f_out = 32, so the Stiefel matrix is 288×32. Tasks are English–Spanish sentence translation from the Tatoeba corpus (encoder–decoder with attention, over 100,000 pairs, 80,000 train / 20,000 test) and one-step-ahead video frame prediction on the KTH action dataset (128×128 frames reshaped to 64×64×4, a five-block bottleneck recurrent stack).

## How they did it

The core move is a classical numerical-linear-algebra identity brought into machine learning, which the authors say had not been used here before. A composition of L Householder reflections can be written exactly as the identity minus a rank-L correction: the normalised reflection vectors are stacked as columns of an N×L matrix U, and the correction is U S⁻¹ Uᵀ where S is (1/2)I plus the strictly upper triangle of UᵀU. Because S is upper triangular with a constant diagonal it is cheap and stable to invert. This turns a chain of L sequential reflections into two dense matrix products, which is the whole point. They then offer two runtime regimes. With L = N the transition matrix is materialised once and used like any other RNN weight, costing what the exponential and Cayley methods cost but behaving better as a model. With L < N they never form Q at all and instead expand the recurrence into three steps per timestep — project the state down with Uᵀ, solve against S⁻¹, project back with U — giving a per-timestep cost linear in L rather than quadratic in N, at the price of covering only the subset of orthogonal matrices expressible with L reflections. A truncated variant extends the same construction to the Stiefel manifold: truncate the identity to the first M columns, keep an M×M upper block of U in the correction, and the same U S⁻¹ structure produces an N×M matrix with orthonormal columns. They prove this truncated map is surjective onto St(N,M) by an induction that peels off one column per reflection, mirroring the classical Householder QR peeling. Finally they build a convolutional recurrent unit by imposing the Stiefel constraint on the flattened recurrent kernel and argue, via a Frobenius-norm chain and a q² factor from the receptive field, that the hidden state cannot grow exponentially. Experiments compare against URNN, EURNN, Householder RNN, exponential RNN, scaled Cayley, orthogonal weight normalisation, and several Riemannian-gradient-descent variants with Cayley or QR retractions under two inner-product choices, plus LSTM, GRU and unconstrained RNN baselines, and on the convolutional side ConvLSTM, a no-recurrence baseline, and Stiefel-initialised unconstrained recurrence.

## What counts as an explanation here

Nothing explanatory in the sense this survey cares about. The deliverable is a **parameterisation**: a map from an unconstrained set of real vectors to the constrained set of matrices, proved (or asserted) to cover the set you want, with a stated computational cost. Understanding, in this paper, means knowing exactly which subset of the manifold a given number of reflections can reach — the notation O_L(N) for "orthogonal matrices obtainable with L reflections" is the clearest expression of that — and knowing the flop count of getting there. There is no claim about what any trained matrix means. The one predictive statement is a bound: the convolutional unit's hidden-state norm cannot blow up, because the recurrent kernel has bounded spectral norm and transposition preserves operator norms.

## How they judged success

Three currencies, reported separately and never combined into a single score: **runtime complexity** in a symbol table with a bracket convention marking terms that parallelise, **solution domain** (which subset of the manifold each method covers, including "a strict subset", "all but matrices with eigenvalue −1", "all of O_L(N)"), and **task performance** — test cross-entropy on translation, per-frame L1 loss on video, with parameter counts, wall-clock training minutes, and peak GPU memory. The translation table is repeated as an appendix with three seeds per model giving mean and standard deviation, and the authors state plainly that they did not run extra seeds for the non-CWY orthogonal baselines because those were slow and the comparison with them was about speed. That is an honest disclosure, and it also means the learning-side rankings against those methods rest on one run each.

## What they found

On translation at hidden size 1024 the best CWY setting (L = 128) reaches 0.50 test cross-entropy against 0.55 for LSTM, 0.56 for GRU, 0.73 for a plain RNN, and 0.58 / 0.59 / 2.01 for the scaled-Cayley, exponential and Riemannian baselines, while training in 198 minutes against 1780–2960 for those three and 232 for LSTM. The L sweep is non-monotone: 0.60 at L=64, 0.50 at L=128, 0.64 at L=256, 0.66 at L=512, 0.56 at L=1024, which the authors read as a trade between expressiveness and the geometry of the loss landscape, with the standard deviations in the appendix (0.01 at L=128, 0.03 at L=1024) making the dip look real but the neighbours fairly close. On video prediction the Stiefel-constrained unit beats every Stiefel-optimisation baseline on all six action classes (for example 134.6 against 135.5–143.3 on walking, 166.7 against 169.6–177.8 on running), uses about 0.72M parameters against ConvLSTM's 3.26M, and 3.5 Gb of GPU memory against 8.7 Gb — while ConvLSTM itself mostly fails to beat the no-recurrence baseline. The unconstrained Stiefel-*initialised* model is close behind the constrained one (145.8 Glorot-init against 134.6 constrained on walking), so a good part of the gap to ConvLSTM is attributable to the simpler unit and the initialisation rather than to the constraint being maintained throughout training.

## Limits and open problems they name

The paper's own conclusion is that flexible, sub-N expressiveness has been worked out for the square orthogonal case but not yet for the Stiefel variant — the L < N regime that gives CWY its scaling advantage has no T-CWY counterpart, so every Stiefel experiment here pays the full O(NM² + M³) with an upper-triangular inverse. Beyond what they name: the CWY construction covers O(N) only up to the sign of its determinant (a matrix with det = (−1)^N in the square case), so half of the orthogonal group is reachable only by a post-hoc sign flip that they mention for the exponential method and not for their own; the exact-arithmetic equivalence with explicit Householder products means the contribution is a speed and numerics claim, not a coverage claim, in the full-L regime; the translation table's own error bars mean the headline 0.50 versus 0.55 over LSTM is a gap of roughly one standard deviation; the video-prediction comparison against ConvLSTM carries an instance-normalisation difference the authors flag; and the gradient-explosion argument is a sufficient-norm argument with a generous q² factor, which bounds growth but does not compute how tight the bound actually is on a trained kernel. Nothing in the paper inspects the learned reflection vectors, the fitted S, or the resulting spectrum.

## What we take from it

A **framing** and a **method**, both about how a constraint is paid for rather than what it means. The framing: the difference between two parameterisations of the same set is not coverage but *where the cost lands* — sequential chain versus dense product, and whether that chain maps onto the parallel hardware. This head's earlier members argued over which set a parameterisation reaches and whether the map is invertible; this paper says that with two exactly equivalent maps in hand, the one that survives is the one whose flops parallelise, and it justifies that with wall-clock minutes rather than asymptotics. That is a discipline we should copy: report a parameterisation's cost next to the identical-coverage alternative. The method: **the compact-WY trick is the general recipe for turning a product of many rank-one orthogonal factors into an identity-minus-low-rank-correction with a small triangular solve**, and as a reading instrument it hands us something the head has never had — the reflection vectors U and the aggregation matrix S are an explicit, ordered, *sparse-generative* decomposition of the trained matrix into L rank-one pieces, with S encoding how much the later reflections interfere with the earlier ones. A trained orthogonal recurrent matrix is therefore stored as a sequence of Householder planes plus a triangular mixing of them, and nothing here reads that sequence. The measure: complexity tables that pair runtime against solution domain in the same row are the honest way to present a constrained method, and the bracket notation for parallelisable terms is a small idea worth stealing for our own reporting. Two warnings. First, the ablation that matters is missing: the unconstrained-with-orthogonal-init model recovers most of the constrained model's gain on video, which is exactly the control that tells you how much the constraint is buying over the initialisation — we should run that control before attributing a result to a manifold. Second, an exactness claim inherited from classical linear algebra travelled across literatures here without its sign condition (det = (−1)^N, half of O(N)), which is the third time in this head that a coverage condition shrank in transit.
