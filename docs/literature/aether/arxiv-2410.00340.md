## What they wanted, and why

The top-down question in this paper is why any particular upstream head counts as a cause of a downstream head's behaviour. They observe the failure directly: inspect the residual and almost every earlier head has written something into some downstream head's query or key subspace, so a criterion of "contributed a nonzero component" admits practically the whole model. Only a counterfactual tells you whether a contribution is function-relevant, and counterfactuals are slow, need a task-neutral corruption set, and are known to provoke self-repair downstream. Their answer is to find a basis in which the contribution of the inputs to an attention score is *sparse* - a few terms carry the score, the rest cancel - so that deleting the small terms suppresses background and leaves a legible signal. The larger goal is circuit tracing that is causal, cheap and single-pass, and that names what travels between heads rather than only which head feeds which.

## What they studied

GPT-2 small (12 layers, 12 heads, residual width 768, head dimension 64) on the 30-way Indirect Object Identification task of Wang et al.: 256 prompts, 106 names, the 15 canonical templates in both name orders, sequences 14-20 tokens. The unit of analysis is the **attention firing** - one head, one destination token, one source token with more than half the head's attention weight - and the object produced is a directed acyclic graph whose nodes are heads and token positions and whose edge weights are signals: the projected components of an upstream head's output that fall inside the sparse subspace the downstream head is using. Nothing here is a feature dictionary; the dictionary-like object is the set of singular vectors of each head's query-key matrix, and only those slices that are switched on for a given token pair.

## How they did it

The pre-softmax score of a head is a bilinear form whose matrix is the query-key product with the bias terms folded in as one extra homogeneous coordinate, and that matrix has rank at most the head width. Its singular value decomposition writes the score as a sum of rank-one terms, each of which the authors call an orthogonal slice. For a given token pair they compute the contribution of every slice, then define the noise set as the largest collection of terms whose total is at most zero; the remaining strictly positive terms are the slice set for that pair, and typically there are twenty or fewer, frequently two or three. The projection of the destination token onto the left directions of that set, and of the source token onto the right directions, are taken to be signal, and their orthogonal complements are discarded as noise. To trace, they move upstream from a firing head and, for each earlier head, measure how much of its output lands in the downstream head's signal subspace on the relevant token; edges are kept by taking the smallest set of upstream heads whose contributions sum to seventy per cent of the total. This is a single forward pass with no counterfactual inputs, and it is spelled out as pseudocode in the appendix. Validation then intervenes on individual edges in two places: globally at the upstream head, which lets every downstream consumer see the change, and locally at the input to the one downstream head, which confines the change to that edge. Each edge gets an ablation (subtract the signal), a boost (add it), and a matched control that subtracts or adds an equally sized projection onto randomly chosen slices outside the set. Effects are read as the change in the logit difference between indirect object and subject. The last experiment is structural rather than edge-wise: they ablate several edges at once, separately and together, on edges they classify from the trace as lying on parallel versus serial paths.

## What counts as an explanation here

A weighted graph plus, for each edge, a low-dimensional vector pair - the slice indices and the actual signal components that the downstream head reads. It is deliberately partial: the graph covers attention-to-attention communication only, ignores the multi-layer perceptrons entirely, skips any head that attends chiefly to the first token, and is restricted to heads that fire on a single source. The explanation is predictive in a limited sense - an edge's weight is meant to rank how much that edge matters to the downstream score and, downstream of that, to the answer - and the paper tests both of those ranks separately rather than claiming the first implies the second.

## How they judged success

Four arms of unequal strength, and one of the controls is a within-model null rather than a random baseline. First, sparsity: the distribution of how many slices a firing uses, compared against a deliberately off-task corpus (the first 256 Pile documents truncated to the same length) to show the sparsity is not an artifact of the task. Second, recovery: a trace skeleton filtered to the most frequent edges, scored against the sixteen heads of the reference circuit that are reachable from the three seed heads, with precision and recall reported. Third, causality: per-edge ablation and boosting against the random-slice control, signed on the logit difference, with the size of the perturbation itself reported as a cosine to the unedited residual and a norm ratio. Fourth, structure: whether multi-edge ablation on parallel paths is additive and on serial paths is not, which is a claim about the graph's shape that a set of individually causal edges would not automatically produce.

## What they found

Scores really are sparsely built in this basis: a typical firing needs under twenty slices of the sixty-four available, often two or three, and the same small slice set recurs whenever a given head fires while a different set is used when it does not - a previous-token head leans on one slice while a duplicate-token head uses a broad spread, which the authors read as consistent with each head's known job. Sparsity persists on off-task text, so it is not a property of the canned prompts. Filtering matters more than any single claim: tracing with the full residual instead of the projected signal yields a graph that both misses most of the reference circuit and, tellingly, gets the long-range early-to-late connections wrong, so the noise is not uniformly distributed but concentrated where a causal story is hardest to check. The most frequent-edges skeleton contains eleven of the sixteen reachable reference heads out of twenty-one heads total, i.e. precision about 0.52 and recall about 0.69 - a real recovery with a substantial false-positive tail. Almost every tested edge degrades the logit difference when ablated and improves it when boosted, with stronger edges having larger effects, and the interventions are tiny: the cosine between the residual before and after a local edit is generally above 0.999 and the norm changes by under one per cent, on a subspace of fewer than twenty of 768 dimensions. The structural test is the sharpest result: ablating two parallel paths together produces an effect larger than either alone, while ablating serial edges together does not add up, which is direct evidence for the backup-pathway story that earlier work could only assert. One dissociating case is reported rather than smoothed over - for the name-mover head (9,6), a local ablation lowers that head's attention score yet *raises* the indirect-object logit, in proportion to how much the attention score fell, while a global ablation lowers the logit as usual. The trace also surfaces heads the reference circuit did not name, notably an early head that attends to the ditransitive verb and feeds both the previous-token head and the subject-inhibition head.

## Limits and open problems they name

The noise-separation rule is by their own account permissive and imperfect, and they suggest the recurring slice sets are themselves the better signal for choosing it. Only positively contributing slices are considered, yet they observe that the few strongly negative slices are what decide when a head does *not* fire, and that the head's behaviour looks like a balance between the two groups - explicitly left open. The method does not probe alternative pathways, adaptive computation or minimality in the reference circuit's sense; it covers attention only, and only heads that fire on one source; and the nature of the signals is not investigated, though they infer from the difference between local and global effects that signals on different edges must be similar in kind. From my side of the reading, one more gap stands out: the graph's edges are validated one at a time against a null, and its shape is validated by additivity, but nothing here measures *overlap* between two pathways - the parallel-path result shows redundancy exists, not how much of one path is a copy of another, and a redundancy measure is exactly what would turn "the model has backup routes" into a prediction about what happens when one route is cut.

## What we take from it

A method and, more useful to us, a measurement discipline. The method: decompose a bilinear score matrix into orthogonal slices, keep the smallest slice set that accounts for the positive total, and treat the projections onto those slices as the channel between units. That gives our own model a candidate channel primitive that needs no learned dictionary and no counterfactuals, and it is defined per token pair rather than per head, which is the granularity a predictive model needs. The discipline: report the perturbation magnitude beside every causal effect (cosine and norm ratio), because a large effect at a 0.999 cosine is a different object from a large effect at a broken residual; and validate a graph's *structure* with an additivity test on parallel versus serial sets, because a set of individually causal edges plus an additivity pattern is a stronger object than a set of individually causal edges. The warning: a permissive sparsity rule buys clean graphs at the cost of a recall ceiling - here six of sixteen reference heads never appear - so any sparse-channel model should publish what it deleted, and the deletions that matter most were the long-range ones.
