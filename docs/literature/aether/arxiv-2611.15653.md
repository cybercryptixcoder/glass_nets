# A Mechanistic Interpretability Study of an Astronomy Foundation Model

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.15653
- **Authors / organisation:** Rachel C. Zhang (University of Cambridge / Polymathic AI); Alberto Bietti, Wes Gurnee (Polymathic AI / Flatiron Institute); Alessandro Favero, Miles Cranmer (University of Cambridge / Polymathic AI)
- **Date:** 2026-11-11
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.15653
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (arXiv HTML v1, ~163.5k chars; abstract, §1–§5, references scanned, Appendices A–E in full)
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

Foundation models are being plugged into scientific pipelines whose outputs astronomers will act on, and nobody knows whether the computation inside them corresponds to any operation a domain scientist recognises. Answering that would do double duty: it would say whether the model can be trusted on the inputs where it matters, and it offers a route to new science if the model turns out to have discovered a better operation. The authors pick the smallest well-posed instance they can — why a multimodal astronomy model predicts redshift better when it is shown galaxy images as well as brightness measurements — and try to localise the gain to concrete components and then say what those components compute. The larger goal is to test whether the standard interpretability toolkit (probe, ablate, patch) is even adequate on models where ground truth is measurable, since that is the one setting in which the tools' failure modes could be checked against an answer rather than argued about. It is a test of the instruments dressed as a study of one model.

## What they studied

AION-1 base (~300M parameters, 12 encoder blocks, hidden width 768, 12 heads of dimension 64 per block), a multimodal masked-modelling model trained on several hundred million observations across five surveys; the encoder is frozen and only a small readout head is trained. The task is photometric redshift regression on the DESI Early Data Release bright-galaxy sample, 67,755 galaxies after quality cuts, split 80/20 into 54,204 train and 13,551 held-out. Two input configurations are compared: nine photometry scalars (four optical fluxes, four infrared fluxes, and a Milky Way reddening scalar) giving nine tokens, versus those nine plus a four-band 160×160 cutout encoded by a convolutional codec into 576 tokens, for 585 in total. The unit of analysis runs down four levels: the whole model's accuracy, the encoder block, the individual attention head, and a single linear direction in the residual stream. Ground truth for the scientific properties (redshift, stellar mass, and three dust parameters) comes from spectral fitting in an external catalogue, so every internal measurement has a real number to be checked against.

## How they did it

They first establish the target effect behaviourally and stratify it: for each test galaxy both readouts are scored correct or incorrect, and the authors compare the galaxies only the multimodal readout gets right against the galaxies both get right, using two-sample distribution tests on candidate explanatory properties. Then they localise the gain in depth with ridge probes fit at fourteen encoder stages (the token embedding, each of the twelve block outputs, and the post-encoder representation), regressing each of ten galaxy properties on the mean-pooled hidden state. They confirm that localisation causally with block-resolved activation patching: a corrupted run keeps each galaxy's photometry tokens but receives another galaxy's image tokens, and one block's residual contribution at a time is swapped from clean to corrupted (sufficiency, "denoising") or corrupted to clean (necessity, "noising"), with both fractions normalised by the clean-minus-corrupted contrast. Within the implicated block they probe each of the twelve heads separately, then zero-ablate each head and re-run the unchanged readout, and rank heads two ways — by probe quality and by causal effect — precisely to see whether the two rankings agree. For the property whose extent they want to manipulate, they intervene on the input rather than the activation: each cutout is replaced by a compact point-source template or an extended blur, rescaled per band so that the flux inside the codec's central crop matches the original, which changes morphology without changing what brightness the codec sees. They also erase a property's linear direction from the residual stream with a closed-form concept eraser, with a shuffled-label eraser as the control, and probe every downstream stage afterwards. Finally, for the head that turns out to be causally necessary, they enumerate four candidate operations drawn from the astronomy toolbox and test each with its own designed prediction, then screen all 144 encoder heads with a first-order attribution estimate, verify the flagged ones by ablation, and test whether any later head mediates the effect by cutting single edges and by ablating the source head while freezing candidate mediators. Every interval in the paper is a galaxy-paired bootstrap, and the authors discuss at length why pairing matters here.

## What counts as an explanation here

A partial, component-level causal account, in the "circuit" genre: a block, then a head, then — for the property that resists localisation — an explicit statement that no single head or linear direction carries it. For the localised head the explanation stops short of a function: the paper enumerates four candidate operations and rejects all four, so the deliverable is a precise statement of *where* the computation lives and what it is *not* doing, plus the causal ledger that says how much of the output depends on it. There is no replacement model, no dictionary, and no attempt to predict the network's behaviour under arbitrary interventions; prediction appears only in the narrow form of predicting the size of an ablation effect from a cheap attribution estimate. The paper is candid that this is a weaker object than an explanation, and that the gap between "localised" and "understood" is where it stops.

## How they judged success

Held-out R² for both the readout and every probe; normalised median absolute deviation and a catastrophic-outlier fraction for the behavioural gap; Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests for the population comparisons; joint logistic regression with Wald intervals and an explicit interaction test to show that two explanatory properties act independently rather than as one mechanism; sufficiency and necessity fractions under activation patching with the contrast normalised by the clean-corrupted difference; head ablation effects with Holm correction over seventy-two tests (twelve heads × six evaluation sets) and two retrained readouts on a different data partition as a robustness check; mean ablation as an alternative to zeroing, to rule out the zero reference itself driving the effect; a shuffled-label eraser as the control for direction erasure; and a matched-redshift patching design in which a head's output is swapped between galaxies of opposite morphology at nearly identical redshift, with a regression slope as the statistic. The competitor methods are the two obvious alternatives — probe ranking and zero-ablation ranking — and the paper's whole point is that they disagree.

## What they found

The multimodal gain is real and moderate: R² 0.869 from photometry alone against 0.931 with images, with scatter falling from 0.0225 to 0.0150 and catastrophic outliers from 0.32% to 0.18%. The galaxies that images rescue are faint within the small spectroscopic aperture and heavily dust-attenuated, and the two contributions are nearly additive: a joint logistic fit gives both positive coefficients with intervals excluding zero, an explicit interaction term is consistent with zero, and the accuracy gain rises from about 2% when neither property is high to about 8% when both are.

Depth-wise, the gain is almost entirely block 1: only block 1 is on its own both sufficient (restoring essentially the whole clean-versus-corrupted contrast) and necessary (removing almost the whole contrast), while every later block removes at most a few percent, and no later block restores the multimodal advantage even when it restores part of the contrast. Decomposing inside block 1 shows the redshift gain is mostly in the attention sub-layer.

Inside block 1 the two rankings invert. Head 1 has the best probe on seven of eight targets — spatial extent at R² 0.767 against 0.643 for the next head — and its geometry is exactly what a spatial-extent computation would look like: one output direction explains 84% of the variance across a galaxy's image tokens and correlates with distance from image centre at 0.739. But zeroing it changes the prediction by under 0.002 R² in every morphology stratum, and the matched-redshift patch slope is essentially zero. Spatial extent is nonetheless causally used — substituting a compact template or an extended blur drops R² to 0.556 and 0.766, most severely where the substitution contradicts the galaxy's true extent, a pattern an off-distribution artefact would not produce. The resolution is redundancy: every other block-1 head carries some extent information on its own, a probe refit without Head 1 is as good as Head 1 alone, and erasing extent's linear direction leaves it decodable again one attention sub-layer later — while still costing downstream redshift accuracy more than a shuffled-label eraser does, which says the recomputed version is not a complete substitute.

The dust side is the mirror image. Head 3 ranks last of twelve on spatial extent and middle on redshift, yet zeroing it costs about 0.052 R² overall — an order of magnitude more than any other block-1 head — and about 0.108 in the most dust-attenuated quintile, more than eleven times the next head. Keeping Head 3 as the only active head in block 1 while mean-ablating the other eleven recovers 96% of the multimodal advantage overall and essentially all of it in the dustiest quintile. Ablating it also makes the model's error *worse than photometry alone* in a specific way: the regression slope of predicted on true redshift, which images raise from 0.806 to 0.867, falls to 0.710, i.e. below the photometry value, while the scatter stays slightly better. Two ablations give two magnitudes — mean ablation costs more than zeroing — so the paper reports the smaller of its own estimates.

Then the identification fails. Its dust-information probe is weak, erasing that dust information costs nothing relative to a shuffled control, its attention to the photometry tokens does not shift with redshift as the classic spectral-break explanation predicts, its output tracks neither local colour nor brightness structure inside the galaxy, its effect *shrinks* rather than grows with photometry–image disagreement, the tokens covering the galaxy's light carry almost none of its effect while ablating central and outer subsets separately sums to less than half the joint effect, and concatenating its output onto the photometry representation closes only about a tenth of the probe gap between the two representations. A screen over all 144 heads finds nothing remotely comparable in size, and neither single-edge path cutting nor freezing the candidate mediators attributes more than a few percent of its effect downstream. What the head computes is left open, in both senses: either it computes something no enumerated astronomy operation matches, or the methods cannot express what it does.

## Limits and open problems they name

One model, one task, one survey — deliberately, and the authors argue that anchoring to a task is what makes any of this tractable, while conceding scale and cross-survey behaviour are untested. The readouts are early-stopped on the test set used for reporting, which they flag and then address by retraining with a held-out validation split on two different partitions, getting one- to three-thousandths changes and the same head ranking. The concept-erasure guarantee holds for the mean-pooled representation, not for individual token activations. The circuit search covers attention heads only, so mediation through MLPs, through many weak edges jointly, or directly through the residual stream is not excluded. The candidate-operation tests are limited to operations the authors could enumerate, which makes "we found nothing" ambiguous between a model limitation and an analyst limitation — stated as the paper's central open problem. Shuffled-label concept erasure makes the model *inactive* on that property rather than merely blind to its direction, which they note rather than fix. And the two headline dissociations are each demonstrated on one property, so how general the pattern is remains a conjecture.

## What we take from it

A **measure** and a **warning**, both directly usable. The measure: rank components two ways and print both rankings, because decodability ranking and causal-effect ranking are different instruments and here they invert — the best probe sits seventh of twelve by ablation and the biggest ablation effect sits last on one probe target. A predictive white-box model that localises by probe quality alone will confidently point at the wrong head. The warning: a null ablation is not evidence that a property is unused. Spatial extent is demonstrably used by the model and yet survives ablation of every single head that encodes it, so "we removed X and nothing happened" means "X is redundant", not "X is irrelevant" — which means any ablation ledger we build needs a redundancy census beside it (leave-one-out probes, and a per-head probe showing what survives), and any claim of necessity needs the sufficiency arm to go with it.

Three cheaper transfers. First, the **input-side counterfactual with matched observables**: build the counterfactual so that the intended variable changes and the obvious confound does not — here the substituted galaxy image is flux-matched inside the codec's crop, and the check is that an artefact would hit all strata equally, which the stratified curve rules out. That design transfers directly to any perturbation of a measured input. Second, the **matched-intervention patch**: swap an activation between two examples that agree on the confound (here, nearly the same true redshift) and differ on the variable of interest, then regress the output shift on the output gap; a slope near zero is a much stronger null than a zero-ablation, because the substituted value is one the model actually sees. The paper's own sanity check for this — patching an example with its own activation must reproduce its prediction exactly — is one line and should be standard. Third, the **normalisation warning**: their patching fractions are normalised by the clean-versus-corrupted contrast but the substantive claim is about the much smaller multimodal advantage, and the paper shows that the same per-block numbers read as ≤3.6% of one and up to 62% of the other. A predictive model's "how much of the effect does this component carry" number is meaningless without the denominator stated, and looking small under one denominator and large under another is precisely how a component gets dismissed.

Finally, the **decomposition of error they use to characterise what a component does**: fitting predicted against true and splitting the variance into a slope-shortfall term and a residual-scatter term turns "this head matters" into "this head fixes the compression of predictions toward the mean, and does not fix the scatter". For us that is the shape any predictive claim about an intervention should take — not a single delta in an aggregate score, but which named error mode moved and by how much. And the paper's negative result is itself a caution for the folder: an audit that enumerates candidate explanations and rejects them all has produced a fact about the enumeration as much as about the model, so a white-box account needs to generate explanations rather than score a fixed menu.
