# Are LLM Uncertainty and Correctness Encoded by the Same Features? A Functional Dissociation via Sparse Autoencoders

- **Key:** arxiv-2604.19974
- **Authors / organisation:** Het Patel, Tiejin Chen, Hua Wei, Evangelos E. Papalexakis, Jia Chen; University of California, Riverside and Arizona State University
- **Date:** 2026-04-21
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.19974
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

Uncertainty quantification is normally graded on one criterion: whether the score separates correct answers from incorrect ones, with AUROC or AUPRC. That grading assumes confidence and correctness are two readings of the same internal quantity. The authors point out that models are routinely uncertain while correct and confident while wrong, so a strong AUROC is compatible with the score tracking something other than the cause of the error. Their question is narrower and more mechanical than the UQ literature's: are the sparse-autoencoder features that carry "this answer is uncertain" the same features that carry "this answer is wrong"? The larger goal is to show that a single-axis evaluation can conflate two internal phenomena, and that the interpretability toolkit can separate them where output-level methods cannot.

## What they studied

Two instruction-tuned models of comparable scale, Llama-3.1-8B (32 layers, Llama Scope SAEs, 4,096-dimensional residual stream into 32,768 latents) and Gemma-2-9B (42 layers, Gemma Scope SAEs, 3,584 into 16,384), with one pretrained SAE per residual-stream layer. Data is multiple-choice only, because correctness is binary and uncertainty is read directly off the four answer-token probabilities. The unit of analysis is the individual SAE latent at a single layer, encoded at the final-token position; the target quantities are the model's output entropy and its top-answer correctness.

## How they did it

Each question is run forward and tagged on two axes: correct or incorrect, and confident or uncertain, where confidence is an entropy threshold at the 25th and 75th percentiles of the entropy distribution and the middle band is dropped so the contrast is tail-versus-tail. The four quadrants give two controlled comparisons: uncertain-correct against confident-correct varies confidence with correctness fixed, and confident-incorrect against confident-correct varies correctness with confidence fixed. For every latent the authors run a Mann-Whitney U test on each comparison plus Cohen's d, keeping only features with a positive effect size at p < 0.05, and sort them into pure-uncertainty (first comparison only), pure-incorrectness (second only) and confounded (both), with the confounded effect size taken as the smaller of the two. Discovery uses one half of the MMLU test split (14,042 questions split into roughly 7,021 each); screening and every reported number use the untouched other half. Intervention is encode-modify-decode at the chosen layer: the residual stream at the last token goes through the SAE, the targeted latents are zeroed, the modified vector is decoded, and the difference between modified and original reconstruction is added back so non-target features are untouched. A reconstruction-only pass with nothing suppressed is run as the error control. Features are first suppressed one at a time (up to the top five per layer per category by effect size) and kept only if accuracy does not fall while entropy does; the survivors are then suppressed together on the held-out set. Generalisation is tested by applying MMLU-selected features to ARC-Challenge and RACE with no re-selection, and by repeating the whole pipeline on Gemma.

## What counts as an explanation here

A three-way partition of the SAE dictionary with a functional label attached to each part: a latent is "essential", "inert" or "actionable" because of what happens to the model's behaviour when it is clamped off, not because of what it correlates with. On top of that sits a predictive object - a per-layer logistic regression on one category's activations that outputs a correctness probability, small enough (three features) to be inspected. The explanation is deliberately partial: the authors say "pure" means significant on one axis among the two tested, not exclusive of other concepts, and they cannot say what an inert feature is doing in other tasks.

## How they judged success

Classification statistics (Mann-Whitney p, Cohen's d, category counts per layer, count retention under a looser median split); behaviour under suppression measured as accuracy and mean output entropy on the held-out validation half; a random-feature control matched in features-per-layer and passed through the identical screening filter, drawn from active latents only; a selection-criterion ablation contrasting accuracy-plus-entropy, accuracy-only and entropy-only screening; cross-dataset transfer deltas with no re-selection; per-layer AUROC for correctness prediction, with output entropy as the reference predictor; and an accuracy-coverage table for abstention, compared against the model's own fifth-option self-abstention row. No significance test on the accuracy deltas themselves is printed, and the authors concede in their limitations that thousands of features are tested at p < 0.05 without a multiple-comparison correction.

## What they found

Counts follow a consistent ordering in both models: pure-uncertainty features are the most numerous (700 in Llama, 7,766 in Gemma), pure-incorrectness next (255, 3,778), confounded smallest (102, 934). The effect sizes do not follow the counts. Pure-uncertainty features reach d above 6, while the other two categories stay below 0.8 - a huge gap in how cleanly the uncertainty axis splits its groups, and one that predicts which population will turn out to be load-bearing: suppressing pure-uncertainty features collapses accuracy, with the worst single latent costing 10.21% at layer 31 in Llama and the worst Gemma drop at 8.70% in the late layers. Pure-incorrectness features do nothing: of 137 tested in Llama, 68% produce no measurable accuracy change, with a mean delta of -0.03% and mean entropy change of +0.001; of 195 in Gemma, 42% are flat, mean accuracy delta -0.01% and entropy +0.0003. Confounded features are the only actionable population. On the held-out set, suppressing 55 screened confounded latents in Llama (39 in Gemma) raises accuracy from 61.91% to 63.01% and cuts mean entropy from 0.824 to 0.205, while the matched random control moves accuracy only from 61.91% to 61.96% and entropy from 0.824 to 0.818; Gemma goes from 67.65% to 67.85% against a random 67.63%. The selection ablation is the sharpest warning in the paper: choosing confounded features by entropy reduction alone admits 86 features and costs 10.78% accuracy, so the accuracy gate is what does the work, and a filter that optimises the outcome you can see cheaply can move the outcome you care about the wrong way. Transfer without re-selection is positive in all six cells (Llama +0.81% MMLU to ARC, +0.70% MMLU to RACE, +0.75% RACE to MMLU; Gemma +0.23%, +0.63%, +0.16%), so the confounded population is at least partly dataset-general rather than MMLU-specific. The predictive result is the one we care about most: per-layer logistic regressions on confounded activations peak at AUROC 0.749 at layer 16, and a regression on just three confounded features from layer 18 reaches 0.787, against 0.805 for output entropy - which needs a full forward pass and the answer distribution to compute. Pure-incorrectness features stay near chance as predictors despite their significance, so correlation with error and prediction of error come apart in the same dictionary. Turning the three-feature score into abstention, the accuracy-coverage frontier runs 89.6% coverage at 65.98%, 76.8% at 70.96%, 64.4% at 75.90%, 52.8% at 81.29% and 42.8% at 86.55%, against the model's own fifth-option abstention at 91.2% coverage and 63.67% - the internal score dominates self-abstention at every matched coverage.

## Limits and open problems they name

Multiple-choice only, with an explicit concession that features inert here may participate in chain-of-thought or open-ended generation; two models in the 8-9B band, so nothing about scale; the partition is contingent on the specific published SAEs, and alternative dictionary sizes or sparsity penalties are untested; zeroing is one intervention among several, and patching, steering or gradient methods could reveal relations clamping cannot; no multiple-comparison correction across the feature tests; the confidence axis needs a discrete entropy threshold, which they probe but which is still a knob. The paper's own robustness check is also a quiet problem for the inertness claim: under a median split only 7.5% of Llama's pure-incorrectness features and 16.4% of Gemma's keep their category, against 90.2% and 63.1% for confounded, so the pure-incorrectness population is the least stable of the three and moving between thresholds mostly migrates those features into confounded rather than erasing them.

## What we take from it

A **measurement protocol and a warning**, both cheap to copy. The protocol: when our white-box model nominates a set of units as encoding some property, the test is a crossed 2x2 that holds one variable fixed while moving the other, because the population that is significant on both axes behaves differently from the population significant on one, and a single-axis screen cannot tell them apart. If we stratify by two output quantities - correctness and a confidence proxy - we should expect three populations and should report the count, the matched random control, and the joint screen, with the entropy-only failure (86 features, -10.78%) as the permanent reminder that screening on a proxy can move the target the wrong way. The second thing to take is the small predictive result: three latents from one layer, chosen by significance on both axes and screened for safety, reach an AUROC within a hair of a predictor built from the full output distribution, and their abstention frontier beats the model's own abstention at every coverage. That is a concrete existence proof for our central claim - a predictive white-box state read off a handful of internal units - and it comes with the coverage-accuracy curve printed rather than a single number. The warning is about inertness: significance measured by rank test says nothing about whether clamping a unit changes behaviour, so our nominations should carry a suppression (or better, an optimised-ablation) delta and not a p-value alone, and a unit that is statistically associated with an error should not be described as carrying it. Finally the depth gradient - effects negligible in the first third of layers, strongest in the last third, in both architectures after depth normalisation - suggests that whatever internal state we build a predictive model on should be read late, and that mid-network is a reasonable compromise only if the readout is trained per layer as these are.
