# A Single Neuron Is Sufficient to Bypass Safety Alignment in Large Language Models

- **Key:** arxiv-2605.08513
- **Authors / organisation:** Hamid Kazemi, Atoosa Chegini (equal contribution; Chegini's present affiliation University of Maryland), Maria Safi; Apple (Chegini's work done during an Apple internship)
- **Date:** 2026-05-08 (v1)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.08513
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

The standard story about safety alignment is that it is spread across the weights: the whole network is retrained to recognise and decline harmful requests, so no single component should be decisive and small local perturbations should be survivable. Arditi et al. had already undercut one half of that story with a single residual-stream direction whose ablation at every layer removes refusal, but that object is global — one vector applied everywhere — rather than a component. The authors ask whether the bottleneck can be pushed to the finest granularity a transformer offers: one scalar MLP coordinate, in one layer, at one site. Their larger goal is a structural account of where safety actually lives, and, as a paired question, whether harmful *knowledge* (not just the refusal gate) is similarly concentrated. The two findings are offered as a two-system picture: a gate of refusal neurons and a substrate of concept neurons.

## What they studied

Seven instruction-tuned models across two families and five sizes: Qwen3 at 1.7B, 4B, 8B, 14B and 32B (thinking mode disabled), plus Llama-3.1-8B and Llama-3.1-70B. Base checkpoints for four Qwen3 sizes (1.7B through 14B) are used for a pre-alignment comparison. The unit of analysis is the **neuron**: a single coordinate of the pre-down-projection MLP intermediate activation, written as the elementwise product of the gated and up-projected values, which is the post-gating value that multiplies one row of the down-projection. They note this basis is privileged — the gating nonlinearity makes individual coordinates meaningful in a way residual-stream coordinates are not — and they test the residual stream directly as a contrast. Data: 128 harmful and 128 harmless training prompts drawn from the standard refusal-direction datasets with Alpaca for harmless, 191 HarmBench behaviours as validation after removing overlaps with the test set, and the 100 JailbreakBench behaviours as a held-out test set never used for tuning.

## How they did it

Selection is a gradient-times-activation score computed at post-instruction tokens only. They hook the MLP intermediate at every monitored layer, define a refusal log-odds loss over a small set of model-specific refusal phrases, and backpropagate it to the neuron activations. For each neuron at each post-instruction token they take the mean signed gradient over harmful prompts plus the mean signed gradient over harmless prompts, and multiply that combined gradient by the harmful-minus-harmless mean activation difference. Neurons are filtered to those that activate more strongly on harmful prompts, and the best token for each neuron is chosen by score. The intuition they state is that a refusal neuron should both fire almost exclusively on harmful inputs and have a gradient opposed to that firing, so that moving it toward the harmless value lowers refusal. Discovery is a single forward-backward pass per prompt across the first two-thirds of layers.

The top five candidates by score are then reranked empirically on the 191-prompt validation set, because the authors observed that the two rankings diverge: the score is a local gradient signal, while attack success is a property of full generation. For each candidate they sweep a constant value pinned onto the neuron, in the direction opposite its harmful activation, and keep the candidate and multiplier with the highest validation attack success. Two intervention forms result. The *constant* intervention pins the neuron to a fixed value `m` at every token position, during prefill and during generation. The *anchor* intervention first reads the neuron's natural activation on the prompt in a hook-free pass, takes the max or min over the post-instruction tokens depending on the sign of the harmful gap, and then clamps a formula that scales the multiplier by how strongly *this* prompt activates the neuron, so a prompt that barely fires the neuron receives a small offset rather than the full pin. The chosen triple is evaluated once on JailbreakBench with no further tuning, and attack success is scored by an LLM judge requiring both non-refusal and coherence, with Llama-Guard-3-8B as a second verifier; capability is measured by MMLU next-token choice and GSM8K greedy generation against an unmodified baseline in the same harness.

For the concept side, they scan corpus examples that maximally activate individual neurons and find one self-harm-associated neuron per model in three Qwen3 sizes, then *add* a constant to it at every position and ask whether twenty innocuous prompts now produce the concept, judged on three separate criteria: mentions the target, coherent, and still relevant to the original prompt.

## What counts as an explanation here

A single signed scalar coordinate plus a layer index, and for each neuron an intervention recipe — a constant pin or an anchor formula — with one multiplier. The explanation is deliberately minimal and is *not* a feature dictionary, a circuit or a direction: the authors' point is that the unit the safety gate is written in is smaller than any of those. It is meant to be sufficient rather than complete: they do not claim the neuron is the only gate, and in fact report a second and third independently sufficient neuron in one model. It can predict, in a narrow sense — the sign and magnitude of the neuron's activation predicts whether a prompt will be refused (see the detection result) — and pinning it predicts that refusal will be removed. They additionally supply a token-level reading: per-token activation patterns show whether the neuron fires on the harmful content tokens or only at the assistant boundary, which a global direction cannot show.

## How they judged success

Attack success rate under two independent judges (LLM judge and Llama-Guard-3-8B) on HarmBench-191 for development and on the disjoint 100-behaviour JailbreakBench for held-out evaluation, with multipliers fixed before the held-out run. The direct comparison is the full refusal-direction ablation of Arditi et al. run on the identical train, validation and held-out sets. Capability cost is MMLU and GSM8K absolute change from an unmodified baseline under the same tokenizer and decoding. Detection is scored on XSTest — 450 prompts, 200 harmful and 250 safe, including adversarially constructed safe prompts — with AUROC, accuracy, F1, precision and recall compared against Llama-Guard-3-8B. Weight geometry is tested against a random-directions null: they decompose the refusal direction onto the down-projection columns, take the top cosine, and compute a Bonferroni union-bound p-value against the expected maximum cosine of uniformly random unit rows, whose closed form they give. Concept amplification is judged on three criteria separately (mention, coherence, prompt relevance) and reported as cumulative counts across a sweep of intervention strengths.

## What they found

Averaged over the seven models on held-out JailbreakBench, the constant intervention reaches 91.9% attack success under Llama-Guard and 91.7% under the LLM judge, the anchor variant 90.1% and 90.4%, against Arditi's every-layer direction ablation at 91.6% and 93.1% — so a single scalar at a single site matches an intervention that touches the whole network. The cost profile separates the two variants sharply: the constant pin loses 8.8 points of MMLU and 1.2 of GSM8K on average, with Llama-3.1-70B the worst at minus 18.2 MMLU, while the anchor variant loses 0.6 and 0.1, comparable to Arditi's 0.3 and 0.5. Validation and held-out numbers are within about a point, which they read as evidence the multipliers transfer.

The refusal neurons are also good detectors and bad ones in an informative way. On XSTest, the Llama-3.1-8B neuron at layer 11 of 32 matches Llama-Guard-3-8B on accuracy at 90.2% and on AUROC at 0.969 versus 0.975, and beats it on recall (0.950 against 0.834) at a precision cost (0.848 against 0.949) — one scalar against a dedicated 8-billion-parameter classifier. All other models sit above 0.9 AUROC except Qwen3-1.7B at 0.853.

On provenance: the same neurons are already discriminative in base checkpoints, before any alignment training. What changes between base and instruct is *when* they fire — at harmful content tokens inside the prompt in base models, at the assistant-turn boundary in instruct models — which the authors read as alignment rewiring the trigger rather than installing the content. On geometry: the top-cosine neuron against the refusal direction beats the random null in all seven models with union-bound p-values from 3e-8 to 1e-47, and in two models (Qwen3-1.7B and Llama-3.1-70B) two entirely independent identification procedures — gradient-times-activation and pure weight geometry — land on the *same* neuron.

The residual-stream contrast is the sharpest negative: applying the identical ranking to residual-stream coordinates yields at most 45% attack success on Llama-3.1-8B and 39% on Qwen3-8B at the best constant, and the authors show why — the activation distributions of the best residual coordinates overlap almost completely between harmful and harmless prompts, whereas the MLP neurons separate them cleanly. Corpus profiling shows the harmful pole dominated by explicit sexual and regulated content, and, where a safe pole exists, by meta-language about restriction — warnings, disclaimers, prosecutions — so the neuron appears to separate content that gets restricted from talk about restricting it. Concept neurons replicate the pattern: adding one suicide-associated neuron at increasing strength takes the model from no mentions to nearly all twenty benign prompts producing the concept, with the majority still coherent and prompt-relevant.

## Limits and open problems they name

Concept neurons are a proof of concept on one concept in three models; a survey across harmful concepts and families is left open. The multiplier is chosen by empirical sweep, and they say principled selection remains open. They report that the score does not rank the best attacker first, which is why the empirical rerank exists — the selector is not yet a predictor. They are explicit that a neuron may respond to several concepts through superposition, so the corpus profile is a correlation reading rather than a clean semantics. They do not claim the gate is a single neuron in general: Qwen3-14B has two or three independently sufficient ones. And their broad-impact note concedes the method is dual-use, mitigated only by requiring white-box access.

## What we take from it

Three transferable things. First, a **basis argument with a measured contrast**: the same selection procedure run on a privileged basis (MLP post-activations) and a rotation-invariant one (residual stream) gives 90-plus versus 45/39 attack success, so our model should test every candidate site in at least two bases and report them side by side rather than assuming a site is the right granularity. Second, **a convergence test across independent identification routes**: gradient-times-activation and cosine-to-a-known-direction agree exactly in two of seven models and disagree in five, which is the honest way to report whether a selected unit is real or an artefact of one method — agreement is evidence, disagreement is information, and both should be printed. Third, the **constant-versus-anchor distinction is the same lesson as dose in earlier entries**: the pinning intervention removes the behaviour but costs 8.8 MMLU, and making the intervention conditional on a per-prompt read of the neuron recovers the behaviour at 0.6 MMLU cost. Our model should therefore never publish an intervention without its context-sensitive variant beside it, because a fixed edit and an edit scaled by how strongly the unit already fires are the same efficacy with very different side-effects.
