# On the relationship between class selectivity, dimensionality, and robustness

- **Key:** arxiv-2007.04440
- **Authors / organisation:** Matthew L. Leavitt, Ari S. Morcos — Facebook AI Research, Menlo Park, California (Leavitt's contribution made under the Facebook AI Residency)
- **Date:** 2020-10-13 (v2; v1 2020-07-08; ICML 2020 Workshop on Uncertainty and Robustness in Deep Learning)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.04440
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

The sparsity-versus-distribution trade-off is one of the oldest arguments in deep learning, but it had been argued about *task* representations — how many units a class needs — and not about the sparsity of *semantic* content. Class selectivity offers a way to make the second concrete: it measures how differently a unit responds across data classes, so a network can be described as packing meaning into few sharply tuned units or spreading it across many weakly tuned ones. The prior paper in this folder had shown that concentrating meaning this way does not buy generalization and in fact slightly costs it. The question here is the natural successor: if selectivity is not bought for accuracy, is it bought for *stability* — does a sparse semantic code survive perturbation of its input better than a distributed one? The authors' own framing is that this is a causal question, answered by turning the property up and down during training and looking at what happens to the network under two very different kinds of perturbation, average-case corruption and worst-case adversarial attack. The result is that the answer depends on which perturbation you ask about, and the two answers point in opposite directions.

## What they studied

Two residual image classifiers: ResNet18 trained on Tiny ImageNet, and ResNet20 trained on CIFAR10. The unit of analysis is the **feature map at a ReLU**, treated as a single unit: a sample's activation for that unit is the mean over all elements of its map, and that value is then averaged within each class. The property on that unit is the class selectivity index inherited from the source paper — the gap between the highest class-conditional mean and the mean over the remaining classes, divided by their sum, lying in [0, 1]. Perturbations are of two families: naturalistic corruptions from the CIFAR10C and Tiny ImageNetC benchmarks, and white-box gradient attacks (FGSM and PGD). The third quantity of interest is not a property of units at all but of a *layer*: the fraction of its dimensions needed to explain 90% of the variance of the activation matrix, measured both on clean activations and on the matrix of differences between clean and perturbed activations. The last layer is excluded from regularisation and from all analyses.

## How they did it

Class selectivity is made differentiable and put in the loss: the cross-entropy has `− α · μ_SI` added to it, where `μ_SI` is the network's mean selectivity computed as a mean within each layer and then across layers — the within-then-across order being deliberate, so that the deeper layers, which have more channels, do not dominate the average. A negative scale discourages selectivity and a positive scale encourages it; the magnitude sets how strongly. Each minibatch recomputes the statistic for the training signal, while all reported selectivity values are computed once over the whole clean test set. Twenty seeds are run per hyperparameter setting, with bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals reported throughout, and every result uses the epoch that had the best clean validation accuracy. Corruption robustness is read as mean test accuracy over all corruption types and severities, and also as the ratio of corrupted to clean accuracy so that a gain in robustness cannot be a by-product of a gain in clean accuracy. Adversarial robustness is read as the accuracy-versus-epsilon curve for FGSM and the accuracy-versus-steps curve for PGD. For the dimensionality analysis, PCA is applied to the activation matrix of each layer (and to the clean-minus-perturbed difference matrix), counting components to 90% variance and dividing by the layer width; one representative perturbation is used per family — PGD at 25 steps, and brightness at severity 3 for Tiny ImageNetC / motion blur at severity 3 for CIFAR10C — a choice that matters for how far the dimensionality result generalises. The input–output Jacobian magnitude is used as the stability instrument: a network whose Jacobian is large moves far in output space for a small input change.

## What counts as an explanation here

A causal claim of the form "this property causes this vulnerability", established by a training-time intervention rather than by ablation or correlation, plus a **mechanism sketch** in the currency of linear geometry: how many dimensions the representation uses, how many dimensions a perturbation moves it along, and how large the Jacobian is. Nothing here is a feature dictionary, a circuit, or a description of what any unit means. The explanation is meant to be partial and is explicit about that: dimensionality of the change is offered as the reason selectivity trades one robustness for another, and the authors are careful to say the changes "do not consistently project onto coding-relevant dimensions", i.e. that the geometry is suggestive rather than a demonstrated mechanism. In its strongest form the claim is predictive only in a narrow sense — given a network's mean selectivity, one predicts which *direction* its robustness trade-off has moved, not its accuracy on any particular input.

## How they judged success

Metrics: corrupted test accuracy averaged over corruption types and severities, normalised corrupted/clean accuracy, per-corruption-type accuracy at a fixed severity, adversarial accuracy curves over epsilon (FGSM) and attack steps (PGD), input–output Jacobian magnitude, and fraction-of-dimensionality per layer for clean activations and for both difference matrices. Baselines and controls: unregularised networks at the same recipe are the reference point for every sweep; the normalised-accuracy measure is the control for "the effect is really a clean-accuracy effect"; twenty seeds with bootstrapped confidence intervals stand in for significance tests; and the second model/dataset pair is the replication check. The dimensionality analysis carries its own control in the comparison between corruption-induced change and adversarial-induced change on the same layers. The paper's own pre-registered framing is unusually clean and worth copying: before running the corruption sweep they write down three mutually exclusive predicted outcomes — fewer potent dimensions means selectivity *helps*, dilution means selectivity *hurts*, orthogonal directions means *no relationship* — and then report which one holds.

## What they found

Decreasing selectivity makes networks more robust to naturalistic corruption, and increasing it makes them more vulnerable: lowering it raises mean corrupted accuracy by as much as 4% on Tiny ImageNetC, and the effect survives normalisation by clean accuracy. Per corruption type, regularising against selectivity improves normalised accuracy in 12 of 15 Tiny ImageNetC types for ResNet18 and in 15 of 19 CIFAR10C types for ResNet20, and in *all* corruption types in both models when absolute accuracy is used instead. The same intervention runs the other way for adversarial attacks: lowering selectivity *increases* vulnerability to both FGSM and PGD in both models. The Jacobian explains that half — its magnitude is inversely proportional to selectivity, so distributed codes are less stable in the input–output sense. The dimensionality measurements tie the two together: clean representation dimensionality is inversely proportional to selectivity in early ResNet18 layers (up to layer 9) and across all of ResNet20; the change caused by either perturbation is higher-dimensional in low-selectivity networks; corruption-induced change is higher-dimensional than the clean representation itself in both models; and the increase in early-layer dimensionality is much larger for adversarial perturbations than for corruptions. The authors read the last point as evidence that adversarial perturbations project specifically onto coding-relevant dimensions, which is why distributed codes fall to them.

## Limits and open problems they name

The mechanism is the weakest joint: the dimensionality story is described as intuition rather than as established cause, and the paper states outright that the perturbations' changes do not consistently project onto coding-relevant dimensions, so "high-dimensional change is the reason" is an interpretation of a correlation between two measurements both taken from the same perturbed activations. The dimensionality analysis uses exactly one representative perturbation per family, and the corruption chosen differs between the two datasets, so the layer profiles are not a summary over all corruptions. The models are small by current standards and the datasets are small classics, so the effect sizes are not evidence about large-scale networks. The robustness measures are all clean-label classification accuracy on fixed benchmarks; nothing here speaks to what the networks *represent*, only to how their accuracy degrades. There is also an unremarked asymmetry that a reader should notice: the regulariser is a single global scale, so "low selectivity" means the whole network was pushed toward distributed codes, which makes it impossible to say from this paper alone whether the interesting variation is between layers, between units, or between regimes.

## What we take from it

Three reusable things. (1) **The opposite-sign result is the template for a zero-sum warning**: an intervention that improves one robustness measure can be provably hurting another, so our own evaluations of any steering, ablation or training-time knob on a probe battery must report at least one average-case and one worst-case metric side by side, and must not report a robustness gain without naming what it cost. (2) **The Jacobian-as-stability instrument** is cheap, needs no labels and no held-out probe fit, and is a legitimate second instrument to pair with a separation read-out — it measures how much an output moves per unit input change, which is exactly the kind of "prediction under intervention" quantity our own project wants, computed without any interpretability machinery. (3) **The pre-registered three-outcome design** is a discipline worth copying verbatim into our batteries: write the competing predictions and the observation that would falsify each *before* running the sweep, because "the property helps / hurts / is orthogonal" are all easy to announce after the fact. Warnings: dimensionality of a difference matrix is not a mechanism, and a per-layer profile taken on one perturbation should never be quoted as a property of the perturbation family. Coreference `arxiv-1803.06959` (the source of the selectivity index and of the ablation instrument this paper replaces with a training-time knob) and `arxiv-2003.01262` (the prior paper whose regulariser this one inherits, and whose negative result on accuracy this paper converts into a positive result on stability).
