## What they wanted, and why
The field reads residual-stream features and MLP neurons with feature-visualisation tools, but has no equivalent instrument for attention heads, which are harder units: each head is high-rank, contains two separate circuits (query–key and output–value), and is defined over a whole context window rather than a single token. The authors build that instrument and use it to answer a question their own earlier work raised: when a head has been characterised on a narrow task, what is it actually doing across the full data distribution? The larger goal is to make attention decomposable the way MLPs are, and the paper's argument is methodological — before proposing a decomposition method you need concrete examples of what the units would have to handle. That is exactly our problem in inverted form: we want units whose behaviour can be predicted, and this is a catalogue of the ways a plausible unit escapes a tidy description.

## What they studied
Claude Haiku 3.5 attention heads (all layers browsed; four heads examined closely), with secondary demos on all heads of Gemma 3 1B. The unit of analysis is the individual attention head, but the analysis is conducted at three levels at once: the token-level attention pattern, the head's Q/K/O activations at high-attention pairs, and — through attribution — the *features* those activations read and write, taken from the same 10-million-feature weakly causal crosscoder used in the lab's earlier head work. So a "head" here is really a small circuit between feature sets, and the paper's case studies are about how often that circuit collapses to a readable rule.

## How they did it
HeadVis has three parts: a frontend for browsing heads, an offline script that precomputes per-head metrics and attention patterns over a dataset, and a server for real-time queries (QK/OV attributions, arbitrary sequences, ranking heads by attention on a chosen token pair). The frontend opens on a scatter plot of heads whose axes and colour can be bound to any per-head metric, which is how they hunt for heads worth reading. For a chosen head they bucket dataset examples by maximum attention value and read the attention pattern token by token; a PCA of Q, K and O activations on high-output-norm pairs is used as a cheap cluster check on whether what looks like three behaviours is one behaviour to the head; QK attribution writes an attention score as a sum of (query-feature, key-feature) interactions and OV attribution writes the head's output as a sum of (value-feature, output-feature) interactions, both computed live for any pair; virtual weights — projecting one feature through the head's key (or query) weights and asking which features land nearby — are used to ask whether superficially different cases share a template. Behavioural claims are then tested on controlled inputs: sentences composed by hand, and generated sets of 185 sentences of the form "these five cities are all in {country}" with four cities in-country and one foreign. One composition is traced by finding, for a given (query, key) pair, which earlier head attends there, then checking whether that head's OV circuit writes the features the later head's key side reads.

## What counts as an explanation here
A verbal, per-head behaviour description backed by a feature-level circuit: for each head the authors state what it attends from and to, name the top QK and OV feature interactions, and draw the resulting three- or four-node diagram. Nothing is claimed complete. The object is explicitly a hypothesis with a stated verification route — a behaviour description that could be checked against every dataset example, ideally by grading model — and the authors say outright that where they lack such a description they cannot distinguish "one behaviour with many instances" from "many behaviours sharing a template". It is predictive only in the narrow sense: the QK geometry of the same-set suppression head predicts its behaviour on the controlled sentences, and correctly predicts what a single-token substitution will do.

## How they judged success
No aggregate benchmark, no head-level accuracy metric, no held-out protocol — success is a demonstration that the workflow yields hypotheses that survive a targeted test, plus coverage of the cases. The quantitative evidence is local and specific: the induction-score proxy (average attention on literal copying pairs) used to find induction heads; the 98% figure on generated country/city sentences; mean-subtracted cosine similarity between country query vectors and city key vectors (mean −0.47 for a country's own cities, +0.01 for others, against a residual-stream baseline of +0.17 for country-vs-own-city, i.e. the head applies a sign flip); the single substitution that drops attention from 0.87 to 0.08 against a median of 0.27 across 51 other African countries; the 1000–1986 sweep limit on the years behaviour; the clustering-or-not of PCA on three activation types. Controls are of the "does the simple story hold everywhere" kind rather than matched-random baselines: scanning all dataset examples for previously unclassified behaviour, and checking the opposite head (line width) for contrast in the virtual-weight test.

## What they found
- **Induction is only literal in early layers.** Induction heads exist at every depth, and the same proxy that finds the literal copy heads stops tracking the later ones; the fuzzy head they dissect matches on an "-ea sound at line end" query feature against a "newline after an 'ee' sound" key feature, then copies with exactly the literal-induction OV circuit. The fuzziness is entirely on the query–key side.
- **A "line width" head is genuinely polysemantic.** An early-layer head computes characters since the last newline, but also links the two halves of tokenised years (1 + 776) and the two halves of tokenised long words (interpret + ability). All three behaviours separate in PCA of Q, K and O activations, and the authors count very few dataset examples they cannot classify into one of the three. Their honesty here is the useful part: knowing the behaviour is not characterising it — a year sweep shows the head attends strongly only across 1000–1986, a constraint that neither the attention patterns nor the per-example attributions surfaced.
- **"Answer selection" is a general behaviour, not a multiple-choice one.** On the full distribution none of the top examples are multiple-choice questions; the Q, K and O activations form one continuous cloud with no clusters; the OV circuit always copies the content stored at the attended token, and the complexity is all in QK, which matches "content the model marked as relevant" on the key side and "about to produce content matching that mark" on the query side. Virtual weights show the multiple-choice "correct answer" feature lands near features that are not about quizzes at all but are other instances of relevance-marking.
- **A head that suppresses attention between items that belong together.** Top query and key tokens are dominated by places. On generated sentences, the country token attends to the foreign city more than to any of its own 98% of the time. The mechanism is geometric: after mean-subtraction, country queries are anti-aligned with their own cities' keys and near-zero with others. Applied back to dataset examples the picture is *not* clean — many place examples are not of this shape — and the one clean example turns out to be K-composition: a country that does not fit the sequence's topic attends from a token that carries a feature an earlier head wrote there, which is why token-level reading of that head fails.
- **Attention needs units it does not currently have.** The discussion is an agenda rather than a result: MLP layers can be decomposed with transcoders into rank-1 units that reconstruct the layer, and there is no corresponding object for attention. Four obstacles are named — we do not know what attentional features should look like; a head can be polysemantic **without** superposition (its output is high-rank, so it can write unrelated behaviours to distinguishable directions, which a single-direction MLP neuron cannot); attention superposition has toy-model evidence but no confirmed real example; and some attentional features are plausibly irreducible high-rank (a head's pre-softmax score is a bilinear form of rank equal to the head dimension), which is beyond the field's experience of interpreting rank-1 objects. They note that existing decompositions (attention-output SAEs, multi-token transcoders, low-rank sparse attention) all use a rank-1 OV basis, which splits genuinely higher-rank behaviours such as succession or copying induction across many learned features, and recommend testing those methods on early-layer heads that HeadVis already makes legible.

## Limits and open problems they name
The tool does not do many of the things that would close the loop: custom-prompt input, logit lens on a head's output, attention-pattern similarity search between heads, and head-composition heatmaps are all withheld from the open-source release (the authors say they found these useful internally and are not shipping them). Attribution is per-example and cannot yield a rule that predicts the circuit on arbitrary inputs — the 1000–1986 finding is their own example of a constraint that all their instruments missed. The answer selection head is only "best guess monosemantic"; the authors concede they have surveyed a handful of examples, not the head, and that without a text description there is no way to grade coverage. They also flag that the whole study looks at single attention edges, so any logic implemented by competition among keys — "attend to A if present, otherwise B" — is invisible to the method, and that long-context heads are unstudied. The polysemanticity of the line width head is not attributed to a cause: they do not know whether it is superposition or just high-rank writing.

## What we take from it
- **A prediction target to add, and a discipline for auditing it.** Any white-box model of ours that predicts what a component does has to be scored on a *distribution*, not on the narrow task it was identified on — and the failure this page documents is specific and diagnosable: a per-head (or per-feature) description built from one task silently becomes a description of that task. Our evaluation set should therefore include a "full-distribution" arm where the same unit's description is graded against examples drawn without regard to the identification task.
- **PCA as a hypothesis generator, never as a test** — the authors say it plainly and explain why (separability in high-variance directions need not affect the attention score, and a head could in principle be built so PCA misleads). This is the same category as the folder's known-negative rule: it is a screen that can only add candidates.
- **The composition trap for any predictive model.** A component can appear to read a token while actually reading a feature an earlier component wrote there (their K-composition example is the cleanest one in the folder). A predictor that takes tokens as input will therefore be right on custom prompts and wrong on data — report inputs both as tokens and as the feature contents the upstream circuit supplies, and where the two disagree, say which one the prediction is stated over.
- **A unit-selection warning for our own decomposition work.** "Polysemantic without superposition" is a real possibility for attention and impossible for an MLP neuron, because a head's output is high-rank; so a dictionary of rank-1 output directions cannot in principle separate everything a head does, and a behaviour that gets split across many dictionary entries may be one high-rank feature rather than a dictionary failure. Before reporting a split, check whether it is a split of one thing.
- **The cheapest thing to steal is the screen.** A held-out, task-agnostic example stream over a component, ranked by activation, is the single most productive move in this paper — it is how three of the four case studies were found — and it costs one precompute pass.
