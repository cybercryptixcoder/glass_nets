# Cross-seed explainability using Procrustes-conditioned Joint End-to-end Top-K Sparse Autoencoders

- **Key:** arxiv-2607.08499
- **Authors / organisation:** Bendegúz Váradi, Zoltán Kmetty — Centre for Social Sciences (CSS-RECENS Research Group, Budapest) and Department of Sociology, Eötvös Loránd University
- **Date:** 2026-07-09
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.08499
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (HTML, §§1–7, Tables 1–6, Figures 1–4, Declarations, Appendices 8–11)
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

Independently trained models of the same architecture and data learn feature spaces whose axes do not correspond, so a "feature" recovered from one run cannot be matched to a feature recovered from another, and a reader cannot tell a learned concept from an artefact of one initialisation. The authors want a cheap way to obtain a *single* dictionary that is shared across seeds, so that features can be called universal in a sense that has a measurement behind it. The larger goal is cross-seed and eventually cross-model explainability with a lightweight compute budget — they say explicitly that the work is meant to be affordable for a small social-science research group on one consumer GPU. Their motivating example is qualitative sociolinguistic analysis, where the researcher wants to point at a feature and show that it exists in more than one trained model.

## What they studied

Ten BERT-base-uncased models (110M parameters) fine-tuned on three English classification corpora: SST-2 (binary sentiment), the three-class Stanford Politeness dataset, and the five-class TweetEval emotion task. The unit is the SAE latent: a 6,144-element dictionary trained on layer 10 of 12 hidden states, with TopK sparsity at k = 32, per-token activations, and sentence-level mean pooling only as a robustness check in an appendix. Ten independently seeded BERTs are paired into five seed pairs (42/137, 99/7, 11/110, 25/225, 35/446), and each pair is the unit of a comparison row.

## How they did it

They train BERTs from scratch with random initialisation, then compute, before any SAE work, an orthogonal Procrustes rotation between the hidden states of the two members of a pair on 500 sequences: the rotation W is the solution of the Frobenius-norm alignment problem, obtained from the SVD of the cross-covariance of the two activation matrices, so one model's activation space is rotated into the other's coordinate frame. Then a *single* TopK SAE is trained jointly on both members with one encoder and one decoder; the second member's activations are rotated into the first's frame on the way in and unrotated on the way out, so both models pass through the same dictionary. The loss has five terms: a KL divergence between clean and SAE-hijacked logits for each model, a downstream hidden-state MSE, a local reconstruction MSE, a cross-seed MSE between the two sparse code matrices, and the TopK auxiliary dead-feature revival loss on the detached residual. Six conditions are compared: independent SAEs with raw index alignment (the null), independent SAEs matched post hoc by a Hungarian assignment on the sparse codes, that same matching plus a post-hoc Procrustes transform, joint training with no rotation and no cross-loss, joint training with rotation but cross-loss off, and the full pipeline. Everything is averaged over the five seed pairs with standard deviations, and the rotation is additionally tested against 30 random orthogonal rotations, an identity rotation, and a "mismatched" rotation computed on shuffled token correspondences.

## What counts as an explanation here

A feature dictionary shared across seeds, plus a matching: a feature is "universal" if it is active in both models on at least ten evaluation samples and its per-token activation vectors across the two models correlate at Pearson r of at least 0.70. The understanding is therefore a set of named latents with a cross-seed agreement score attached — a dictionary plus a reliability label per entry, not a causal model. It is partial by construction: the fraction of the 6,144 entries that qualify is in every table, and in several conditions it is small. Prediction is not attempted; the one predictive test is correlational (alignment quality against universal-feature count).

## How they judged success

Three headline metrics per condition and dataset: mean Pearson r over the top 10 and top 100 features by joint mean activation; the count and percentage of universal features, where the percentage is taken over features active in both models rather than over the whole dictionary; and the dead-feature percentage (dictionary elements firing on fewer than ten samples in either model). Classification accuracy of the SAE-hijacked model is reported as a guardrail, as a percentage-point change against the clean model. Controls: the index-aligned baseline, the Hungarian-matched baseline, post-hoc rotation, 30 random rotations with the smallest attainable permutation p-value stated as 1/31, identity and shuffled-correspondence rotations, and a sample-size study for the rotation budget showing a cross-split standard deviation of the rotation matrix at or below 0.011.

## What they found

Rotation plus joint training dominates on all three corpora and the rotation is the load-bearing ingredient. On Politeness, full pipeline gives top-10 r 0.764 and top-100 r 0.722 against 0.459/0.602 for joint-without-rotation, with universal share 57.9% against 44.9%; on SST-2, 0.702/0.503 against 0.156/0.220, universal 19.0% against 11.7%, and on TweetEval 0.865/0.846 against 0.737/0.723, universal 58.3% against 49.3%. The cross-loss adds a little beyond rotation (rotation-only rows are 0.738/0.696, 0.609/0.432, 0.859/0.828). Post-hoc rotation on independent dictionaries does *not* help reliably — it lowers top-10 r on two of three corpora and moves universal counts inconsistently — which is the paper's own strongest argument that the rotation works by conditioning joint training rather than as a transform. The rotation-specific control is sharp: with the cross coefficient at zero, one Politeness seed pair yields 113 universal features under the identity rotation against 212 under the true rotation, SST-2 17 against 79 and TweetEval 287 against 417, while the mismatched rotation and the best of 30 random rotations never exceed 2; and alignment quality (one minus the normalised residual error) explains 70.4% of the variance in universal count. Against the strictest baseline — independent SAEs matched by Hungarian assignment — the full pipeline wins on top-100 r and universal share on all three corpora, but the dead-feature fraction *rises* with joint training (Politeness 56.5% → 71.1%, SST-2 81.9% → 87.5%, TweetEval 73.6% → 85.7%), and hijacked-model accuracy moves by fractions of a point in either direction. The qualitative section works one feature end to end: a Politeness latent covering sentence-initial modal verbs in questions, tested by minimal edits that swap modality, flip interrogative to declarative, or move the modal, with the peak activation dropping by 11.8% for a modal swap and to zero when the mood becomes declarative.

## Limits and open problems they name

Only one model family, one layer, and one language; the layer chosen is the late one where they expect agreement to be *hardest*, which they present as a strength but which also means nothing is said about where in depth universality actually lives. They do not compare against Feature-Aligned SAEs or Orthogonal SAEs, the two closest rivals, and they flag this as important future work. The universality percentage is computed over active features, so the high dead rate of the joint pipeline inflates it relative to the dictionary; the mean-pooled appendix shows "much more universal" features and a lower dead rate purely as a consequence of the evaluation rule becoming looser, which the authors themselves attribute to mean pooling letting a feature fire if it appears anywhere in the sequence. There is no seeded replication of the SAE training itself, no confidence interval on the correlation estimates beyond the across-pair standard deviation, and no test of whether the matched features agree on *which tokens* they fire on — the definition is a correlation of activation vectors, which is closer to behavioural agreement than a direction cosine, but the matching is by Hungarian assignment on the codes, so the pairing is already chosen using the thing being scored.

## What we take from it

Three instruments worth copying, and one trap. The instrument: **the ablation that isolates the alignment transform from the alignment protocol** — here the post-hoc row and the rotation-only row, which together show that the same matrix helps only when it conditions training. The second: **the negative-control family for a rotation** — identity, shuffled-correspondence, and 30 random orthogonal matrices, with the shuffled one being structurally identical to the true one and failing completely, which rules out "any rotation spreads the codes out". The third: **an alignment-quality scalar that is regressed against the downstream universality number**, giving a 70.4% variance figure instead of an assertion. The trap: **the universal percentage is denominated over active features while the same pipeline nearly doubles the dead fraction**, so the denominator arithmetically improves as the dictionary degrades — this is the folder's recurring denominator problem in a new place, and it is why our account must print the dictionary-wide count beside the active-only percentage. It also gives a concrete instance of the "agreement on behaviour versus agreement on direction" split from run 585: correlating activation vectors over shared tokens is the behavioural axis done properly, but the pairing was chosen by matching the codes, so the matching and the measurement share information.
