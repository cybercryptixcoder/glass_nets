## What they wanted, and why
The number of linear regions a ReLU network *could* have is not the number it uses, and the gap between the two is where most of the interesting questions live. Earlier work had bounded the expected region count for random parameters; a separate empirical line had observed that in the terminal phase of training the number of regions *near the data points* drops and the network becomes more adversarially robust. None of that theory was about the data distribution - it counted regions in ambient space. The authors want a complexity measure that is a **density over the input distribution** rather than a property of the whole input space, so that "complex" means "many boundaries where the data actually is". Three questions drive it: does representation learning (their proxy: the rank of the intermediate Jacobian) reduce local boundary density; does local density relate to adversarial robustness through total variation; and does optimisation implicitly minimise it. This is the paper that makes the partition object *local*, which is the gap four recent entries in this folder had been circling - every one of them measured partition geometry in a way that was indifferent to which regions the data occupies.

## What they studied
Fully connected ReLU networks, output dimension 1, unspecified general widths/depths in the theory. The unit of analysis is a **pair**: a neuron's activation boundary and the input distribution that the boundary is averaged over. Concretely they track two statistics per model - *local complexity* LC (expected boundary density per unit volume near a data point, averaged over the data distribution) and *local rank* LR (the expected approximate rank of the layer's input-Jacobian, i.e. how many input directions the layer still transmits). Empirics are small: 4-layer MLP with 200 units per layer on synthetic Gaussian-to-Gaussian regression with 100-dimensional input and 2-dimensional output; the same 4×200 MLP on a 1,000-image MNIST subset; a 3×200 MLP on MNIST; a 1-hidden-layer 5,000-unit MLP on MNIST; a 2-layer 1,000-unit MLP for the weight-decay sweep; and MLPs on CIFAR-10 and Imagenette in the appendix. No transformer, no network larger than a few million parameters.

## How they did it
The key move is a robustness trick applied at the level of the *definition*. Counting regions for fixed weights requires solving a parametric system - hard. Instead they add zero-mean Gaussian noise δ to the **biases only** (weights are left clean, because bias noise translates boundaries rather than rotating them, and Appendix B.1 shows weight-plus-bias noise gives qualitatively the same pictures), and define a *density* f(x) as the expected (n₀−1)-volume of the nonlinear locus inside a shrinking ball around x, divided by the ball's volume, in the limit of small radius. **Definition 1** is LC = E_{x~p}[f(x)] - the density above, averaged over the data distribution. **Theorem 2** turns that abstract object into something computable: LC is approximately the sum over neurons of E[‖∇z_i(x)‖₂ · ρ_b(z_i(x)) · 1{z_i good at x}], where "good" means the neuron lies on some active path to the output at x, and ρ_b is the Gaussian bias density. So a neuron contributes to local complexity only where it is on a live path *and* its pre-activation is near its own threshold - boundary density is a product of path-liveness with a threshold-crossing weight. **Corollary 3** sandwiches this with the raw gradient sum, so the analysis can proceed on E[‖∇z_i‖] alone with explicit Gaussian-tail error terms. From there: **Theorem 5** relates LC to the local rank (LC is bounded above by a sum of layer-wise √(C²·LR + ε²n), and conversely the summed approximate rank is bounded by LC divided by an ε² and a Gaussian factor - a two-way but loose relation through gradient-norm constants). **Theorem 7** bounds total variation below by LC minus those error terms, scaled by the network's largest Lipschitz constant - the robustness link. **Proposition 8 / 9 / Corollary 10** tie LC to representation cost and to weight decay: LC ≤ (weights' Frobenius norm)^L up to width/depth constants, so under the known rich-phase scaling of parameter norm, LC is bounded by Θ(log(1/λ)) for decay λ. Empirically they estimate LC from test-set samples by the Theorem 2 expression with σ=0.05, estimate TV as the mean input-gradient norm, and plot both against training iteration, alongside adversarial accuracy.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **complexity measure plus a chain of bounds** - not a circuit, not a dictionary, not a description of any particular computation. The explanation is that a named, computable geometric quantity (local boundary density relative to the data) is provably sandwiched by other named quantities the field already cares about (representation rank, total variation, parameter norm), so that an observed training-time phenomenon (the terminal-phase drop, the robustness gain) has a geometric candidate explanation. It is deliberately *partial*: the bounds are inequalities with constants the paper does not pin down, and the authors are explicit that they do not explain the training dynamics. Predictive content is narrow but real in one direction: given the weights of a trained ReLU MLP and the data distribution you can *compute* LC, and LC is claimed to track a measurable robustness quantity. It does not predict which region a given input lands in, or what the network computes on it.

## How they judged success
No benchmark suite, no external baseline network, no accuracy comparison. The evidence is (i) proofs of each bound, several via the co-area formula and Kac-Rice-style level-set reasoning with a change-of-variables sanity check in Appendix A.2 on a single neuron and a two-neuron network; (ii) a **qualitative validity check** that the estimated density f(x) reproduces the geometry of exactly-visualised linear regions for a 2-D input network, using a general-purpose exact-region visualiser from prior work (a "Stanford Bunny" grayscale-image regression net trained with MSE and Adam at 1e-4); (iii) an ablation on the noise scale σ (0.025 / 0.05 / 0.1) and on dropping the bias-density factor ρ_b entirely, both of which leave the picture qualitatively unchanged; (iv) Pearson correlations between LC and LR over training for six random cross-covariance matrices, with standard-deviation error bars - reported as 0.852 at layer 2, 0.957 at layer 3, 0.985 at layer 4; (v) an initialization-scale ablation showing the TV/LC correspondence holds at 2× He but **breaks down at standard He or 3× He**, and (vi) an attempted repair of that failure by estimating the bound's own Lipschitz constant (max over layers of the suffix matrix product operator norm), after which the lower bound tracks LC again across 88 repeated runs; (vii) a weight-decay sweep reporting a linear fit with R² = 0.697 between the log(1/λ)-type bounding quantity and end-of-training LC in a 2-layer 1,000-unit MLP.

## What they found
The framework holds together, and the authors' own numbers say the *bounds are not the finding* - the pictures are. On the synthetic Gaussian regression task LC and LR move together almost exactly and both fall sharply late in training; on MNIST the LC drop coincides with a rise in adversarial accuracy, and the local-rank drop coincides with the *second* of two LC drops, not the first. The weight-decay relation is the cleanest quantitative result: heavier decay produces an earlier and deeper LC drop, and the bounding quantity explains roughly 70% of the variance in final LC across runs. The bias-noise construction does what it was designed to do - the density is stable across σ and across noise placement, and it visibly agrees with exactly computed region boundaries. The interesting failures are reported rather than hidden: at ordinary initialization scale the TV/LC relation does not appear, LC occasionally *rises* late in training while TV spikes, and attributing that spike to a growing Lipschitz constant (rather than to slack in the bound) is offered as the more likely reading, with the authors calling the behaviour "unexpected" and leaving it open. In the appendix, UMAP of hidden weight vectors shows clustering emerging after the LC drop - offered as qualitative evidence that the drop is a feature-learning transition rather than a shrinkage of the function, and notably *not* quantified.

## Limits and open problems they name
Stated: ReLU only, though they expect the proof technique to transfer to other piecewise-linear activations; the training dynamics of LC are explicitly left open as a "detailed analysis ... remains an open problem"; the empirical interactions between LC, LR and TV are richer than the theory covers; and bounds on the generalisation gap in terms of LC are named as a natural next step but not attempted. Beyond what they name, and worth recording: **(a)** every bound is loose by constants that depend on quantities they do not measure at scale (the C_grad and C_bias constants, the "B" count of neurons off every layer - measured as exactly 0 for their wide MLPs, which is the only place B is controlled); **(b)** the headline empirical setting is one 4-layer 200-unit MLP on 1,000 MNIST images with a deliberately non-standard initialization chosen *because* it is the setting where the phenomenon shows - the paper is honest about this, but it means the LC/robustness/rank story is demonstrated in one favourable configuration and merely "evidence that the trend persists" on CIFAR-10 and Imagenette, where MLPs perform poorly and input dimension makes the estimate expensive; **(c)** the locally-complexity estimate drops the "good at x" indicator and uses the trivial bound, so it is an *upper* estimate of the true density; **(d)** there is no transformer, no large model, and no experiment where LC is *predicted* from weights before measuring it - the measure is computed, then plotted; **(e)** adversarial robustness is measured by accuracy against PGD, whose relevance to a low-Total-Variation argument is asserted via the fact that PGD is first-order; **(f)** the theory is for width-fixed fully connected nets, while the local rank of real features in a deep network is a quantity the folder has repeatedly found to be threshold-sensitive.

## What we take from it
Four things, one of which is a genuine upgrade of how we should compute a partition statistic. **A definition to adopt:** a measure of boundary density that is (i) averaged over the *data distribution* rather than ambient space, (ii) made robust by perturbing the *biases only* rather than the weights - a perturbation that translates boundaries without rotating them, so the object being averaged is still the same partition geometry - and (iii) reducible to a per-neuron sum of gradient norm times a threshold-crossing weight. That last factorisation is the reusable part: it says the contribution of any single unit to the network's complexity near a point can be computed for one backward pass and attributed per neuron, which is the kind of quantity a white-box model should report per unit rather than per layer. **A measure to borrow with its caveat attached:** LC is an upper estimate by construction (they drop the liveness indicator), so when we compute the analogous statistic we report both variants and the gap, exactly as they partially do in their own σ ablation. **A warning about the folder's favourite quantity:** the local rank - the same quantity runs 719, 723, 724 have been quoting as r90/effective rank - is ε-dependent by definition here (rank_ε = count of singular values above ε), the relation to LC is only two-way through constants, and the paper's own Figure 12 shows local-rank dynamics on MNIST can be *more complex* than on the synthetic task. This is an independent confirmation of the folder's standing rule that rank-at-a-threshold is a statement about a spectrum's tail, arrived at from theory rather than from an observation. **The pattern worth remembering:** the paper reports a relation that fails under an ablation (TV vs LC at standard initialization), then *diagnoses* the failure by measuring a quantity inside its own bound rather than by softening the claim - and the diagnosis is inconclusive. That is the correct handling of a bound that does not hold in the regime that matters, and it is the template we should follow when our own complexity statistics disagrees with our behavioural ones: report the disagreement, name the constant that could absorb it, estimate that constant, and say whether the estimate rescued the bound. **One framing to reject:** they never predict LC from weights before measuring it. For our goal the executable version of this paper is "given θ, compute LC and local rank, and check they predict held-out robustness/behaviour without being fit to it" - which is exactly the prediction-under-intervention test the folder has been listing as missing since run 718.
