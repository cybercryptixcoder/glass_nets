# Mechanistic Interpretability of Deep Neural Networks: Methods, Frameworks, and Perspectives

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.13868
- **Authors / organisation:** Lorenzo Cirillo, Irene Amerini, Andrea Cavallaro — Sapienza University of Rome (AlcorLab / DIAG) and EPFL
- **Date:** 2026-11-10
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13868
- **Kind:** survey/agenda
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
The authors write for a signal-processing readership (the IEEE copyright block and the reference list make that clear) where interpretability has mostly meant saliency: integrated gradients, Grad-CAM, heat maps over the input. Their pitch is that this family answers the wrong question — which input pixels mattered — when the interesting question is which internal component caused the behaviour. The article is therefore an organising survey rather than a new method: it argues that mechanistic work across images, audio and language can be sorted into four operations and then recombined into frameworks, and it closes with five open problems it thinks the field should be judged on. The stated novelty against earlier reviews is the framing — how techniques are *combined* into causal pipelines — plus deliberately choosing worked examples outside language (an audio classifier, a convolutional autoencoder) to show the methods are domain-general.

## What they studied
No new models are trained for the survey's own claims. The worked examples are a ViT-B/16 pretrained on ImageNet-1K then fine-tuned on ESC-50 spectrograms for environmental-sound classification, and a six-layer convolutional autoencoder trained on CIFAR-10 for reconstruction. Everything else is a digest of published results across vision transformers, convolutional networks, vision-language models, diffusion models, speech-recognition encoders and LoRA-adapted Whisper. The unit of analysis moves across the paper — neurons, channels, modules, layers, attention heads, edges of the computational graph — and the survey's own contribution is to make that choice explicit as a *granularity* parameter that each method fixes.

## How they did it
The taxonomy is four categories of operation over the same object (a component's activation). Representation visualisation observes without editing: activation maps take the gradient of a chosen layer's activation with respect to the input to show which input regions move that layer most, and the logit lens projects intermediate hidden states into the output space. Representation disentanglement factors dense activations into sparse ones, with SAEs and difference-of-weights task vectors as the two exemplars. Causal intervention edits the activation — zero, mean, or interchange with the value from another input — and reads the change in the output. Circuit tracking iterates intervention over the graph: automated circuit discovery deletes edges in reverse topological order whenever removal costs less than a KL-divergence threshold, and edge attribution patching replaces the per-edge forward passes with one gradient-times-difference score per edge, keeping the top k. The survey then shows how practitioners chain these: visualise to propose candidates, disentangle to isolate them, intervene to test them, track to connect the survivors into a circuit.

## What counts as an explanation here
A causal chain at a declared granularity: a set of components, plus an intervention result on each, plus the edges between them that survive ablation. The explanation is explicitly partial and local — responsible-for-this-behaviour rather than a description of the model — and its evidential weight is carried entirely by the intervention, not by the plausibility of the feature or the map. The survey is clear that a disentangled feature on its own is a *candidate* causal variable and that only the interventional test promotes it. Prediction is not on the menu anywhere: no entry in the paper is scored by whether the extracted circuit forecasts behaviour on new inputs.

## How they judged success
There is no common metric, and the survey does not propose one — this is the paper's largest omission for our purposes. Success is reported method by method, as a patch that flips a prediction, a KL threshold that yields a small circuit, a probe accuracy, or a human judgement in a two-alternative task. The one quantitative worked example of causal patching: patching a single feed-forward module moves the target class's logit from 0.002 to 7.753 and restores the base prediction, and patching any of the first three feed-forward modules is enough to do it. The one quantitative attribution-patching example keeps top-3 layers with ΔM scores of 0.00058, 0.00059 and 0.00089. Borrowed results include a linear probe reaching 85.6% word-category accuracy at the final encoder layer of an ASR model, a decoder probe predicting hallucinations at up to 93.4%, white-noise injection restoring acoustic accuracy in 85% of error cases, and a human study in which participants identified the more strongly activating image more accurately for the *unscaled* model than the scaled one.

## What they found
The survey's own findings are framings. (1) The four categories are not alternatives but a pipeline, and the paper's Figure 6 argument is that a disentangled feature which fails the intervention test should not enter the circuit — i.e. the pipeline is a filter. (2) Frameworks that skip disentanglement exist and are the more common pattern in vision-language work: visualise, then patch the candidate directly, with the corrupted-activation trick used both to measure a component and to render the concepts it carries. (3) SAEs have a structural limit the survey states plainly: the encoder's learned dictionary may simply not contain the concept the application wants to suppress, which makes unlearning and bias-mitigation pipelines fragile in a way reconstruction metrics do not reveal; training cost also scales badly enough that SAEs are fitted on one component rather than a whole network. (4) Scale is not a substitute for interpretability — this is reported from a cited human study, not measured here. (5) The efficiency gap is real and is the survey's most concrete methodological point: exhaustive interchange patching costs a forward pass per component, and the gradient approximation that fixes the cost is the one that loses accuracy, so the field is trading causal fidelity for tractability on a fixed budget.

## Limits and open problems they name
Five, in their own framing: generalisation (turn causal subgraphs into training regularisers so models stop leaning on dataset artefacts); alignment of internal representations to human concepts (disentangled signals may lack semantic grounding, so add concept supervision and human-in-the-loop validation); robustness (use causal tracing to find the components that amplify adversarial noise rather than paying for randomised smoothing of the whole network); efficiency (apply circuit discovery to weights, accepting attribution-patching's loss of accuracy); and sensitivity (a component's attributed responsibility depends on the intervention data, and circuit structure moves with the threshold τ, so results are not stable properties of the model). The sensitivity item is the one they call out as most in need of work, and it is the one a predictive white-box model cannot avoid: if the circuit changes with the probe distribution and with an arbitrary cut-off, the "explanation" is conditional on two choices the paper gives no rule for.

## What we take from it
Mostly a framing and a caution, not a method. Framing: the four-operation pipeline is a defensible skeleton for our own write-ups, and its discipline — disentangle, then intervene, and do not let a plausible feature into the circuit without a patch — is the right default. Granularity as an explicit declared parameter is a good habit we should adopt at the top of every entry. The caution is the sensitivity section: this survey, from authors with no stake in our approach, states that attributed causal responsibility depends on the intervention data and that circuit membership depends on a threshold, which is exactly the failure mode a predictive model is supposed to fix. That is the gap our work should be aimed at, and it is worth quoting the framing rather than the methods. As a catalogue of what to read it is thin — most claims are one sentence per cited paper and no numbers are tabulated — so its value to us is the argument, not the survey content.
