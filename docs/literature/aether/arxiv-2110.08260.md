# Abstract Interpretation of Fixpoint Iterators with Applications to Neural Networks

## What they wanted, and why

Standard abstract interpretation propagates a set of inputs through a program and keeps an abstraction of **all reachable states** — every intermediate iteration included — because that is what a sound analysis of a loop needs. The authors point out that for one important class of programs this is wasteful and, worse, wrong-headed: numerical solvers, and neural architectures built out of them, loop until they reach a fixpoint, and the only thing anyone cares about is the state they *stop at*. Keeping every iteration in the abstraction, which is what Kleene iteration with joins does, guarantees a result that is sound but bloated — the union of everything the loop ever produced. Their goal is an analysis that abstracts the **final states only**, and still proves a sound statement about them. The larger aim is to make a class of implicit-depth networks verifiable at all; the concrete target here is the monotone deep-equilibrium model, which they argue is the hardest instance because its latent iteration is high-dimensional and non-linear.

## What they studied

Monotone operator deep equilibrium models (monDEQs): a network whose prediction is a linear readout of the unique fixpoint of a shared iteration function, with the monotonicity parameterisation that guarantees existence and uniqueness of that fixpoint. Experiments train monDEQs on MNIST and CIFAR10 (fully connected with latents of 40, 87, 100 and 200, plus a small convolutional model with latent 648 and 800) and on the HCAS aircraft-collision-avoidance tabular policy (a 100-wide latent). The unit of analysis is the whole model's input-to-output map for a set of inputs: the object is an over-approximation of the set of fixpoints reachable from an input region, and then of the corresponding output region.

## How they did it

The construction is a two-stage abstract fixpoint iteration, implemented as a tool they call Craft, and it can be described without any of the paper's notation. Stage one: initialise the abstract state to the single concrete fixpoint of the given input point; repeatedly apply a sound abstract transformer of one solver step, with **no join and no widening and no abstract version of the termination test**; stop as soon as the next abstract state is contained in the current one. A theorem (3.1, proved in Appendix B.1) says that this containment is exactly the condition under which the state they stopped at contains every concrete fixpoint for the whole input region — the argument is that once one step maps a set into itself, no number of further concrete steps escapes it, and since the concrete iteration converges in finitely many steps the limit must be inside too. Stage two: keep applying abstract solver steps to tighten the answer, which is sound because of a second property they define and prove — *fixpoint set preservation*, that any sound abstraction of a locally Lipschitz iterator with a unique convergent fixpoint maps an over-approximation of the fixpoint set to another over-approximation of it (Theorem 3.3, with a wider parameter range proved for forward-backward splitting in Theorem 5.1). Two different splitting methods are used, one chosen for the containment search and one for the tightening. The second contribution is the abstract domain: a hybrid zonotope (Minkowski sum of a centred zonotope and a box) with the extra requirement that the zonotope's error matrix be invertible, plus a sound over-approximation operator that consolidates an arbitrary number of error terms back down to exactly as many as the latent dimension (PCA basis, Theorem 4.1). That fixed representation size is what makes containment a matrix computation rather than an intractable geometric test (Theorem 4.2), and the containment test is the thing the whole loop is waiting on. They also deliberately **expand** the abstraction, by a multiplicative and an additive constant, while searching for containment — the paper is explicit that this is not widening: widening forces an increasing chain to terminate, whereas here the iteration over non-monotonic transformers produces incomparable sets and the expansion breaks that stall.

## What counts as an explanation here

A sound over-approximation of a set — the reachable fixpoint set and the output set derived from it — plus a certificate that a postcondition holds for all of it. It is a partial account by construction: the pipeline is sound but not complete, and the paper names the four sources of imprecision (consolidation, expansion, and the two abstract transformers) and the incomplete containment check as the reason. It is predictive in a narrow, exact sense: the certificate is a claim about every input in the region, and a failure to certify is not a claim at all. Nothing in it is attributable — which neuron or feature does what is entirely outside the object of study, and the same instrument would work unchanged on a permuted network. The one place a richer object appears is a case study where they extend the analysis to a termination condition: a bound on the distance between any value that satisfies the stopping test and the true mathematical fixpoint lets them expand the certified fixpoint set into a certified set of reachable returned values (Appendix A, Theorems A.1–A.2).

## How they judged success

Three quantities: certified accuracy on the first 100 test samples at a given perturbation radius (with the empirical accuracy against a strong projected-gradient attack printed beside it as an upper bound on what any verifier could certify), the number of samples for which an abstract post-fixpoint was found at all, and the mean wall-clock time per correctly classified sample. Baselines: the state-of-the-art semidefinite-programming certificate for monDEQs, restricted by its solver to latents of at most 87, compared on the two smallest networks across five radii; and the Lipschitz-bound family, which they argue is both looser and, in one case, unaccompanied by any certificate. Ablations remove each component one at a time: the box part of the domain, the zonotope part, the choice of iterator, the slope optimisation in the rectifier transformer, the expansion, and — the informative one — a variant that requires the state used for the final certificate to also be contained in its predecessor, which tests whether the second stage's weaker guarantee is earning its place.

## What they found

The headline comparison is at the two small latents. At the smallest radius both tools certify nearly everything and Craft is roughly three to four orders of magnitude faster (about a second per sample against 401.5 and 1388.1 seconds). At the larger radii the gap widens in Craft's favour on precision as well — at radius 0.05 on the 40-wide latent, 36 certified against 18, and on the 87-wide latent 30 against 24 — at roughly two orders of magnitude lower runtime. Scalability is the other axis: Craft certifies latents of 200 and 648/800 and CIFAR10 models at radius 2/255, where the semidefinite approach cannot run at all; on the fully connected CIFAR10 model it certifies 22 of the first 100 and on the convolutional one 29, at 16.8 and 41.1 seconds per sample. A surprise they report plainly: the convolutional monDEQs are easier to certify than the fully connected ones on both datasets, giving the highest certified accuracies. The global-guarantee experiment certifies 82.8 per cent of the relevant input region of the collision-avoidance policy by exhaustive splitting, and the plot shows the expected uncertifiable band at the decision boundary plus a small uncertifiable pocket in the interior. The ablations are the most reusable numbers: removing the zonotope component leaves a box that certifies **nothing** (though it converges on every sample and takes 0.38 seconds); removing the expansion halves the samples for which a post-fixpoint can be found at all (100 to 50) and drops certification from 30 to 9; requiring the certifying state to itself be contained in its predecessor certifies **not a single sample** out of 100, which is the paper's evidence that the weaker second-stage guarantee is what makes the analysis work. On the square-root case study, at the wider input interval Kleene iteration diverges to an unbounded interval while Craft returns a tight one, and Craft needs 10 and 18 iterations where Kleene needs 30 on the narrow interval.

## Limits and open problems they name

Two, and they are the ones that matter. First, the termination test requires the abstract iteration to *contract*, and contraction is not guaranteed even with exact abstractions — and their containment test is incomplete, so it can miss a contraction that did occur. Second, the over-approximated iteration is not guaranteed to converge even where the exact one is, because errors accumulate through the incomplete transformers. Both are honest and both are the same limitations Kleene iteration has. Two further softnesses the paper does not raise: the expansion parameters and the splitting parameter were found by coarse manual search (stated in Appendix D), and the "optimise the slope iff the sample is close to certifying" heuristic in the transformer is described in terms of the outcome it buys (six additional samples for about ten seconds each) rather than as a rule fixed in advance.

## What we take from it

The framing first, because it is the general form the folder has been circling: **abstraction is defined relative to the question, and a question about endpoints licenses a cheaper abstract domain than a question about trajectories.** Standard abstract interpretation abstracts the whole run because a program might stop anywhere; here the stopping point is pinned by a mathematical property, and the property can be *used* rather than analysed — the paper never needs an abstract transformer for the termination test because the test's invariant is substituted by a theorem. That is the same move as entry 615's closure on output categories but applied at the other end of the semantics, and it delivers a concrete rule. Second, the containment test is not cosmetic: the entire loop terminates when one abstract state is inside another, so an inclusion check that is sound-but-incomplete is a load-bearing assumption, and the paper is careful to keep the outer object in a form where the check is cheap. For us that means any iterative account we build should be built around the check that stops it, not around the precision of the per-step description — the ablation that removes expansion loses half the samples, and the one that insists on containment-at-certification loses all of them. Third, the distinction between the two stages is exactly the distinction between a *sound answer* and a *useful answer*: Theorems 3.1 and 5.1 give different warranties (containment of all fixpoints, versus preservation of that containment under further steps), and the paper reports which stage each number came from. Fourth, the numbers that keep recurring in this folder recur here too — the tight representation bought precision that did not translate into decisions (entry 614's subproblem count, entry 615's invariant safe column), and here the parallel is that the box alone converges fastest of all and certifies nothing. Warning to carry: the reported improvements are all against one baseline family whose small-dimensionness is a limitation of the baseline's solver, so the "two orders of magnitude" is a comparison at the only size where the competitor can run.
