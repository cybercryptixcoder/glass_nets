# CaLA: Causal Latent Adjustment for World Models under Adaptive Policies

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.31431
- **Authors / organisation:** Robert Ness, Sofiane Ennadir, Oleg Smirnov (equal contribution); Microsoft Research and King AI Labs, Microsoft Gaming
- **Date:** 2026-11-23
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.31431
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
A world model is supposed to answer "what would happen if we did X", but the rollouts it learns from were produced by a logging policy that chose actions using information the model does not see. When that hidden information also drives the outcome, the model's ranked actions are biased: it recommends whatever the hidden factor was already correlated with, not what would cause the better result. The authors want the imagined rollout to estimate an interventional expectation rather than a conditional one, and they want a benchmark where the interventional answer is known exactly so that the various ways an estimate can be wrong (confounding, dynamics error, estimator error) can be separated rather than blamed collectively on the model. This is the folder's recurring question — a model that predicts the next state versus a model that predicts the effect of a change — moved into the setting where the change is a decision rather than a probe.

## What they studied
A purpose-built Match-3 game environment with 2,400 simulated player identities, each carrying a hidden four-dimensional skill vector (search, pattern recognition, planning horizon, execution). The unit of analysis is a *landmark* intervention: the difficulty served on attempt 20 is a continuous treatment, and the outcome is churn before attempt 21. Three level families (Orchard, Harbour, Foundry) differ in board colour count and refill; the adaptive policy assigns difficulty as a function of the hidden skill, so skill affects both the treatment and the outcome. The model class is a DAG-constrained variational world model over board states, actions, completion and churn, compared against the simulator's own structural equations used as paired ground truth.

## How they did it
The structural model defines skill as shared across a player's attempts, entering the difficulty assignment, the action policy, the behavioural proxies and the churn hazard, but never the board transition kernel — "the game responds to those actions in the same way for everyone". Difficulty is drawn from a normal whose mean rises with the player's standardised skill projection, which is what creates confounding; churn is an absorbing Bernoulli depending on a recency-weighted experience update and the signed margin, with over-challenge raising the hazard more steeply than under-challenge. Ground truth is obtained by abduction–action–prediction: for every player the authors keep the pre-intervention history and the exogenous noise fixed and re-simulate the whole attempt for each of 17 candidate difficulties on a grid from -2.00 to +2.00, giving paired counterfactuals whose differences come from the intervention. CaLA adds a recurrent encoder that reads attempts 1-19 (never attempt 20 onward) and outputs a Gaussian latent; the latent is trained by auxiliary heads that predict historical difficulty, behavioural summaries, completion and proxies, plus a KL penalty. The latent enters the propensity density, the action policy and the churn model but is excluded from the transition model by construction, and this exclusion is presented as a claim about the environment rather than a capacity limit. At test time the logging policy is dropped, the treatment is set, and the remaining factors are sampled ancestrally with the latent held fixed across candidate difficulties and posterior draws, using shared random-number streams so the comparison is paired. Three estimators are compared: a direct doubly robust estimator with a kernel-localised propensity-weighted residual correction, world-model Monte Carlo over imagined rollouts, and the same residual correction applied to rollout risks. Five adjustment arms manipulate what is available before the landmark: no history, 40 handcrafted history summaries, a deterministic point encoding, the variational latent, and the oracle skill vector. A randomized-assignment regime sets the skill-dependence coefficients to zero while preserving the marginal difficulty distribution, as a negative control.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not an explanation of the network in the interpretability sense — there is no network being read. The understanding takes the form of a *replacement model with declared causal structure*: a factorised generative model over the trajectory whose factorization is dictated by a DAG, plus a latent adjustment state whose purpose is explicitly not semantic recovery of skill but retention of common-cause information. The claim attached to it is predictive-under-intervention, and it is partial: the latent is not identified, and the authors say a deployment would need defensible proxy assumptions instead of the oracle they have here.

## How they judged success
Two metrics against the paired engine reference: weighted curve MAE (level-player-count weighted) and decision regret (ground-truth churn at the recommended difficulty minus churn at the true causal optimum), plus a pooled individual regret. The controls are the strongest part of the design: natural versus randomized assignment isolates the confounding; oracle skill as an arm bounds what any latent could buy; three training seeds with sample SDs; whole-player bootstrap resampling (2,000 joint resamples) on the per-level recommendations to expose instability; and a per-cell propensity "support" fraction, with the kernel bandwidth, propensity floor and support threshold all fixed before test evaluation. The world model is separately scored by a persistence-normalised skill metric on logged-action replay and by an energy score on autonomous imagination against engine-generated trajectories.

## What they found
The bias is real and expensive in the simulator. Observational estimates pick the hardest candidate on every level family, while the ground-truth optimum sits at -0.25 to +0.25 on Orchard, -0.75 on Harbour and +0.25 to +0.50 on Foundry; following the observational curve costs about 5.3 to 12.9 percentage points of churn, and the per-level added churn ranges are 0.053-0.069, 0.109-0.129 and 0.079-0.088. Using history helps substantially: with direct adjustment under natural assignment, the variational latent cuts pooled individual regret by roughly 62% relative to no history (0.118 to 0.045 in Table 9), and on the rollout estimator it cuts it by almost 80% (0.078 in the no-history row to 0.016 in Table 3). But the learned latent does not beat handcrafted features on every metric: under natural assignment with rollouts, handcrafted history has the lowest curve MAE (0.019, against CaLA's 0.019 with a wider SD) and the lowest pooled individual regret in the randomized regime (0.013 against CaLA's 0.014), and the authors state plainly that it does not uniformly outperform features on population metrics. Oracle skill is not a lower bound either — under natural assignment with direct adjustment its pooled individual regret (0.049) is worse than the variational latent's (0.045) and its population regret (0.011) worse than both the latent (0.007) and the deterministic encoding (0.010), because every arm still depends on estimated nuisance functions. Remarkably, the oracle latent is *degraded* by the residual correction: with WM-DR under natural assignment, oracle skill's population regret is 0.011 against the deterministic encoding's 0.005 and CaLA's 0.007. The correction itself is a failure: applying it to rollouts raises curve MAE by about 19% and population regret by about 238% under natural assignment, and degrades estimates under randomized assignment for every arm including the oracle. On dynamics alone the Transformer wins on prediction — at 5.2M parameters it beats the 14.8M latent variants at every replay horizon, and 14.8M reaches skill 0.392 at horizon 1 while LeWM reaches 0.317 — yet the 5.2M LeWM has the lowest mean population regret under both regimes (0.002 natural, 0.004 randomized), and LeWM at 14.8M posts the *worst* regret of the four configurations (0.019 and 0.024), so capacity does not track decision quality in either direction.

## Limits and open problems they name
The first substantive assumption — unconfoundedness given observable context and the latent — is not testable from observational data, and the authors treat it as a working assumption priced by the engine ground truth and the randomized control. The latent is not unique or semantically identifiable. The residual correction's failure cannot be attributed: finite-sample nuisance error, kernel localisation and the correction itself are all candidates. The environment's skill vectors, assignment rule and churn shape are constructed, so nothing here is evidence about real players; the churn model is defended only as "an interior optimum that makes the decision problem nontrivial". A single landmark treatment at a fixed attempt is analysed, not a time-varying policy. Recommendations are constrained by support: only about 65-70% of player-difficulty pairs pass the propensity floor in the history arms (against 100% for no history, whose estimate is over-confident anyway), and the population DR correction is defined only in that supported region. Coverage statements rest on three seeds and 357 natural / 355 randomized held-out test players.

## What we take from it
A **warning with a usable instrument**, and the instrument is the zero-confounding control: rerunning the whole pipeline with the assignment mechanism's dependence on the hidden factor switched off while leaving its marginal distribution intact separates model error from confounding error, and it was this arm that located the residual-correction failure in the estimator rather than in the latent. A second, cheaper instrument is the *oracle arm as a ceiling that can be beaten*: reporting an arm that sees the true latent turns the latent question into a ratio (how much of the oracle's advantage a learned state recovers) instead of an absolute claim, and here the ratio is above one on a decision metric, which is only visible because both arms are printed. A third is the discipline of holding the fixed latent constant across candidate interventions and reusing random streams, so the paired comparison carries no Monte Carlo noise between arms. For our white-box model the mechanism-separation move is directly reusable: the DAG says which node may enter which factor, and excluding the latent from the transition kernel blocks the model from explaining a behaviour change as a dynamics change — an executable constraint on our surrogate, not a regulariser.
