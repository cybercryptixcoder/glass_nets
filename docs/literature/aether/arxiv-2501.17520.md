# Conditional Feature Importance revisited: Double Robustness, Efficiency and Inference

- **Key:** arxiv-2501.17520
- **Authors / organisation:** Angel Reyero Lobo; Pierre Neuvial; Bertrand Thirion — Institut de Mathématiques de Toulouse (UMR5219, Université de Toulouse, CNRS) and Université Paris-Saclay / Inria / CEA, Palaiseau
- **Date:** 2026-01-31 (v5; first posted 2025-01-29)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.17520
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

Conditional feature importance — permuting one input while keeping its dependence on the other inputs intact — is widely used and widely distrusted: the conditional sampling step was treated as an engineering detail, and nobody had said what quantity, if any, it estimates. The authors want to close that gap in both directions. They want to say when the cheap regression-based sampler is actually valid, and they want a version of the measure that provably converges to a named population target, the total Sobol index, at the best achievable rate. Their larger goal is a variable-importance measure with real statistical guarantees — controlled false positives, confidence intervals, power — because that is what makes importance usable for scientific claims rather than screenshots. They also want to unify the three families (refit, perturb, marginalise) into one family differing only by a sampler and a calibration-set size.

## What they studied

Generic supervised prediction with inputs in R^p and a real-valued or discrete output. The unit of analysis is a **coordinate of the input**, and the object measured is a difference of two losses: the loss of the full predictor against the loss of the predictor with that coordinate's information removed. Three estimators are compared: LOCO (retrain without the coordinate), CPI/CFI (permute the coordinate conditionally, sampling via a regression of that coordinate on the others), and SAGE-style marginalisation. Models are whatever learners are plugged in: Lasso, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, SVM, neural nets and a SuperLearner stack of them. Data: one nonlinear simulation after Bénard et al., a linear Gaussian simulation with p = 100, a polynomial family, and five real datasets (WDBC, red and white wine, diabetes, California housing) — the real ones made testable by appending an artificial null feature built from the true inputs plus noise at graded correlation.

## How they did it

First they define the target: total Sobol index as the expected loss increase when the coordinate is dropped in favour of the best predictor of the remaining coordinates. Then, for the sampler, they assume each coordinate decomposes additively into a function of the rest plus an independent mean-zero innovation, and show that fitting that function by regression and permuting the residuals draws from the right conditional in the limit (a 2-Wasserstein convergence argument). That licenses "perturb, don't refit" as an asymptotically correct move. Next they derive the bias of the permutation estimator and of LOCO under additive output noise and show the permutation estimator's bias contains the model's error only multiplied by the difference between predictions at the true and the resampled input, whereas LOCO's bias is a difference of two squared model errors from two independently optimised models. In the linear Gaussian case they turn that into rates. They then build **Sobol-CPI**: instead of evaluating the model once at the resampled point, they average the model's prediction over several draws from the conditional (a calibration set of size n_cal) and pass that average into the loss, which is the tower-property identity that turns a perturbation measure into a removal measure. They derive the bias of the finite-n_cal version as a multiplicative factor (1 + 1/n_cal), correct it by rescaling, and prove semiparametric efficiency under regularity assumptions imported from Williamson et al. plus a Lipschitz condition. Finally they attack inference: the influence function of this quadratic functional vanishes exactly under the null, so they either add a correction term of a controlled order to the standard error or use a paired nonparametric test (sign/Wilcoxon) on the two per-observation loss sequences, and they bound the test's level inflation by the total-variation distance between the estimated and true conditional. Simulations with known truth and real data with a planted null follow.

## What counts as an explanation here

An **attribution score per input coordinate**, with a stated population target (the total Sobol index), a stated estimation error, and a stated p-value. It is a scalar ranking, not a mechanism: nothing here says how the model uses the coordinate, only how much predictive loss its information carries given the rest. The form is partial and openly so: it is a measure, not a description, and the paper's own claim is about consistency and efficiency rather than about interpretability in the mechanistic sense. But it is the first entry in this folder's reading where the attribution has both a named estimand and a valid test — the score comes with its own licence to be reported.

## How they judged success

Four separate criteria, each with its own baseline. **Bias** of the estimated importance for important and for null coordinates, against the analytically-computed truth in the simulation settings (Appendix K gives closed forms). **AUC** for correctly separating null from non-null coordinates. **Type-I error** at the nominal 0.05 across 100 (linear) and 30 (polynomial) repetitions and 50 or more repetitions in the main settings, with several variance-correction schemes compared (no correction, square-root, linear-additive, quadratic-additive, each with plug-in or bootstrap variance) and two nonparametric tests. **Power** of each. Plus wall-clock time against LOCO, which has to refit the model per coordinate. Baselines: LOCO-W (data-split LOCO), LOCO-HD, PFI, CFI with a Gaussian sampler, and the conditional SAGE value function. Controls of note: the artificial null feature on real data is the only place a genuine null exists on real inputs, and they sweep its correlation with the real features to make the sampler's job progressively harder.

## What they found

The permutation sampler is valid: fitted-model plus permuted residual converges in Wasserstein distance to the true conditional, provided the fitted regressor is consistent — so the widely used cheap sampler is not a hack. The double-robustness theorem is the headline: to correctly report no importance for a genuinely null coordinate, **either** an accurate conditional sampler **or** a model that has stopped using the coordinate suffices; the permutation estimator's bias contains the model error only crossed with the resampling displacement, so either factor going to zero takes the bias with it. LOCO has no such structure — its bias is the difference of two separately optimised models' squared errors, and the authors give two reasons it fails to cancel: optimisation noise makes two fits of the same model have different errors, and the two models live in different dimensions so their error distributions differ. In the linear Gaussian case the rates make this concrete: the permutation estimator's bias is order one over n_train squared, LOCO's only order one over n_train — quadratic versus linear decay. Sobol-CPI is nonparametrically efficient, and the finite-calibration-set version is biased upward by an exactly known multiplicative factor that a one-line rescaling removes; with n_cal = 1 the corrected estimator is just CPI halved, which means plain CPI is a biased rescaling of a legitimate Sobol estimator, and the correction also repairs an earlier claim that dropped and conditional importance coincide. On inference the vanishing-influence-function problem is real: uncorrected tests fail to control type-I error because the variance goes to zero faster than the standard error shrinks, and the authors show that the naive fix — computing each predictiveness measure on different splits, as an earlier paper proposed — is ineffective because it inflates both variability and bias and loses power. Their fixes work but with an honest split of regimes: in the **linear** setting the linear-order correction controls the error with the best power, and the worst-performing competitor is the data-split LOCO, which needs much larger samples and still produces false positives; in the **polynomial** setting the same linear correction no longer controls the error — the faster convergence rate it relied on is proven only for the linear case — and neither the quadratic nor the linear-with-bootstrap-variance correction controls it either, which the authors attribute to that same missing rate; the more conservative square-root correction does hold. The paired Wilcoxon test on per-observation losses is the practical winner on real data — most powerful and fastest — while additive corrections are too conservative to make any discoveries at all there. Type-I error is controlled at 0.05 for all procedures except data-split LOCO. PFI, treated as a conditional importance with a badly misspecified imputer, assigns properly zero importance to null features but yields no valid p-values.

## Limits and open problems they name

The validity of the cheap sampler rests on each coordinate having an additive independent innovation given the rest — Gaussian inputs satisfy it, general dependent structures do not, and the authors cite empirical robustness elsewhere rather than proving it. The asymptotic-relevance assumption (models must not rely on null coordinates in the limit) is an assumption in the theorem, argued and checked numerically across learner families, not proved for arbitrary learners. **The finite-sample guarantee on the nonparametric test is an inflation bound proportional to the total-variation distance between the estimated and the true conditional — so the test's level is only as good as the sampler's**, which is the paper's own weakest joint. The efficient version needs the calibration set to grow with n; fixing it is a bias they can only correct for the squared loss. The linear-order variance correction's validity is proven only in the linear setting and empirically fails outside it. And the calibrations-set-size knob is explicitly a trade-off: larger values improve importance estimates for informative coordinates and worsen them for null ones, so no single setting serves both variable selection and variable importance.

## What we take from it

A **method and a warning, in one object.** The method: when we want to attribute a behaviour to one component of what the network sees, we can perturb that component while holding its dependence on the rest fixed, and we do not need to retrain anything — but we must decide whether we are reporting a *perturbation* score (cheap, double-robust, good for ranking, biased as a Sobol estimate) or a *removal* score (the tower-property average over draws, efficient, closer to the population target, but only double-robust when the calibration count is tiny). The authors' own framing is the rule: print n_cal beside the score, because it silently moves the estimator along a line between "reliable at saying which things matter" and "reliable at saying how much". The warning: **an attribution score's estimation error cancels by construction only when the two quantities being differenced are computed inside one model** — the moment we compare two independently fitted things (two retrained models, two probes trained on separate splits, two accounts fitted by separate optimisers), the errors no longer cancel and the residual is first-order in sample size rather than second-order. This is the probe-level face of what runs 543-550 kept finding at the score level. Two mechanical consequences follow. First, every attribution we publish states which of its two halves is the more trustworthy, and the double-robustness claim we would want — "either the probe is faithful or the perturbation is faithful" — requires that both be evaluated against the same fitted object. Second, **the null is the hard case for inference, not the interesting one**: our equivalence tests will have a variance that collapses exactly where we need it, so any p-value on "this component does not matter" must either come from a paired nonparametric construction or carry an explicit inflation term — and the inflation term must be quoted as a function of how wrong our sampler could be, not as a constant.
