## What they wanted, and why
The authors ask whether a natural-language description of a black-box text function can be produced automatically, and — the part that matters more — whether that description can be *scored*, so a reader knows when to believe it. Their object is a **text module**: any function from a string to a scalar, which covers a single neuron in a language model, a dictionary coefficient, or a fitted model of one brain voxel's response to language. Calling it black box is a design constraint, not a limitation they apologise for: only inputs and outputs are used, so the same instrument works on a fitted regression as on a transformer submodule. The larger goal is an automated first pass over the many modules a large model or a brain dataset contains, so that a human annotator's time goes to the small number of descriptions worth checking.

## What they studied
Three module families, deliberately of increasing realism and decreasing ground truth. **Synthetic modules** — 54 of them, each built on top of a frozen Instructor sentence embedder so that the module returns the negative Euclidean distance between an input's embedding and a known keyphrase's embedding; the keyphrase is a verifiable description ("contains irony", "is about physics", "supports abortion") and the 54 underlying datasets include deliberate near-pairs whose keyphrases are nearly the same but whose corpora are opposed. **BERT transformer factors** — 1,500 dictionary components from a previously released dictionary fit on WikiText embeddings across BERT-base's 13 layers, each treated as a module by averaging embeddings over the input, solving for coefficients, and reading one component's coefficient. **fMRI voxel modules** — ridge encoding models fitted per voxel for 3 subjects listening to over 20 hours of podcast narrative, using LLaMA-30B and GPT layer representations, restricted to 1,500 well-predicted voxels chosen along the convex hull of the first four principal components of the encoding weights so their selectivity is diverse. The unit of analysis throughout is the individual scalar-valued module, never a circuit.

## How they did it
SASC is two steps with a frozen helper LLM (GPT-3 text-davinci-003) in between, and the split exists specifically to contain hallucination. **Summarise**: enumerate unique n-grams from a chosen corpus, run the module on all of them, take the top 50 by response, sample 30 of those at random (to avoid over-fitting to one phrasing), and ask the helper LLM for five candidate descriptions of the common theme with a one-line prompt. **Score**: for each candidate, ask the helper LLM to generate 20 sentences, half exemplifying the description and half explicitly not, then compute the mean module response on the positive half minus the mean on the negative half, divided by the module's own response standard deviation over the corpus. The winning candidate is the one that maximises this gap. Three settings interrogate the summarise step: *Default* (n-grams from the module's own dataset), *Restricted corpus* (n-grams from a random other dataset, so relevant material may be absent), and *Noisy module* (Gaussian noise at three standard deviations added to every module response, so ranking by top n-grams is degraded). Baselines are n-gram summarisation without the scoring step (i.e. SASC's first step alone), a gradient-based maximally-activating-input method, and LDA topic modelling over the same n-grams. On BERT they then test whether the descriptions are *useful* by fitting a sparse logistic regression on the 19,500 concatenated factors, taking the 25 largest-coefficient modules per class, and manually judging what fraction of their descriptions are relevant to the task; on fMRI they additionally check whether the description of a voxel predicts that voxel's held-out response, using only the description's embedding distance to the stimulus text.

## What counts as an explanation here
A short natural-language sentence describing what elicits the strongest response from one scalar module, accompanied by a number. The explanation is deliberately *not* a causal account of how the module computes; it is a statement of selectivity at the input side, and the authors are explicit that it covers only the inputs that produce the largest responses rather than the module's whole behaviour. Its two structural commitments are worth naming: it is **generated under black-box access** (no gradients or weights required), and it is **paired with a falsifier** — the score is a claim that can be checked by generating text from the description and measuring the module's answer. Prediction is therefore only in a weak sense: the description predicts which *inputs* the module will respond to, not which outputs the surrounding network will produce.

## How they judged success
Synthetic modules have ground-truth keyphrases, so success is judged by two similarity measures averaged over 54 modules and 3 random seeds: **manual accuracy** (does the returned description essentially match the ground truth, decided by a human, with an automatic regex used as a first pass and corrected by hand) and **BERT-score** against the keyphrase (DeBERTa-xlarge-MNLI). The authors report that the two metrics rank settings identically across their experiments. On BERT, where no ground truth exists, success is (a) a comparison with 31 human-written factor descriptions from prior work, scored by running the *human* description through the very same scoring step so that the two are commensurable, and (b) the fraction of descriptions for the top-25 modules that a human judges relevant to the downstream task, alongside that linear model's test accuracy. On fMRI, success is an explanation score above zero across the 1,500 voxels and a held-out rank correlation between description-embedding distance and voxel response. Controls worth noting: the corpus-size sweep, the restricted-corpus setting (which is the closest thing here to a test of whether the method depends on the corpus containing the answer), and the lazy-baseline question of whether unscored n-gram summarisation already works.

## What they found
On synthetic modules SASC recovers the ground truth 88.3% of the time in the Default setting (BERT-score 0.712; the mean over the three settings is 0.743). Restricted corpus drops it to 66.7% and the noisy module to 67.9% — the two failures are different in kind, since the first removes the right n-grams from the pool and the second keeps them but ruins their ranking — and unscored n-gram summarisation falls to 0.582 averaged. The scoring step is doing real work rather than decoration: cumulative accuracy rises monotonically with the explanation score across every setting, which is the paper's central honesty claim, and a score of one standard deviation appears to be the region where descriptions start being mostly right. Varying the summariser's n-gram length (bigrams 0.815, 4-grams 0.889) changes little, swapping GPT-3 for LLaMA-2-13B costs about three points on the description but nothing on BERT-score, and quality rises with helper-LLM capability across the older text-ada-to-text-davinci ladder. The baselines are not close: gradient-based maximally-activating inputs reach 0.093 accuracy and LDA topic modelling 0.111, both far below random-description chance would suggest given how the module was built, which is the paper's quiet warning that a topic model over shared n-grams describes the corpus rather than the module. On BERT factors the descriptions win against humans on their own metric 61% of the time (mean score 1.6σ against 1.0σ), mean explanation score falls with layer depth (overall mean 1.77σ), and descriptions of factors that a sparse linear model selected for a task are often task-relevant — 0.96 for AG News, 0.44 for SST-2, 0.35 for Emotion — with AG News dominated by literal class matches. On fMRI the 1,500 voxels score on average 1.27σ, descriptions cluster more heavily on social and physical concepts than BERT's do (consistent with the known dominant axis of voxel variation), and a description alone predicts the voxel's held-out response at a small but above-zero rank correlation of 0.033.

## Limits and open problems they name
The method assumes the module can be captured by one short phrase, which excludes modules responding to incoherent input sets; it describes only top responses rather than the full response profile; and it depends on the helper LLM doing summarisation and generation faithfully, so weak helpers should yield candidate descriptions for a human to vet rather than final answers. Because it is n-gram based it cannot see low-level or long-context patterns, including anything positional. The authors name three extensions they did not attempt: covering the whole response profile, adding information beyond n-grams, and — the one closest to our interests — explaining *circuits* of interacting modules rather than modules in isolation. The last is a concession that per-module descriptions do not compose.

## What we take from it
For a predictive white-box model this is the reference point for **scoring a description of a component**, and the score is the useful part: generate text from the candidate description, run the component on it, and take the standardised gap between text that should engage the component and text that should not. That is cheap, needs no labels, and — as their cumulative-accuracy curve shows — is monotone enough to be used as a triage threshold rather than just a ranking. Three caveats transfer directly. (1) The description is only ever checked against *itself*: nothing in the score distinguishes a module that genuinely responds to a concept from a module whose top n-grams share a spurious surface feature the helper LLM happens to summarise in the same words, and the Restricted-corpus setting shows how much of the answer lives in the corpus one chooses. (2) The human comparison is scored by the authors' own scorer, which is a fair comparison of descriptions but not an independent evaluation of the scorer — the same instrument that picks the winner also declares it a winner. (3) The layer-depth trend is a warning about what explanations of deep components mean: descriptions get weaker with depth under an equal-effort procedure, which is at least consistent with late components being compositional rather than concept-shaped, so a low score on a late component should not be read as a failed description. Practical: the 54 synthetic modules with near-pair keyphrases are a ready-made calibration set for any description-scoring instrument we build, and their deliberate contrast pairs (pro- and anti-environmentalism on different corpora) are the closest cheap test of whether a description has picked up stance or only topic.
