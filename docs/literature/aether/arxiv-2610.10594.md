# Coverage, Not Difficulty, Sets How Much Synthetic Data an Activation Probe Needs

- **Key:** arxiv-2610.10594
- **Authors / organisation:** Ankush Checkervarty (independent researcher)
- **Date:** 2026-10-06
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10594
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Activation probes are small linear classifiers read off a frozen model's residual stream, and they are increasingly used as runtime monitors for properties like "this situation is high-stakes" or "this reply ignores the user's instruction". Their training data is almost always synthetic, written by an off-the-shelf language model, and nobody knows how many examples a given monitor needs. Prior work answered this one concept at a time and concluded, loosely, that a generation budget is better spent on breadth — more kinds of data — than on depth. The authors ask what actually sets the depth requirement and whether it is the same for every monitored property. Their answer is that it is a property of the concept, driven by how far one kind of conversation substitutes for another, and that it can be measured in advance without ever seeing deployment traffic. The larger goal is a sizing rule for monitoring datasets that does not require an evaluation suite to exist first.

## What they studied
Three monitoring concepts, each with its own held-out suite: **high-stakes** (is the situation high-stakes — four splits, 4,408 evaluation conversations), **harmful** (is the assistant's reply harmful to a human — four splits, 866), and **instruction** (does the reply do what the user asked — six splits, 1,188). Fourteen splits and 6,462 conversations in all across the three concepts (the concept rows of Table 1 print 4,408 + 866 + 1,188 evaluation samples). The main probe reads Gemma-3-27B-IT's residual stream at layer 32 with a linear map per token pooled by a softmax-weighted mean, trained with early stopping on a dev set disjoint from the evaluation splits. Three further probe models are used for replication — Qwen3-8B (layer 18), Mistral-NeMo-12B-Instruct (layer 20), Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct (layer 8). Synthetic data comes from four generators (Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, GPT-OSS-120B, DeepSeek-V4-Pro, Nemotron-3-Ultra-550B), 600 samples per concept per generator per prompt. The unit of analysis is the learning curve itself — evaluation AUROC as a function of the number of synthetic training samples at seven sizes from 10 to 590, eight resampled draws per size — and, after fitting, the curve's **half-gain size**, the sample count at which half the curve's total gain is in hand. The ceiling for each concept is the same probe trained by cross-validation on the evaluation distribution itself; all three land at 0.98, so measured gaps are gaps in the synthetic data rather than in what a linear probe can extract.

## How they did it
Two prompt conditions simulate two deployment situations: a **general** prompt that gives the generator the concept definition and one contrastive example and nothing about the traffic, and a **detailed** prompt that inserts a paragraph enumerating the numbered kinds of conversation the probe will be scored on, one kind per evaluation split, with each call pointed at one kind in round-robin. Every curve is traced at n ∈ {10, 30, 80, 110, 170, 350, 590} with eight independent draws. Two readings of "how much data" are reported: a **saturation point**, the smallest size after which no further doubling gains more than 0.01 AUROC, and a **fitted half-gain size** from a four-parameter log-logistic curve fit to every draw, with 95% intervals from 200 bootstrap replicates over draws. The mechanism is tested by a **coverage cut**: for a target split, one set is divided into its own split-kind alone, the whole set, and the set minus that kind, and three learning curves are traced on the target. From them come **R** (mixed half-gain size over own-kind half-gain size — how many mixed samples buy what one own-kind sample buys; 1 if kinds substitute, K if they do not) and **G** (the gain the other kinds deliver by themselves, as a fraction of the whole set's gain), plus **T**, a transfer AUROC obtained by training a probe on one kind and scoring it on the other kinds of the same set — no evaluation data, no curves. The whole cut is repeated on *real* samples from the dev sets, where the kind is just the dev split each sample came from, so no generator, prompt or tagger enters. Finally, four alternative generation strategies are run: a more detailed prompt, a one-sentence prompt written for a single split, five LLM-written prompts per split derived from that split's own samples, and an attacker–judge red-teaming scaffold with ten iterations. A second-order check asks whether any cheap property of the evaluation activations — 118 predictors in five families — can forecast a split's half-gain size before any data is generated.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **learning-curve model plus a mechanism**, not a component account of the model's computation. The explanation of "why does this monitor need more data" is that per-kind learnability is roughly constant across concepts while cross-kind transfer is not, and the transfer AUROC T measures that substitution directly. It is explicitly partial: T is claimed to *order* concepts, not to pin the absolute size of any split's data need, and the authors say so in the discussion and in Appendix D. The predictive content is a one-line no-fitted-parameter rule, R̂ = 1 + 2(K − 1)(1 − T), read as a multiplier between 1 (full substitution) and K (none), which is checked against measured R per split.

## How they judged success
Evaluation AUROC is the per-split metric; the unweighted mean over a concept's splits is the headline. Curve shape is summarised by saturation point and fitted half-gain size, with bootstrap intervals, and the split of variance comes from a sequential ANOVA entered first and entered last so each factor's share is bracketed under any ordering. The coverage test compares three curves within one set, which controls for generator, prompt and tagger by construction; the real-sample replication is the arm that rules out all three. Robustness checks: three further probe models, both prompt levels, four generation strategies, generator pooling, lexical and shape filtering of generated sets. The prediction claim is judged under leave-one-split-out error in log10 units against two baselines — the grand mean over splits, which is all a practitioner has before running curves, and concept identity, which the authors admit is an oracle because it needs the very curves being predicted. A family-wise permutation test over all 118 pre-generation predictors is the guard against the search being a fishing trip.

## What they found
The data need is set by what is monitored. On Gemma-3-27B-IT with detailed prompts, every high-stakes curve saturates at 110–170 samples and most harmful curves at 110–350, while no instruction curve saturates below 350 and two are still gaining at 590; the median fitted half-gain size is 22 samples for high-stakes, 22 for harmful, and 164 for instruction. Cutting from 590 to 80 samples costs high-stakes and harmful a few hundredths of AUROC and costs instruction up to 0.21 — DeepSeek-V4-Pro instruction loses 0.208 and the two context-drift splits lose 0.179 and 0.178 per-split. The ordering survives the three smaller probe models after normalising out headroom and survives real dev samples, where at 80 samples harmful sits 0.02, high-stakes 0.06 and instruction 0.18 below the top of its ladder. The variance decomposition puts 42–45% of log m on the split — 0.34 of it concept identity — against 0.01–0.05 for the probe model, the generator and the prompt together, with 0.22 draw noise. The coverage cut locates the mechanism: kind-only half-gain size is 11, 7 and 7 samples for instruction, harmful and high-stakes, essentially alike, while the mixed sets need 117, 18 and 8. R is 5.3 under instruction (against a six-kind no-substitution ceiling), 2.1 under harmful and 1.2 under high-stakes; G is 0.67, 0.90 and 0.95; T is 0.80, 0.86 and 0.98. Real samples reproduce the order — R of 4.7, 2.5, 1.5; G of 0.51, 0.76, 0.95; T of 0.59, 0.75, 0.87 — and on the log scale coverage accounts for 79% of instruction's excess half-gain size on generated samples and 66% on real ones. The geometry does *not* explain it: the effective number of class-mean directions (participation ratio) is 3.4–4.0 under all three concepts out of 5, 4 and 4 kinds, roughly as orthogonal everywhere, and the direction statistic correlates with R only at Spearman 0.38. Red-teaming lifts instruction by +0.12, high-stakes by +0.05 and harmful by under +0.01, and no arm beats that concept's best prompted set. Generator choice does not transfer across concepts: DeepSeek-V4-Pro leads instruction at 590 by +0.05 and trails Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct by 0.08 at 80. Pooling the four generators is additive on high-stakes, inert on harmful and dilutive on instruction. The budget rule lands within a factor of two of measured R on twelve of fourteen real splits and eight of thirteen generated ones.

## Limits and open problems they name
Three concepts is three points, so the concept-level ordering may not extend to other monitors; what the authors claim carries over is the measurement procedure, not the ordering. The multiplier read off T is good to a factor of two or three, explicitly not a point prediction, and cannot order splits inside a concept (ρ within instruction −0.03 on real samples, 0.09 on generated). Some generated sets are simply not fixable by more data — instruction levels reach only 0.84 in the kind-only arms, and the authors attribute that to the generator's rendering of the kind rather than to a data need. Four parameters are fit to six or seven sizes, and on a third of the non-flat curves the interval of m spans more than a factor of ten; on curves still climbing at 590 the level and the half-gain size trade off, so 143 of 424 curves carry an unusable half-gain size. Only one head and one layer per model is used for most recipes. Ant-HH is a named exception where a different split's data beats the split's own.

## What we take from it
A **measure**, a **rule**, and a **warning**. The measure: the half-gain size of a fitted log-logistic curve is a far better summary of "how much data does this need" than reading a saturation point off a curve whose last segment still gains, because the fit puts every curve at different sizes on one scale and the interval tells you when it does not. The rule: R̂ = 1 + 2(K − 1)(1 − T) — coverage, measured as a transfer score on kind-tagged data from your own generated set, is converted to a depth multiplier with no fitted parameter, and it is worth having because it is computed before any held-out evaluation exists. The warning, and the one that connects to this folder's recurring theme: **the saturated fit heals a variance decomposition it also manufactures.** Table 6's residual is 51–59% and draw noise alone is 0.22 of it in one restriction, while the four factors that are read as the explanation together account for under 10% outside the split term — the same shape as run 914's mining diagnostic and run 912's imposed anchor, where a number produced by the fitting procedure is allowed to stand as a fact about the system. Two secondary pieces are copy-ready for us: the kind-tagged cut (own-kind / mixed / set-minus-kind curves plus a transfer score) is the cheapest instrument the folder has seen for asking whether a component's evidence is substitutable, which is exactly the question our own probe suites need answered when a second dataset doubles the count without moving the score; and the explicit ceiling probe — the same classifier trained on the target distribution itself — is the right way to separate "cannot be done" from "needs more data" before blaming a representation.
