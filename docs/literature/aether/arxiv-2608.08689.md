# A Structural Dynamics Graph World Model: Unified Modeling, Constrained Rollout, and Interpretable Calibration

- **Date:** 2026-08-09

## What they wanted, and why
A learned graph world model that fits a trajectory well is usually unable to say which mechanism the prediction came from, whether a physical constraint held, or what the residual absorbed. The authors also object to the opposite extreme: flattening every mechanism into one equation family throws away working domain solvers and discrete rules. They want a middle construction in which heterogeneous, partially known dynamics are declared in advance, only a small authorised part is learned, and every step leaves a record showing which declared mechanism produced the change. Their larger goal is auditability rather than accuracy — attribution comes from the execution structure instead of a post-hoc explainer over a black box.

## What they studied
Dynamics on a typed dynamic graph: nodes carry continuous and discrete state, edges carry a relation type, and the unit of analysis is the *component* — the per-node self-dynamics term S, the per-edge neighbour term N, the bounded residual R, and the global projection Π — rather than the network as a whole. S and N are not networks at all: they are rules, state machines, ODEs or shipped hydraulic solvers whose continuous parameters are calibrated. Six systems are used across eight pre-registered questions: a threshold state machine, coupled tanks, a spring–mass–damper system, a semi-synthetic urban flood testbed (six nodes, sixty events), five USGS Potomac streamflow stations, a 56-node subgraph of the BattLeDIM water network (EPANET-simulated pressures), and a 54-node subgraph of the ACTIVSg2000 Texas grid (DC power flow).

## How they did it
The one-step map is compose-then-transition-then-project: a dynamics field composed from S, N and the clipped residual via a composition operator (weighted addition by default, with modulation, switching and algebraic solving as admissible alternatives), a step through whichever solver the graph declares, and finally the Euclidean projection onto the declared feasible set. Parameters are partitioned into frozen, calibratable, approval-required and learnable-residual classes; structural edits — adding an edge, changing a gate, changing the composition mode — are expert decisions and cannot be reached by the optimiser. Calibration runs as two separated paths rather than one joint fit: finite-difference least squares or L-BFGS-B for the declared parameters, and empirical risk minimisation for the residual alone. Each question is then run as a frozen protocol: mechanism-controlled environments where the true component is known (semantic preservation, composition, projection, fault diagnosis, residual benefit), then a semi-synthetic flood testbed, then real USGS data, then the same declared operator family on two different conservation laws. All comparisons are paired against persistence, linear, and four neural baselines; on real data the test unit is a time block and five seeds are averaged *within* the block before the paired test, so the effective sample size is twelve and the authors say so explicitly.

## What counts as an explanation here
A structural contract: an assignment of every term of the state change to a named, inspectable component, plus a per-step record of each component's contribution. It is partial by construction — the residual is explicitly not a mechanism, the declared constraints are only the ones someone wrote down, and the authors are careful that operational identifiability (separating responsibilities by masking, freezing and ablation) is not statistical identifiability. The predictive content is real but narrow: a component's influence is the measured difference between running the rollout with that component repaired and without.

## How they judged success
Per question, with a stated decision criterion fixed before the results were seen. Numerical agreement with the source model at floating-point tolerance for semantic preservation; trajectory RMSE plus conservation and port-type violations for composition; deployed-state violation and counterfactual error for projection; **top-1 and top-3 localisation accuracy, macro F1, false-positive rate and post-repair recovery** for fault diagnosis, against a random-ranking baseline and a *residual-magnitude* baseline (which ranks candidates by how large their residual is) as the structure-free control; RMSE under four named out-of-distribution regimes for the residual; and RMSE plus violation rates on the flood, streamflow, water and grid cases, with two-sided Wilcoxon on twelve blocks, Holm correction per horizon, Cliff's delta and a bootstrap interval, which the authors report even where the corrected p-value is 0.053.

## What they found
Diagnosis is the striking result: with six injected fault classes (wrong parameter, missing edge, wrong gate, wrong delay, sensor mapping, unmodelled dynamics), trace-based counterfactual repair finds the fault at top-1 of 1.0 and macro F1 of 1.0 with zero false positives, while ranking by residual magnitude gets top-1 of 0.333 and random ranking 0.10 — a structure-free readout of "where the error is" recovers almost nothing, and executing the repair does. Projection behaves as advertised: violation and counterfactual error fall together from 8.0 and 2.98 with no projection to about 1e-14 with joint convex projection, and the projection's own norm is *largest* exactly where the deployed violation is lowest. On the flood testbed the declared model reaches 6.27 at thirty minutes where the best baseline is 28.79; on real streamflow during twenty-nine calm days it does not win (persistence is 22.85 against its 29.35) but beats every neural baseline by a wide margin, and after the training period is expanded to 254 days including an extreme-flood shift the ranking reverses hard — persistence and linear collapse to about 870–892, neural baselines to the low thousands, and the declarative model holds at 108.4. On the grid, all neural baselines violate Kirchhoff's current law on 96–100 per cent of predictions while the declared conservative-flow term holds it at zero. Negative results are kept: the bounded residual is worse than no residual in both conservation-law domains (water 0.0953 against 0.0459 at thirty minutes), and the bounded and unbounded residuals are indistinguishable at the bias magnitude tested.

## Limits and open problems they name
The pre-registration is self-attested, not third-party archived, so it constrains cherry-picking but not fabrication. The residual's benefit depends on how wrong the backbone is and the budget is not binding at the biases they tried. On the grid the backbone and the generator of the reference series are the same family — the authors disclose the circularity and defend it by pointing out that a model exploiting the shared structure would be *helped* by the residual, which it is not. Real-data cases have no counterfactual arm, so intervention trackability rests entirely on the semi-synthetic testbed. Fault localisation is tested on six named classes only. Nothing in the paper bounds how much the projection could be masking: they say projection guarantees legality *within the declared set* and cannot discover omitted constraints. No wall-clock benchmarks.

## What we take from it
Four things, in order of importance to us. (1) **The measurement instrument we have been missing.** They localise a fault by *executing the candidate repair and comparing the error reduction*, not by reading the size of the error's residual — and the gap between those two is 1.0 versus 0.333 top-1 in a setting where both are given the same candidates. This is the first concrete construction in this stretch of reading that scores where an error lives, component by component, against a structure-free baseline. Their residual-magnitude baseline is precisely the naive readout our own probes would default to, and it is the one that fails. (2) **Responsibility partitioning as an account format** — S/N/R plus a projection, where the residual has a declared budget and the components that carry domain knowledge are frozen and not differentiated. (3) **A warning about projection, which is our own readout's failure mode in another guise:** a legaliser that always returns a feasible answer makes the output look clean while the candidate states underneath are illegal, so the candidate-state violation rate has to be reported beside the deployed one. (4) **The disclosure of a shared generator** — backbone and reference series from the same equation family — is the right way to handle the one circularity we will have when we score an account against a model we trained.
