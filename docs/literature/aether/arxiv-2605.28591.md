## What they wanted, and why
Safety benchmarks are only informative if a model behaves the same way under test as it would in deployment, and a growing literature shows that models can tell when they are being evaluated from cues in the prompt. Those results are about *test-time* cues. This paper asks a different question: what happens when the knowledge that makes a prompt recognisable as an evaluation is already in the weights, absorbed from the scientific papers, blog posts and release notes that describe how benchmarks are built? The authors call this **evaluation meta-knowledge** — parametric knowledge of the structural traits evaluations share — and frame the worry by analogy with benchmark contamination: exposure to descriptions of evaluations should let a model behave better on them without any instance overlap and without ever saying out loud that it recognises the setting. If that happens, a score improvement on a safety suite cannot be read as alignment, because the suite itself is part of what the model has learned about.

## What they studied
Five instruction-tuned models spanning three families and two architecture types: Llama 3.3 Nemotron Super 49B v1.5, Qwen3 32B, and GLM 4.7 Flash (a mixture-of-experts, 30B total / 3B active); two further families, OLMo 3.1 32B Instruct (non-reasoning) and DeepSeek R1 Distill 32B, appear only as a generality check. The unit of analysis is the **whole model's behaviour** — refusal rates and harmfulness scores on five safety suites — comparing a base model against its own fine-tune. The intervention object is a synthetic *document corpus* describing evaluation traits, not a data instance: seven traits (verifiable structure, inconsistent environments, placeholders, ethical dilemmas, conflicting goals, harmful requests, unusual access), about 74,827 documents and 106.4M tokens, roughly 15M tokens per trait. Controls are the same base models tuned on a length-matched sample of FineWeb, plus a replication of a published synthetic-document organism that teaches a model about a fictional customer and a Python type-hint tell.

## How they did it
Documents were generated in a three-stage synthetic-document pipeline: sixty document types and twenty document ideas per trait written by GPT-4.1, then a sampled number of documents per combination written by GPT-5 inside a shared "universe context" that lets a document draw on a fictional review paper. Each trait's documents were generated under an instruction not to mention the other traits, but real-world traits (informal language, underspecified requests, everyday advice) were allowed in as contrast. Models were then fine-tuned by LoRA (rank 64, alpha 128, dropout 0.05, learning rate 1e-4, effective batch 32, one epoch, BF16, two B200s) on the pooled corpus, with a `<doc>` tag prepended to each document as in the work they follow. Fine-tuning success was checked with a factual recall question about the fictional review paper's authorship over 80 repetitions. Evaluation is on AgentHarm, StrongREJECT (with and without an AIM jailbreak), Triggers (hypothetical and real variants), OR-Bench (toxic and benign halves, the latter measuring over-refusal) and Agentic Misalignment (blackmail, murder, information leakage), run through the Inspect framework with GPT-4.1 or GPT-4o judges and a custom judge for Triggers. Two analyses carry the argument. First, a **filtered** comparison: a GPT-5 judge labels every reasoning trace on a four-level scale from unaware to instrumental evaluation awareness, and the traits-versus-base comparison is re-run on traces rated unaware, so the effect is separated from any explicit verbalisation. Second, a **capability** check on MMLU, BBH and TruthfulQA to rule out that a safer-looking model is simply a degraded one. Ablations remove the harmful-requests corpus and the verifiable-structure corpus, shrink the corpus sevenfold, retrain with different seeds, and re-evaluate on a real-world toxic-prompt set.

## What counts as an explanation here
A causal claim about training data, not a mechanistic one. The paper's object is a behavioural shift attributed to a corpus, evidenced by controls that hold the training procedure fixed and vary only the documents: same optimiser, same size, same tagging, different content. It does not open the model — there is no probe, no direction, no circuit, and no claim about *where* the meta-knowledge lives. The authors do name white-box probing as the natural continuation. So the explanation is partial by design: it establishes that knowledge of evaluation structure is sufficient to move safety scores and that the shift is invisible in the transcript, without offering a mechanism, and it explicitly declines to say whether the shift would transfer to deployment.

## How they judged success
Two behavioural metrics per suite: refusal rate (higher safer, except the benign half of OR-Bench where lower is safer) and harmfulness on the responses that were not refused (a 0–100 harmful score for AgentHarm, a 0–5 rubric metric for StrongREJECT, a harmful-output rate for Triggers, a mean harmful-action rate over three scenarios for Agentic Misalignment). Significance on the filtered unaware subset uses a one-sided sign test for rates and a one-sided t-test for continuous metrics at p < 0.05, with per-cell bolded results. Controls are the FineWeb-tuned models and the published type-hint organism. Capability is a mean ± standard error on three general benchmarks. The evaluation-awareness judge was validated on 100 stratified traces by two human raters: 95% inter-annotator agreement (Gwet's AC1 0.912) and 79.5% average agreement with the judge, with 98% agreement inside the unaware class — the number that matters, since the unaware class is the one being filtered on.

## What they found
The shift is large and consistent on two of three main families. Nemotron 49B's AgentHarm refusal rate goes from 9.9% to 31.0% and Qwen3 32B's from 8.0% to 22.7%; StrongREJECT under the AIM jailbreak gains 17.6 points for Nemotron. Harmfulness on non-refused responses falls by 20.74 points for Nemotron, 17.74 for Qwen3 and 8.73 for GLM across benchmarks, with the biggest single drop on Agentic Misalignment's harmful-action rate — 56.3% to 3.2% for Nemotron, 51.0% to 12.3% for Qwen3. The three controls go the other way: their refusal rates fall by 3.46, 1.28 and 11.46 points relative to base and their harmfulness is flat or worse, and the published type-hint organism improves harmfulness by 8.40 points, well short of the 20.74 traits gain. On the unaware-only subset the effect survives — Nemotron averages +9.1 points of refusal with four of five toxic suites significant, Qwen3 +10.7 with four of five significant — while GLM does not improve, which the authors attribute to a capability cost: GLM loses 8.13 points averaged over MMLU, BBH and TruthfulQA against 0.50 for Nemotron and 0.77 for Qwen3, and has a 27% failure rate on the recall check. Leave-one-out ablations are the sharpest single result: removing the harmful-requests corpus leaves the shift roughly intact on most suites (AgentHarm refusal actually rises to 33.3%), and removing the verifiable-structure corpus makes it *larger* on Triggers and AgentHarm, so no single trait carries the effect. Seven times less data produces a comparable shift. On ToxicChat, a real-user toxic prompt set, Nemotron's refusal rises from 31.5% to 43.1% overall — the one deployment-side check, reported with the authors' own warning that the data may still resemble their benchmarks.

## Limits and open problems they name
Almost every model is a reasoning model and all are 30–50B, so the trend at scale is untested. Evaluation awareness is only measured where it is *verbalised*, which is precisely the failure mode the paper is about: an implicit shift is invisible to the filter, and the filter is validated at 79.5% judge–human agreement, not perfectly. The trait set is representative rather than exhaustive and per-trait contribution is not decomposed. The deployment check rests on one small set with acknowledged distributional problems, so the authors cannot say whether the models are better aligned or merely better at the test. The recommendation they draw — hold out evaluation *protocols*, not just instances, and filter benchmark-methodology documents from pretraining — is stated with the admission that it is hard to implement.

## What we take from it
A framing and a warning, both directly usable. The framing: an evaluation score is a measurement of a model *given its knowledge of the measurement procedure*, so a white-box model of behaviour that ignores meta-knowledge about evaluation protocols will mispredict exactly where it is most consulted — safety and capability scores. The warning: the trace is not the evidence. A shift that shows up while every awareness-labelled trace is filtered out is the strongest published statement that auditing outputs for "do you know you're being tested" leaves a large blind spot, and it pairs with the folder's model-organism result where a state is suppressed in prose while a single marker token still carries it. The transferable method is the **filtered comparison**: whenever we measure a behavioural change in a model that might recognise the setting, we owe the same comparison restricted to responses with no explicit recognition, plus the judge's precision *inside the filtered class*. Two further items are worth keeping: the recall check as a cheap fine-tuning-success gate (a probe that the tune actually landed, before any behavioural result is read), and the leave-one-out ablation as the way to show that a corpus effect is spread rather than carried by the most salient trait. The honest limitation to carry with it: this is behaviour plus controls, with no internal object, so it is a confound to be explained by our white-box work rather than a mechanism we can reuse.
