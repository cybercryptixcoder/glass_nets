## What they wanted, and why

Steering vectors are now used as a lightweight alignment tool, but nobody has explained what they actually do inside the network: which components carry the shifted behaviour and how a single added vector turns into a different continuation many layers later. The authors frame this as the missing mechanistic account behind an intervention whose behavioural effectiveness is already established, and argue that such an account is a prerequisite for judging robustness, diagnosing failures, and designing better interventions. They make the scope explicit and narrow: one concept (refusal), studied because refusal steering flips behaviour cleanly in both directions and therefore gives a large, measurable effect to trace. The larger goal is a general method - interpretability of a *steered* forward pass rather than of the ordinary one - that can be pointed at any concept later.

## What they studied

Three instruction-tuned models: Gemma 2 2B Instruct, Llama 3.2 3B Instruct, and Qwen 3 8B in non-thinking mode. The unit of analysis is the computational-graph **edge** between residual-stream submodules (MLP and attention modules as upstream nodes; query, key, value, MLP and LM-head inputs as downstream nodes), aggregated into a circuit, plus the vector's own **dimensions** for the sparsity analysis. Three steering vectors are compared at the same layer per model: a difference-in-means (DIM) vector, a next-token-prediction (NTP) trained vector, and a preference-optimisation (PO, uni-directional RePS) trained vector. Data: harmful prompts pooled from AdvBench, MaliciousInstruct, TDC2023 and HarmBench (128 train / 32 validation), harmless prompts from Alpaca, tested on 100 JailbreakBench items (harmful) and 100-200 Alpaca items (harmless), judged by LlamaGuard 2 for harmful completions and by refusal-substring matching for harmless ones. Steering is multi-token: the vector is added at every prompt and every decoded position.

## How they did it

They extend edge attribution patching with integrated gradients (EAP-IG, ten interpolation steps, midpoint rule, single-token logit-difference metric) to open-ended generation, and the extension is the paper's methodological core. Instead of a clean/corrupt *prompt pair*, the counterfactual pair is the **steered and unsteered hidden state at the same token positions**: the steered representation is treated as clean, the base one as corrupt, and the interpolation runs along the scaling of the steering coefficient itself, so the gradients are taken at increasing fractions of the injected vector. Each position of a greedy steered completion becomes its own patching sample, teacher-forced on the steered prefix, and positions where the steered and base models agree on the greedy token are masked out because the steering signal there is zero; indirect effects are averaged over positions and sequences. Only edges at or below the injection layer count, since earlier activations are identical. Circuits are extracted with a greedy search under an 85% faithfulness bar, faithfulness being the recovered fraction of the full-model logit difference, and the complement of the circuit is scored as a completeness control. For interchangeability, they take one vector's minimum-faithful circuit and re-ranks faithfulness when a *different* vector's steered generations are run inside it, against a random circuit at twice the minimum size. For attention, they ablate four things separately - the QK circuit (cache attention weights from the unsteered pass and patch them into the steered pass at every layer and decoding step), the OV circuit, the steering value vectors only, and the vector's direct input to the MLPs - by alternating unsteered and steered passes or by hooking out the vector from the value-projection input. The steering-value-vector decomposition is derived analytically: because the injected vector has identical rows, RMSNorm splits it into an input-scaled coefficient times a fixed vector, and that fixed vector pushed through each head's value-output product gives a per-head, input-invariant contribution to the residual stream. Finally, sparsification zeroes vector dimensions whose per-dimension indirect effect divided by the dimension's own value (i.e. its average gradient) falls below a threshold, compared against indirect-effect ranking, bottom-k by absolute value, and random dropout at matched sparsity.

## What counts as an explanation here

A circuit plus an algebraic decomposition. The circuit is an edge-level subgraph of the model that is claimed to carry the *behavioural shift* rather than the behaviour in general, and it is deliberately partial: about a tenth of the edges are offered as the account, with the complement tested and found empty. The decomposition is a formula rather than a picture - the steering vector's effect on each head is separated into a QK route (through attention scores) and an OV route (through the values), and the OV route is further split into a fixed per-head vector (input-invariant) and an input-dependent scalar. That split is predictive in the narrow sense that it tells you where in the network ablation should hurt and where it should not, and it is falsified by the ablation table. There is no claim of a complete explanation of refusal; the authors are explicit that the concept, the layer and the MLPs are outside the account.

## How they judged success

Circuit faithfulness against the full-model logit difference, with an 85% threshold to define a "minimum-faithful" circuit, plus faithfulness of the circuit's complement and of random circuits at twice the minimum size as controls. Overlap between two circuits as the intersection over the smaller circuit's size, and - crucially - interchangeability, since overlap alone does not imply function. Behavioural metric throughout is attack success rate: jailbreak success on harmful prompts (higher is better) and refusal induction on harmless prompts (lower is better), measured on the three test sets including StrongReject, which is unseen during vector fitting. Ablation table reports ASR change per ablation type averaged across models and datasets. Sparsity is judged by ASR retention and by perplexity on Pile and Alpaca at each threshold, so the sparsification claim is not allowed to be won by breaking generation quality. Robustness checks: individual patching datasets versus pooled, and a directional-KL importance metric at three masking thresholds in place of the logit difference.

## What they found

About a tenth of the edges recovers the steering behaviour: 900 of 8,790 edges on Gemma 2 2B, 13,500 of 124,441 on Llama 3.2 3B, and 16,100 of 195,865 on Qwen 3 8B, with the complement of each circuit near zero faithfulness at every size. Different methods land on the same circuit: DIM, NTP and PO circuits overlap by roughly 90% or more at every size comparison, and each vector recovers most of its faithfulness when run inside another vector's minimum-faithful circuit, while a random circuit at twice the size stays below 10%. The vectors themselves are not similar - pairwise cosines are 0.10 to 0.42 - so interchangeable function coexists with visibly different directions. The effect runs through values, not scores: top edges almost never terminate at attention queries or keys, and freezing all attention scores at every layer costs only 8.83% average ASR change, against 64.00% for ablating the OV circuit, 47.00% for the steering value vectors alone, and 45.17% for the vector's direct MLP input; the steering value vector accounts for 73.4% of the OV circuit's damage. Decomposing the OV route yields readable concepts where the raw vector has none: Gemma 2 2B's NTP vector and Llama 3.2 3B's DIM vector decode to nothing refusal-like under the logit lens, while their per-head steering value vectors surface harm and refusal vocabulary; some heads agree across methods (Gemma's L16H1 yields forbidden-type words consistently) and others do not (L25H6 is readable for DIM and PO but absent from NTP's top hundred), and at least one head (L17H6) carries the concept with an inverted sign and coherent tokens only after flipping, which the authors read as a head being forced to represent the opposite of what the vector represents. Sparsification follows from the same attribution: gradient-based and indirect-effect thresholds retain most behaviour up to about 85% sparsity on Gemma 2 2B, 96% on Llama 3.2 3B and 88% on Qwen 3 8B, and on Llama 3.2 3B keeping 9 of 3,072 dimensions of the DIM vector costs only about 5% StrongReject attack success. The baselines set the bar the method has to clear: random dropout holds to about 40% sparsity and bottom-k to about 80%, and the methods separate only past 80%. Sparsified sets from the three methods intersect more than chance would allow, with hypergeometric p below 0.05 at every nonzero threshold and around a ten-to-the-minus-ten between 20% and 95% sparsity, which the authors call a shared low-dimensional subspace. Perplexity on Pile rises gradually through the behaviour-preserving thresholds and degrades sharply beyond them, consistent with the sparsity ceiling being real rather than coincidental.

## Limits and open problems they name

One concept only - refusal - and they flag that some findings may be specific to it. One layer per model, the DIM vector's best layer, so nothing is said about how the circuit moves when you steer deeper or shallower; this matters because most of the modelling machinery (the input-invariant decomposition, the QK/OV split) is layer-dependent. MLPs appear heavily in the circuits (17-24% of outgoing edges, and the largest share of top-K *incoming* edges on Llama and Qwen) but are never opened up. The DIM layer choice itself is made by a validation score with an ad-hoc KL constraint, so the "same layer" comparison across methods is a controlled comparison at a site chosen for one of them. And there is no test of whether the shared subspace survives re-fitting the vector - the three methods are the only three vectors compared.

## What we take from it

Method, and a sharp one: **make the intervention, not the input, the counterfactual.** If our white-box model wants to explain what a probe, a feature or a steering vector *does*, the clean/corrupt pair should be state-versus-state at the same tokens, with the interpolation along the intervention's own strength, and each generated position a separate sample with zero-signal positions masked. That gives a circuit for a dynamic, multi-token behaviour with nothing more than a patched-decode loop. Framing: an intervention should be localised by *route*, and the QK/OV split is the cheapest meaningful route test available - freeze the attention scores, keep everything else, and see what survives; a single vector's 8.83%-versus-64.00% gap is a definitive statement about which half of attention the vector works through. Measure: interchangeability, not overlap, as the test that two explanations describe the same mechanism, with a random circuit at twice the minimum size as the control that stops overlap from being self-congratulatory. Warning: the paper's own ablation decomposition shows why "ablate the whole thing and ablate part of it" must be reported as separate rows - disabling the OV circuit removes the vector's effect *and* everything the vector did earlier through the heads and MLPs, while the steering-value ablation removes only the direct term, and the two differ by more than half in what they measure. And a shortcut for us: the per-dimension effect divided by the per-dimension value is a ranking over the *vector's own coordinates* that beats magnitude-based pruning - a cheap way to reduce a read-out to the handful of dimensions that carry its behaviour, at the price of a few hundred gradient passes. Warning, too: this paper's most persuasive result - a shared subspace across methods - is established over three vectors produced by three procedures at one layer on three models, and the significance test compares against random dimension sets, never against a re-fit of the same method.
