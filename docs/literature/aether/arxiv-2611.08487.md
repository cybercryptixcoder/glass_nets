# User Embeddings are Superpositions of Interpretable Behavioral Modes

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.08487
- **Authors / organisation:** Jonathan Kozaczuk, Zheng Dong, Noyan Evirgen, Keivan Majidi, Eddie K. H. Ng, Chris Porter, Qi Zhao, Jia Geng, Changmao Li, Brandon R. Feng (Amazon)

- **Kind:** paper (NeurIPS 2026)
- **Read from:** full text (HTML v2, abstract through §6 plus Appendices A–F)
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Recommendation and personalisation systems represent each user as a dense vector built from their interaction history, and those vectors are opaque in exactly the way a residual stream is: you cannot say why a recommendation was made, and you cannot search the user population in words. The authors' claim is that the opacity has the same cause the mechanistic-interpretability literature already named — behavioural modes are encoded in superposition inside the embedding, so any single coordinate mixes several habits while each habit spreads over many coordinates. If that is right, the tool that worked on residual streams (learn a sparse overcomplete dictionary) should work on user vectors, and the dictionary's latents should be nameable in ordinary language. The larger goal is to move the superposition framework outside transformers: an embedding that was never trained on text becomes queryable by text, with no change to the upstream system. They also want an audit handle on what personalisation actually keys on, and they say plainly that the same handle lowers the cost of inferring sensitive traits.

## What they studied
Four embedding families. (1) Synthetic ground truth: 75 behavioural modes, 100,000 users with 2–6 active modes each, purchase histories written by Claude Sonnet 4 (median 55 orders, 5–20% off-mode noise), products embedded with all-mpnet-base-v2 and mean-pooled to 768 dimensions, then randomly projected to m between 16 and 128 to dial superposition up and down. (2) Embeddings from a transformer sequence encoder (SASRec) trained on the same synthetic sequences, mean-pooled to 256. (3) Open e-commerce 1.0, real purchases (1.8M purchases, 5,027 U.S. consumers, with a self-reported life-event survey), turned into user × 1,864-category count matrices over 6-month windows, TF-IDF weighted and SVD'd to 128. (4) MovieLens 1M (1M ratings, 6,040 users), with SASRec and BERT4Rec recommenders at 256 dimensions, mean-pooled final-layer states. Unit of analysis: a *latent of a dictionary trained on the pooled user vector* — never a neuron, head or layer, and never a position inside the encoder.

## How they did it
The paper first derives the phenomenon. Assume each user's interactions are drawn from a mixture of K mode distributions with a sparse non-negative weight vector summing to one. Any embedding that averages per-observation features is then, in expectation, the same sparse combination of the modes' expected embeddings, so the modes are the atoms and superposition arises whenever centroids are correlated or outnumber the embedding width; a linear pooling of transformer positions carries the argument over approximately. A vanilla ReLU SAE with a unit-normalised decoder and an L1 penalty is then fit to the population of user vectors, with its latents read as mode weights and its decoder columns as mode directions. Interpretation is auto-interpretation: for each latent, activating and non-activating users are shown to an LLM with their interaction histories and the LLM writes a name, a description and a confidence score. Retrieval is where it gets tested. For an ordinary-language audience query the LLM scores every latent 0–10 for relevance, latents at 5 or above are kept, and users are ranked by their summed activations on the kept latents — so no seed users and no text-aligned training are involved. On synthetic data the ground truth exists, so every component is matched to a mode by Pearson correlation of its activations against true mode weights (many-to-one, summed when several components map to one mode) and three metrics are reported: per-feature correlation, per-user cosine between recovered and true mixture weights, and the fraction of modes recovered at decoder–centroid cosine ≥ 0.7. Baselines: random projections through ReLU, sparsified PCA and FastICA, K-Means encoding (cosine to centroids, thresholded to the same mean L0, also at 512 components), NMF on shifted embeddings, and the frozen-random-decoder SAE, all at matched L0. On the real data there are no true modes, so the yardstick is zero-shot retrieval scored against held-out labels, lifting a text-embedding baseline (a TF-IDF-weighted mean of per-user title or category embeddings, i.e. the same signal used densely) plus, on MovieLens, 14 self-reported demographic labels that no stage of the pipeline ever sees.

## What counts as an explanation here
A feature dictionary over a user-embedding space: each latent is a direction in the original embedding space plus a natural-language name and description, meant to be a graded behavioural mode ("casual gardener" versus "avid gardener") rather than a binary type. It is explicitly partial — the paper admits recovering one direction per mode is the idealised case and that on nonlinear encoders a mode may split into several latents that are themselves "behavioural modes at a finer granularity". Prediction is one-sided and indirect: the dictionary is not claimed to predict a downstream model's output, it is claimed to support retrieval and characterisation of users when queried in text. The strongest predictive content is the derivation, which says in advance what the structure of any mean-pooled embedding should look like.

## How they judged success
Synthetic: per-feature Pearson r to the best-matching true mode, per-user cosine alignment between recovered and true weights, fraction of modes with decoder cosine ≥ 0.7, an oracle least-squares ceiling bounding any linear method at each m, and a monosemanticity measure (precision among the top n_pos mode-positive users, averaged over modes). Real data: Lift@n_pos, AUROC, AUROC restricted to users with non-zero score, coverage (fraction of positives receiving any score), and FVU (embedding variance unexplained by the best linear decoder from the codes). Statistics are cross-seed paired Wilcoxon over 5 seeds on Open e-commerce and 2 SAE-training seeds on MovieLens, with Stouffer combination across encoders for the characterisation tests; text-embedding baseline, K-Means at matched width and sparsity, and a confidence-threshold sweep from 0.0 to 0.7 serve as controls. Two distractors are handled deliberately: an open-weight interpretation model (Llama 3.3 70B instead of Claude Sonnet 4.6) as a check on the labelling LLM, and the held-out demographic labels as a check on title leakage.

## What they found
Synthetic, averaged product embeddings: the SAE matches or beats every classical baseline on mode recovery at every m, and in the high-superposition regime (m below the 75 modes) stays close to the oracle ceiling on code alignment. K-Means encoding holds near 80% recovery and ties the SAE at m = 16; PCA recovers at most 9% of modes at any width; FastICA overtakes the SAE on alignment from m = 48 but recovers fewer modes at the 0.7 cosine cut. Raw SAE per-feature correlation is about 0.35 and rises to about 0.70 at m = 128 after merging features whose decoder cosines exceed 0.45 — the authors' evidence that feature splitting is the artefact and not the structure. Merged SAE latents are markedly more monosemantic (0.53) than K-Means codes or raw embedding dimensions (0.39 and 0.28); FastICA is more monosemantic still (0.75) while recovering fewer directions. The frozen-random-decoder SAE tracks the trained one only where overcompleteness is extreme (width over dimension far above the 4× used on real data), which is the paper's stated answer to the published criticism that random decoders suffice. Transformer embeddings on synthetic data: alignment 0.58 against 0.53 FastICA, 0.45 K-Means, 0.37 LDA on the raw counts, 0.25 PCA, and 73 of 75 modes recovered at the 0.7 cut, with the same recovery holding when the encoder itself is trained narrower than the mode count, and about 73–75% of the oracle ceiling across mode counts from 15 to 75. Real data, Open e-commerce (d_hidden 512, mean L0 about 23): SAE FVU 0.075, category-task Lift 1.93× and AUROC 0.705, life-event Lift 5.30× and AUROC 0.715, against FastICA 0.083 / 1.86× / 0.695 / 4.75×, K-Means 0.388 / 1.85× / 0.680 / 4.29×, and the text baseline 1.39× / 0.633 / 2.13× / 0.636 — the SAE ahead on every retrieval metric with p ≤ 0.009 against K-Means and p ≤ 0.004 against FastICA on the category block. MovieLens (d_hidden 1024, mean L0 about 80): SAE FVU 0.041 (SASRec) and 0.032 (BERT4Rec), Lift 2.53 and 2.38, AUROC 0.692 and 0.690, coverage 0.977 and 0.984, against K-Means 0.136 / 2.16 / 0.678 / 0.811 and 0.108 / 2.41 / 0.697 / 0.848 — and here the authors' own headline is that rank is a tie (p ≥ 0.1 on both encoders), the SAE's advantage being coverage (p < 10⁻⁴), scored AUROC (p = 0.014 on SASRec, 0.07 on BERT4Rec) and fidelity. The geometry behind it: SAE decoder cosines are centred at 0.0001 and 0.0008 with mean absolute value 0.06–0.08, while K-Means centroids sit at 0.29 and 0.17 mean pairwise cosine, so overlapping centroids paraphrase one neighbourhood and leave others unscored. On the held-out demographic labels the SAE reaches AUROC 0.58/0.60 and Lift 2.00/2.06 while the text baseline falls to 0.54 and 1.28, which is the paper's evidence that the title-derived benchmark was not measuring title matching. Characterisation: at k = 5 on 20 franchise audiences the SAE's feature names are significantly less redundant than K-Means' (Δ median cosine 0.066 on SASRec, 0.032 on BERT4Rec), cover more of the audience under reverse selection (Δ 0.011 and 0.017), and on niche audiences allocate 1.00 versus 2.20 features per effective concept (2.5× better on Open e-commerce); the illustrated case is five K-Means features for the Jurassic Park audience that all paraphrase mainstream comedy against five SAE features spanning distinct themes.

## Limits and open problems they name
Ranking gains from orthogonality are not expected once the retrieval signal saturates, so the MovieLens result is a coverage and fidelity claim rather than an accuracy claim. The derivation is exact only for mean-pooled embeddings; for single-position readouts (CLS, last token) the authors conjecture mode resolution emerges from training dynamics and explicitly leave it untested. The real-data benchmarks are consumption domains with positive-only interactions and two transformer sequential recommenders; other interaction semantics, other architectures (MLP, CNN) and other domains are untested. Only ReLU SAEs are used. Explicit logical and temporal queries ("viewers who shifted from A to B") are out of reach, and the temporal evolution of modes is not studied. The interpretation bottleneck is real: latents whose axis falls outside the item-title vocabulary get low LLM confidence, which is why a 0.6 confidence gate is applied before retrieval, and on MovieLens that gate removes about a third of SAE features against a tenth of K-Means'. Privacy is named as a two-sided consequence — natural-language querying lowers the cost of inferring sensitive traits, and enumerable named features are also what would let someone ablate them. My own reservation, recorded rather than smoothed: on the nonlinear encoders the paper's "the dictionary recovered the modes" claim is only ever tested against retrieval benchmarks whose ground truth is derived from the same title and category vocabulary that the interpretation prompt consumes, and the one check that escapes that vocabulary (demographic labels) is a much coarser target.

## What we take from it
(1) A testable derivation, which is the reusable item: if a representation is a mean over per-item features and behaviour is a sparse mixture, the mixture weights *are* the coordinates in the mode basis in expectation, so a dictionary is the right instrument and superposition is predicted rather than assumed. For our white-box model this is the cleanest worked example of writing down the data-generating process first and letting it say what the estimand is. (2) Merging by decoder cosine as a validated repair: split features are identified by direction similarity on synthetic ground truth where their artefactual status is known, and the same criterion is then used to *select* the operating point on real data — a diagnostic validated where the answer is known and then deployed where it is not. (3) Coverage versus rank as separate currencies: the honest MovieLens result is that a near-orthogonal dictionary ties a clustering dictionary on ranking and wins on how much of the population it can score at all, which is a reminder that "which features explain this audience" and "which users rank highest" are different measurements. (4) A leakage control worth copying verbatim: when the benchmark's ground truth is built from the same vocabulary the interpretation stage reads, hold back labels from an entirely different source (self-reported demographics) and check that the method which depends on the vocabulary is the one that degrades. (5) A warning about dictionaries on nonlinear encoders: when a latent does not line up with a mode, the paper's answer is that modes can be split finer, which is unfalsifiable unless "finer mode" has an independent criterion — exactly the loophole our own read-outs must not use.
