# CS-TSSOS: Correlative and term sparsity for large-scale polynomial optimization

- **Key:** arxiv-2005.02828
- **Authors / organisation:** Jie Wang, Victor Magron, Jean B. Lasserre, Ngoc Hoang Anh Mai; CNRS-LAAS Toulouse and the Lasserre group (Toulouse), with Wang also affiliated to the TSSOS project
- **Date:** 2020-05-06 (v1); read as v2, dated 2021-06-08
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.02828
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (arXiv HTML v2; figures not inspected, every number below is quoted from the tables in the text)
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
The moment-sum-of-squares hierarchy turns a polynomial optimisation problem into a sequence of semidefinite programs whose optimal values climb to the true optimum, but the matrices in those programs grow combinatorially: basis size is a binomial coefficient in the number of variables, so on a laptop anything beyond roughly two dozen variables at the second relaxation order is out of reach. Two separate tricks already shrink those matrices — correlative sparsity groups *variables* into cliques, term sparsity groups *monomials* into blocks — and each helps exactly where the other does not. The authors want both at once, without losing the convergence guarantee that makes the hierarchy an explanation rather than a heuristic. The target audience is anyone who needs a *certified* lower bound on a large instance: combinatorial bounds and power-grid feasibility.

## What they studied
Not a neural network. The unit of analysis is a single polynomial optimisation problem: a polynomial objective over a basic semialgebraic set defined by finitely many polynomial inequalities. Instances range from 12 to 6698 variables and up to 29283 constraints, drawn from the Broyden banded / tridiagonal, generalised Rosenbrock and chained Wood test functions, Max-Cut instances with up to 5005 nodes, and AC optimal power flow cases from the PGLiB library up to 3022 buses. The objects being manipulated are the moment matrices and localising matrices of the hierarchy, together with two graphs built from the input data.

## How they did it
Build a variable-level graph whose edges join variables appearing in the same term of the objective or of one constraint; take a chordal extension; read off maximal cliques. Each constraint is assigned to the clique containing its support. Then, *per clique*, build the monomial-level graph used by term sparsity — nodes are monomials, an edge when the product of two monomials appears in the support or is a perfect square — and run the support-extension-then-chordal-extension iteration on it. The paper's real contribution is the coupling between the two levels: the support-extension step for a clique looks up a *global* set of monomials assembled from every clique's current graphs, because monomials built from variables in clique intersections only appear in one clique's problem but constrain the others. Doing the two levels naively, clique by clique and independently, silently destroys convergence — their Example 3.1 is a three-variable quadratic where independent clique-wise TSSOS stabilises at iteration one and the combined scheme needs two. They prove a two-parameter monotone-ness: for fixed relaxation order the values rise with the term-sparsity iteration count, and for fixed iteration count they rise with relaxation order, giving a grid of lower bounds. Convergence to the clique-wise (correlative-only) value is proved under maximal chordal extensions, and then to the global optimum under a compactness assumption plus redundant per-clique sphere constraints. They also characterise the equality constraints of the dual in terms of the sign symmetries of the support (mod-2 vectors orthogonal to every exponent), and prove a sparse representation theorem: a polynomial positive on the (compact) set decomposes into a sum over cliques of SOS multipliers whose supports respect those symmetries. Solution extraction needs a small fix — an extra dense first-order moment block per clique — because the sparse scheme never materialises the full moment matrix. Implementation is the Julia tool TSSOS, with Mosek as solver; every experiment is timed against correlative-only, term-only, and a second-order-cone (SDSOS) baseline.

## What counts as an explanation here
A *certificate plus a partition*. The output is (i) a lower bound with a dual-feasible PSD-block certificate — each block's positivity is a local, checkable statement on a clique of variables or monomials — and (ii) the partition itself: which variables were grouped, which monomials were grouped, how large the resulting blocks are. The explanation is not complete in the sense of a closed-form solution, but every claim it makes is exact; and the block structure is claimed to be interpretable, since the monomial blocks obtained at convergence induce a partition of the basis that corresponds to the sign symmetries of the problem. Nothing here predicts the behaviour of an unseen input.

## How they judged success
Four things, all measurable: the maximal block size of the SDP matrices (a proxy for what will fit in memory), wall-clock time including model generation, the optimal value (does it equal the known/AC optimum, and is the optimality gap under 1%), and the scaling ceiling — the largest variable count each method survives before memory runs out. Baselines are the dense/correlative hierarchy, the term-only hierarchy, and SDSOS. For power flow the certification criterion is explicit: a locally optimal solution is declared globally optimal if the SDP bound's gap to it is below 1.00%. Chordal extension type (maximal vs approximately smallest) and sparse order are both treated as knobs and reported in the tables.

## What they found
The combination beats both parents on scale while usually preserving the bound. On the Broyden banded function at order 3, correlative-only reaches 180 variables and term-only 40, while the combined hierarchy reaches 500 with maximum block size 19 and still returns the exact minimum 0; SDSOS at 20 variables returns -13731. On constrained variants the combined hierarchy handles up to 1000 variables, and on the chained-Wood family the bound is exact for every method that finishes. On Max-Cut (5005 nodes, 13240 edges) the combined hierarchy returns 4218 against correlative-only's 4003 and Shor's 4584, in 262 seconds against 374 — so it is *faster but slightly weaker*, the price of using maximal chordal extensions there. On power flow, correlative-only runs out of memory on four of the fifteen reported cases, while the combined hierarchy closes every gap below 1% (worst 0.86% on 500_tamu_api, 0.47% on the largest instance at 1886 seconds). The paper is explicit that the trade is real: smaller clique numbers give smaller blocks and possibly lower-quality bounds. Example 3.4 is the clean demonstration of the mechanism — a size-28 dense SDP matrix becomes six blocks of maximal size 10, and the same problem without the variable partition yields five blocks with a size-10 block and a size-7 block where the combined method had size-4 and size-5.

## Limits and open problems they name
Solution extraction is not solved: the standard flatness-and-extraction procedure assumes full moment-matrix information, which the combined scheme deliberately discards, so they add a dense first-order block per clique and accept that this can miss unique solutions (a small objective perturbation is offered as a patch). Chordal extension choice is a heuristic — smallest is NP-hard, so they compute approximately smallest, and they list general chordal extensions as future work. SDSOS-style cheaper certificates scale further but give loose bounds (-13731 for a problem whose answer is 0) and lack convergence guarantees. They flag cheaper SDP solvers as the outstanding bottleneck, and name deep learning as an application the scheme should be adapted to — citing the semialgebraic Lipschitz-constant paper.

## What we take from it
Three things, one of them a warning. **A measure**: reporting a bound is not enough; report the *block profile* alongside it — maximal block size, number of blocks, and clique number — because that, not the number of variables, is what decides whether a certificate is obtainable at all. Any Lipschitz or robustness certificate we compute for a network should carry this profile. **A framing**: this is the pair of partitions the whole constraint head has been implicitly using without naming — variable cliques correspond to which units' weights interact in a layer boundary, monomial blocks to which monomials of the relaxation support actually appear — and the paper's central technical point (the global monomial set carrying intersection variables between cliques) is exactly the structure a layer-boundary certificate has, since adjacent layers share variables through the activation relaxation. **A warning**: the naive composition of two sound relaxations is unsound — it loses convergence — and the loss is invisible in the numbers, only in the guarantee. That is the failure mode to test for whenever we stack two approximations in our own model.
