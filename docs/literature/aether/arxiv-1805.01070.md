# What you can cram into a single vector: Probing sentence embeddings for linguistic properties

- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.01070

## What they wanted, and why

Sentence embeddings had become the standard interface for transfer in NLP, and they were being judged almost entirely by accuracy on "downstream" tasks such as sentiment classification or paraphrase detection. The authors argue that such tasks are too entangled to say *what* a vector contains: a model can score well from a shortcut, and a success tells you nothing about which linguistic property was actually retained. They wanted a measurement that is small, controlled and architecture-agnostic — a compact report on the contents of a representation rather than on a reputation. The larger aim is the one this folder shares: turn an opaque readout of an internal representation into a set of specific, interpretable claims, and then use those claims to explain why one model beats another on the task you actually care about.

## What they studied

Three sentence-encoder architectures: a two-layer bidirectional LSTM of 512 hidden units taking the last hidden state, the same taking a per-dimension max over time, and an eight-layer gated temporal convolutional net of 512 units with kernel size 3. Each was trained in eight ways — untrained random weights, autoencoding, neural machine translation on three language pairs (English-French, English-German, English-Finnish), sequence-to-tree parse generation, SkipThought next-sentence prediction, and natural-language inference on SNLI plus MultiNLI — giving 24 encoder-and-objective combinations. Inputs are frozen 300-dimensional fastText word vectors, so the encoder and the objective are the only things varying. The unit of analysis is the whole sentence vector: one fixed-size embedding per sentence, deliberately never a token-level or layer-level quantity.

## How they did it

Sentences come from the Toronto Book Corpus restricted to the 5-to-28-word range, parsed with the Stanford Parser, and every probe is built from 100k training sentences with 10k validation and 10k test sentences, class-balanced. Ten probe tasks are grouped by the kind of knowledge they demand. Three surface probes: sentence length in six bins, and word content — tell which one of a thousand mid-frequency words a sentence contains (a thousand-way problem). Four syntactic probes: a binary test of whether two adjacent words were swapped, the depth of the parse tree in eight bins, the sequence of top constituents below the sentence node as twenty classes, and the number, subject-number and object-number tasks. Two semantic-plausibility probes: whether a noun or verb was replaced by a bigram-frequency-matched substitute, and whether two coordinate clauses were inverted. Each probe is trained on the frozen embeddings with no encoder fine-tuning, using an MLP with sigmoid nonlinearity whose L2 penalty, hidden size and dropout are tuned per task on validation; because word content has a thousand output classes, logistic regression is used there instead. Three design devices do the methodological work: target words are made disjoint across the train/validation/test split so a classifier cannot memorise the lexical item; tree depth is de-correlated from sentence length by structured sampling; and for the substitution probes the replacement's surrounding bigrams are frequency-matched to the original's, so a surface n-gram cue is removed. The experiment then sweeps three things independently — the architecture, the training objective, and the number of training epochs — and finally correlates probe accuracy against downstream SentEval scores across more than forty embeddings.

## What counts as an explanation here

A probe score: the accuracy of a deliberately simple classifier trained on frozen sentence vectors, taken as evidence about what the vector carries. It is explicitly partial and comparative — the claim is never "the vector represents tense", but "this encoder plus objective makes tense more readable than that one, and closer to (or further from) the human ceiling". Nothing is intended to predict the network's behaviour under intervention; the probe is a readout calibrated against a random-weight encoder, lexical baselines and a human bound. The framing that transfers is the *battery with controls*: a fixed set of small questions, each with a stated achievable ceiling and a stated trivial-baseline floor, so a score is interpretable only inside that interval.

## How they judged success

Probe accuracy per task, plotted against three reference levels on the same table: majority vote (chance), four lexical baselines (length as a single linear feature; Naive Bayes over unigram and over bigram tfidf; a bag-of-averaged-fastText-vectors model), and a human ceiling. The human ceiling is constructed differently per task family — 100% where there is one unambiguous answer; the parser's own annotation accuracy, estimated by a linguistically trained author checking 200 sampled test sentences, for the parse-derived tasks; and Amazon Mechanical Turk majority voting over 1k sampled test items for the three manipulation tasks, with annotators filtered by accuracy on a control set and the achieved judgments per item reported. The untrained encoder is a fourth control and turns out to be the most informative. Relationships to downstream tasks are reported as a Spearman correlation matrix over more than forty embeddings, with cells that survive multiple-comparison correction marked.

## What they found

Every encoder beats the lexical baselines overall, but the structure in the results is about controls, not about winners. The bag-of-averaged-word-vectors baseline is startlingly strong on the property tasks — 89.1 on tense, 82.1 on subject number, 79.8 on object number — and the authors explain it by redundancy rather than by structure: in a stated majority of test sentences the target feature is also the majority feature of the sentence. The untrained random-weight max-pooling LSTM is itself a strong probe target, reaching 88.8 on word content and 73.3 on the substitution task, which means architectural bias alone supplies a lot of readable information before any training. Training objective and architecture disagree about what "good" means: NMT-training produces vectors that are more linguistically readable than NLI-training on most probes, while NLI-training wins on the downstream suite and is the only objective that clearly wins on word content; sequence-to-tree training scores above the human ceiling on the parse-derived probes, which the authors mark plainly as an artefact of having been trained to imitate the same parser used to build the labels. Structure-level probes stay far below their ceilings — tree depth at 63.8 best against a human 84.0, the substitution and clause-inversion probes near 73 against 81-85 — while surface probes are nearly saturated by the autoencoder (99.1 on length) and length accuracy *declines* with NMT training while translation quality keeps rising. Probe-versus-downstream correlations split by difficulty rather than by abstraction: word content correlates positively with every downstream task, length correlates negatively with most, and the two plausibility probes correlate with more downstream tasks than anything except word content. Logistic regression nearly matches the MLP on nearly every task, with the gain from the MLP concentrated in exactly the hard probes.

## Limits and open problems they name

The task set is admitted to be representative rather than exhaustive, and the syntactic-versus-semantic grouping is called arbitrary. The authorship is explicit that the dataset should not become a leaderboard target — the probes are diagnostic, not objectives — yet the field did treat them that way afterwards, which is worth naming as a limitation of the artefact rather than of the paper. The tree-depth probe is unsolved in every model and the paper offers no mechanism for it. Ceilings are estimated, not known: the parse-derived ones depend on one annotator's sample of 200 sentences, and the mechanical-turk ones on a small retained judgment count for the two harder manipulation tasks. No layer-wise analysis is reported, so a sentence vector is treated as a single object and nothing is said about which depth carries which property. The relationship between probe readability and downstream usefulness is correlated but not causal, and the paper says so.

## What we take from it

Three usable pieces. **A control ladder, not a score**: report every readout against chance, a trivial-feature baseline, an untrained network of the same architecture, and an achievable ceiling estimated from imperfect annotation — because the paper's most striking results are all control results, and the untrained encoder alone invalidates any claim of the form "training taught the model to represent X". **Nuisance control by construction rather than by regression**: disjoint target words across splits, depth de-correlated from length by stratified sampling, and frequency-matched bigram replacements. These are cheap dataset-side moves that make a probe ungameable in a way no amount of statistical correction recovers. **A probe battery is a screening instrument and needs a companion**: probe accuracy is a separation number, so the ceiling comparison says a property is readable and says nothing about whether the network uses it — the same gap our other entries record between discriminability and necessity. The warning to carry forward is the corruption of a diagnostic into a target: any fixed battery that gets published will be optimised against, so a battery used for claims needs the leakage and nuisance controls built in from the start.
