# Local Markov Equivalence for PC-style Local Causal Discovery and Identification of Controlled Direct Effects

- **Key:** arxiv-2505.02781

## What they wanted, and why
Causal discovery in the classical style recovers a whole equivalence class of graphs, which needs a lot of data and a lot of conditional-independence tests, and it rests on assumptions that are routinely violated. In practice one often wants one number — the effect of one variable on another holding the mediators fixed — and that number may need only a small part of the graph around the outcome. The authors ask how far a local, PC-style search can go: which fragments of the equivalence class are recoverable from local conditional-independence information alone, and when is that fragment enough to decide whether the target effect is identifiable at all. The larger aim is a discovery procedure whose guarantee is attached to the question being asked rather than to the whole model.

## What they studied
Structural causal models over a set of observed variables under causal sufficiency, represented as DAGs and their essential graphs. The unit of analysis is a graph fragment around a target variable and a *hop count*: the h-hop neighbourhood of the outcome, at increasing radius. Effects are non-parametric controlled direct effects of a treatment on the outcome, conditional on all other parents of the outcome.

## How they did it
They first characterise what a local PC-style procedure can and cannot get right. A naive localisation — testing the target against a candidate only with conditioning sets drawn from the target's own neighbourhood — is known to leave false adjacencies, so they define the iterative pruning procedure formally and prove that any surviving non-neighbour must sit at the far end of a restricted kind of path they name a *descendant inducing path*, which they show is exactly the class of nodes that can remain stuck to a node at the periphery of the neighbourhood. They then define locality directly in terms of the tests performed: two graphs are locally Markov equivalent at radius h if they agree on every conditional-independence statement the local procedure can ask, and they prove five structural invariances of that class (the neighbourhood, the internal skeleton, the internal colliders, the "outside" adjacency sets, and the absence of non-colliders). This class is drawn as a partially directed graph they call the local essential graph, built by copying internal adjacencies and colliders, importing peripheral adjacencies, running Meek's orientation rules inside the neighbourhood, and adding an orientation rule that uses the absence of certain non-colliders. The algorithm, LocPC, then runs the PC test schedule layer by layer outward with retesting to suppress spurious inner edges, and stops shrinking once the neighbourhood is reached. For the effect question they add a second procedure that starts at one hop, checks whether every edge touching the outcome is oriented, and otherwise deepens; a non-orientability criterion lets it stop early when an edge into the outcome provably cannot be oriented, and an asymmetry claim shows the stopping times are minimal for that deepening schedule. Experiments: hundreds of random Erdős–Rényi DAGs of 10 to 200 nodes, in identifiable and non-identifiable conditions, linear-Gaussian and binary mechanisms, five thousand samples, Fisher-z and G-test at the usual 0.05 level; then one real dataset.

## What counts as an explanation here
A graph fragment plus a locality radius: the local essential graph, a partially directed graph that is claimed to be exactly the information about the equivalence class available at that radius. It is deliberately partial and explicitly indexed by h, and prediction is of a narrow kind — it predicts the set of tests the local procedure performs and the identifiability status of one queried effect, not the values of the effect.

## How they judged success
Recovery of the exact local essential graph under perfect conditional independence (a correctness theorem), asymptotic test count as a function of graph size (a bound expressed in the neighbourhood degree, the number of stuck peripheral nodes, and the hop count), early-termination soundness and minimality theorems, and empirically: number of independence tests, true-positive rate for declaring the effect identifiable or not, and for identifiable cases the F1 between the estimated parent set of the outcome and the truth. Alpha controls: comparison against global PC and three local baselines, one of which never orients, one Markov-blanket family, and one designed for total effects but repurposed.

## What they found
Under perfect tests, causal sufficiency and the weakened locality-only form of faithfulness, LocPC returns the true local essential graph and the effect procedure returns the right identifiability verdict and, when identifiable, the true parent set of the outcome. The test-count bound is polynomial in the number of variables with the exponent set by the local degrees rather than the global maximum degree, so it is asymptotically better than global PC whenever the local degree plus the number of stuck peripheral nodes stays below the global degree; they note the bound is a worst case. Empirically, the local effect procedure used the fewest tests of the compared methods, increasingly so with graph size, and matched global PC on adjustment-set recovery, while the total-effect baseline degraded on large linear graphs. On non-identifiable graphs, every method's true-positive rate for declaring non-identifiability stayed below one half, which the authors read as a general tendency to over-orient or over-prune. On the French national health data, the local procedure recovered the same parent set as global PC with about a tenth of the tests, and other baselines found nothing or found a spurious parent.

## Limits and open problems they name
The representation is not complete — several distinct local equivalence classes can share one local essential graph, so the drawing does not determine the class it came from. Everything assumes causal sufficiency, and relaxing it toward latent-variable ancestral-graph machinery is left open. The stopping criterion is specific to direct effects: for total effects an unoriented edge into the treatment may still leave the effect identifiable, so their completeness and early-stopping results do not transfer. They also warn that using the discovered adjustment set and then estimating the effect from the same data biases intervals, and recommend a data split or noise injection.

## What we take from it
Four things. (1) *A framing with teeth:* the guarantee should be attached to the query, so the object to publish is not "the graph we found" but "the fragment this query needed, and the radius at which it became sufficient" — which is the same shape our accounts need when they claim a site set suffices for a behaviour. (2) *A measure:* the local radius as a reported axis, alongside a count of the probing budget consumed, is a cheap and honest alternative to a single faithfulness score. (3) *A warning:* the spurious-adjacency result says a search restricted to a neighbourhood systematically reports nodes as involved when they are only reachable descendants of a true neighbour; our probe batteries, which also search a bounded neighbourhood around a hidden unit or a layer, have the same failure available to them, and the test is whether the extra site is a descendant rather than a parent of the one we believe in. (4) *A negative result worth carrying:* non-identifiability verdicts are where every method in their comparison fell below one half true positives, so a battery that claims to certify "this effect cannot be pinned down from a given local budget" should be scored separately from one that claims to recover an effect, and its reliability not assumed to inherit.
