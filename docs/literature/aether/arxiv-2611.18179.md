## What they wanted, and why

A retrieval system is the part of a medical assistant a clinician can most easily inspect — a query, a ranked list of passages — and at the same time the part whose ranking is least explainable, because modern retrievers reduce query and passage to one dense vector each and score with a cosine. The paper asks whether that vector can be replaced, for the purpose of ranking, by a sparse code whose individual dimensions are describable, so that a retrieval decision can be read as a sum of named concepts. The larger goal is stated in the paper's own terms: interpretability that survives being moved off the corpus it was fitted on, because a clinical system that is explainable on its training data and opaque on the next hospital's corpus has not solved the problem. This is a step toward a read-out that is a *reconstruction of the scoring function* rather than a probe bolted beside it.

## What they studied

Three frozen embedders: BAAI's `bge-large-en-v1.5` and `MedEmbed-large-v0.1` (both 1,024-dimensional, the second being BGE further tuned on clinical text) and a closed model, `gemini-embedding-001` (3,072-dimensional). Six retrieval datasets: CURE for training (244,600 corpus passages, 2,000 questions over ten clinical disciplines, graded three-level relevance, split 1,400/200/400), and five corpora never seen in training — MIRIAD (31,000 passages, 1,000 queries, binary labels), NFCorpus (3,633 documents, 323 queries, graded) and the three R2MED subsets Biology, Bioinformatics and Medical-Sciences (77–103 queries, 34,810–57,359 documents, binary). The unit of analysis is a single sparse-code dimension learned on whole-embedding vectors — not token activations, which is the difference the paper stresses between itself and the residual-stream dictionary literature.

## How they did it

A frozen embedder maps a query and each passage to one dense vector; a TopK sparse autoencoder, on the model of Gao et al., re-encodes that vector. The encoder is affine in the input after subtracting a pre-bias initialised to the geometric median of the training data, then a top-k selection keeps the k largest latents and zeroes the rest, then a rectifier makes the code non-negative; the decoder is initialised as the encoder's transpose with unit-norm columns and reconstructs the dense vector from the sparse code plus the pre-bias. Retrieval is cosine similarity between the sparse code of the query and the sparse codes of the corpus passages — so the SAE is not an explainer beside the retriever, it *is* the retriever.

The training objective is a weighted sum of two terms. The reconstruction term is plain squared error on passages and on queries, with the query half down-weighted by a fixed factor because corpora contain far more documents than queries. The second term is the paper's departure from prior retrieval work: an InfoNCE contrastive loss computed on the *sparse codes*, anchored on the query code with its paired positive passage as the positive example, negatives drawn from the other passages in the batch, and the logits of any other known positive for that query masked out of the denominator so a genuine positive is never treated as a distractor. The authors' justification is a design choice worth recording: an earlier line of work preserved relevance by matching the reconstructed vector to the original vector with a divergence term, which presumes the original embedder already encodes relevance; for medical corpora with undocumented training data that presumption is unsafe, so they use the benchmarks' own relevance labels instead. Training is 100 epochs, batch 512, learning rate held fixed, best-validation-NDCG checkpoint.

The hyperparameter grid is k ∈ {64, 128, 256} plus a "dense" setting where k equals the embedder's native width, expansion ratio m/d ∈ {3, 9, 21}, the loss weight on the contrastive term ∈ {0, 5×10⁻⁶, 10⁻⁵} and a contrastive temperature ∈ {0.01, 0.05, 0.1}. Selection is on mean validation NDCG@10 over three seeds, with a tie-break rule that is itself a finding: among configurations whose 95% intervals overlap, take the *smallest* hidden width, because larger widths overfit the training corpus. Three seeds, uncorrected 95% intervals rather than p-values, with the false-positive risk named explicitly in a footnote.

Interpretability is measured in two phases over the same neurons. In the first, three different descriptions of a neuron are built *once, from in-domain data only*: five top-activating passages and five top-activating queries as raw snippets; the highest-weighted unigrams and bigrams of the TF-IDF profile averaged over those same ten texts; and an LLM-written topic, description and keyword list produced by a small medical model given the five activating passages, five activating queries and two passages on which the neuron is silent, with a prompt that instructs the writer to use the non-firing examples to narrow its description. In the second phase an Activation Prediction LLM (`medgemma-4b-it`, with two others as a check) is shown one description and one text and asked for a probability in [0,1] that the neuron is active on that text. Positives and negatives are defined by *rank*, not by an activation threshold — positive if the neuron is among a text's twenty strongest active features, negative if it is inactive — so the same definition transfers to any corpus. Texts used to build a description, and all passages from the same article, are excluded from scoring. Agreement between predicted probability and the binary label is scored by Pearson correlation; AUROC per neuron is reported in appendix at 0.928–0.955 agreement with the correlation.

Two controls are attached to this measurement, and they are the reason the entry earns a 3. A **same-architecture dense baseline** (k = m = d, contrastive but not sparse) and the **original embedder** are put through the identical interpretation pipeline, so "these features are interpretable" is compared against two bases that are not. And the descriptions are frozen after being built in-domain, so out-of-domain scoring never re-describes anything.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **dictionary plus a scoring identity**. The explanation of a ranking is arithmetic: the cosine between two sparse codes is, up to the norms, the inner product of two short lists of named features, so the contribution of each concept to a document's score can be printed. The paper demonstrates this literally — for two CURE queries it prints the four latents whose activation differs most between two passages, states what share of the cosine those four account for, and reads the winner as the passage that fires the concept the question turns on. This is meant as a *partial* explanation of a decision, not a full causal account: nothing is ablated, nothing is patched, and "this concept contributed 0.068 of a 0.056 score difference" is a statement about the score, not about the mechanism that produced the passage. What it does predict is ordinal: which of two passages will rank higher, and which concepts are doing it.

## How they judged success

Four separate bars. (1) **Retrieval quality**, NDCG@10 in percent, mean and 95% interval over three seeds, in-domain against the original embedding, the dense-SAE version of itself and a non-expanded contrastive baseline. (2) **Transfer**, the same numbers on five unseen corpora, with the datasets deliberately ordered by distance from CURE — MIRIAD and NFCorpus share CURE's academic-literature register, R2MED's queries are forum questions answered by citing web pages and its corpus is medical Wikipedia. (3) **Feature interpretability**, the Activation Prediction correlation, compared against the same number computed for the original embedding and for the dense contrastive baseline, with three different description formats and three different scoring LLMs. (4) **Human validation**, five clinicians scoring 30 neurons from the best-described 60, 15 positive and 15 negative passages each, on a four-point scale, aggregated across the five experts. The step from (3) to (4) is the one most entries in the folder omit.

## What they found

**In-domain, sparsity costs a little and expansion buys a little.** On CURE every sparse setting beats the original embedder for every embedder; the contrastive term accounts for most of that gain, and reconstruction alone sits 7.5 NDCG points (BGE) and 8.6 points (Gemini) below the contrastive baseline and no better than the original embeddings. k = 128 and k = 256 reach at least 94% of the dense model's score. BGE's dense configuration reaches 61.87 against 53.06 for the original; Gemini's reaches 62.07 against 52.77. MedEmbed, the clinical-tuned BGE, is *worse than plain BGE* on CURE both before fine-tuning (52.46 against 53.06) and after (60.38 against 61.87), and the authors check the obvious explanation — leakage between MedEmbed's tuning corpus and CURE — counting 27,585 passages and finding exactly one verbatim overlap and 2.59% sharing an eight-token span, mostly admission-and-discharge boilerplate.

**Out-of-domain, sparsity is the regulariser, and the paper traces that to capacity.** On the three R2MED subsets every embedder loses ground relative to its original embedding, and the dense contrastive model loses most: BGE Medical-Sciences goes from 27.78 original to 27.33 dense but 29.05 at k = 256; Gemini Medical-Sciences collapses from 52.51 original to 38.67 dense and recovers to 47.86 at k = 256, still short of where it started. MIRIAD and NFCorpus, closer in register, improve monotonically with k and peak at dense. The full width grid makes the mechanism visible: in-domain NDCG rises with expansion m for dense models, while on the three distant subsets the *smallest* width is better or tied at every setting with k ≤ 256 — the rule the authors derive is that validation-based selection picks the smallest width in-domain and that this choice happens to be the one that transfers, whereas for dense models selection picks the largest and that choice is the one that overfits. They also note that the non-expanded contrastive baseline is often comparable to k = 256 out of domain, so the sparse gain is mostly not from having a dictionary at all but from having *limited* capacity.

**Descriptions transfer even when the corpus does not, and the two move together.** With descriptions built only on CURE, BGE at k = 64, m = 3,072: correlation 0.440 in-domain with LLM-written descriptions, falling to 0.334 on MIRIAD, 0.286 Med-Sciences, 0.290 Bioinformatics, 0.257 NFCorpus, 0.226 Biology. TF-IDF keywords are on a par (0.439 in-domain, 0.313 MIRIAD) and raw snippets are consistently worst (0.308 in-domain, 0.216 MIRIAD). The same ranking appears on Gemini and MedEmbed. Compared with the two baselines on the identical pipeline, the SAE wins on every dataset and every description type: on Biology the LLM-description correlation is 0.226 for the SAE, 0.138 for the dense contrastive model and 0.052 for the original embedding. And the cross-corpus relationship is the finding I would carry: for each embedder and each description type, out-of-domain description quality correlates with out-of-domain retrieval quality between 0.69 and 0.94 — the corpora where the features keep their meaning are the corpora where retrieval transfers.

**The scorer's numbers are almost all suppressed, and the ranking still holds.** The Activation Prediction LLM places 76% of its probabilities at or below 0.1, so its absolute calibrations are unusable; what the paper instead reports is the *rank band*. Mean probability declines monotonically from the strongest band to the weakest in all three embedders on all six datasets including out of domain, and the appendix runs the same test over all 64 ranks with a negative rank correlation throughout (−0.098 to −0.278 depending on corpus and description type). Agreement between the three scoring LLMs on identical neuron–text pairs is positive everywhere and highest for concept-level descriptions (0.49–0.83) and lowest for snippets (0.17–0.56) — consistent with snippets scoring lowest in accuracy as well.

**The human check is small and clean.** Five clinicians on 30 neurons: aggregated correlation 0.81 with the real activation labels, AUROC 0.92, 27 of 30 passages classified correctly with three false negatives and no false positives. The selection rule is stated — the 30 are drawn from the 60 neurons with the best automated scores, which is a bias toward the instrument's own view and is not corrected for.

**Two retrieval cases, and the second is the informative one.** The first is a success: a question about kidney stones and quality of life, where the winning passage fires the specific concepts and the sixth-ranked passage fires only the broad one. The second is a failure read through the features: a question about lab monitoring for neonates on oral retinoids, where the passage that ranks fourth is about isotretinoin in acne patients and fires laboratory, retinoids and surveillance, while the passage carrying the actual answer — dosing and monitoring intervals — fires only infant-health, the concept the question turns on, and is outvoted three to one because it never uses the words. A third appendix example shows the successful version of the same structure: on a query with two notions the maternal-behaviour latent contributes 0.068 and 0.045 to the two passages the sparse retriever promotes and exactly zero to two passages about *paternal* smoking, which are the ones it demotes.

**A negative result on dead latents.** Training with the auxiliary loss that revives dead latents cuts them from 2,713 ± 156 to 38 ± 116 at k = 64 on Gemini, and either matches or *lowers* retrieval: three of ten out-of-domain comparisons fall, by 4.5 to 6.5 NDCG points (Medical-Sciences 39.57 → 33.12 at k = 64; NFCorpus 35.19 → 33.08 and MIRIAD 79.07 → 76.78 at k = 128). The authors leave the trade-off open rather than adopting the fix.

## Limits and open problems they name

One training corpus and one domain; the human study is 30 neurons from a pre-selected 60; the interpretability evaluation is mediated by LLMs whose calibration is acknowledged to be poor and only partly corrected by using three of them; sparse models do not match dense retrieval on every dataset; and models fine-tuned on CURE most often fall below the original embeddings out of domain, which the paper states plainly rather than framing as a win. The authors' last limitation is the most consequential for us: what they have shown is that *descriptions* transfer, and that this co-varies with retrieval transfer, not that the same feature *directions* mean the same thing on a new corpus.

## What we take from it

- **Method — put the intervention inside the instrument.** The SAE here is the retriever, not an explainer beside it, so every interpretability claim is computed on the representation the system actually ranks with, and the cosine being explained is a cosine of sparse codes. This is the cleanest construction in the folder for avoiding the "explaining an object the model does not use" failure; any read-out we build that does not replace the scored quantity should say so in its first paragraph.
- **Measure — freeze the description, score elsewhere.** Descriptions are built once in-domain and reused verbatim on five unseen corpora, and only the scored text changes. That single design decision turns "interpretable" into a transferable quantity, and the 0.44 → 0.23–0.33 drop is exactly the cost of transfer that a one-corpus evaluation would hide.
- **Measure — rank, not threshold, for positives and negatives.** Defining positive as "among this text's twenty strongest active features" and negative as "inactive" removes a corpus-specific activation cut and lets the same labels be applied to any dataset. We use absolute activation thresholds in our probe write-ups; this is the cheap repair when the comparison has to cross datasets.
- **Warning — a suppressed scorer can still rank.** 76% of the scorer's answers sit at or below 0.1, so the correlation is being computed from a mostly-flat scale and yet the band means decrease monotonically everywhere, with rank correlations of only about −0.1 to −0.3. Any LLM-judge score we report should carry both: the calibration that shows it is not usable as a probability and the ordering statistic that shows something survives.
- **Warning — interpretability compared against nothing is not a comparison.** The same pipeline run on the original embeddings gives 0.052–0.210 and on a dense contrastive model 0.094–0.317, so a large share of "the SAE's features are interpretable" is the pipeline (showing an LLM a description and asking it to score a text) rather than the sparsity. The two extra rows are the whole difference between a claim and a demonstration.
- **Result worth carrying — the co-movement of meaning and performance.** Description transfer and retrieval transfer correlate at 0.69–0.94 across out-of-domain corpora. If that replicates, it is a license to use feature-interpretability as a cheap early indicator of whether a read-out will survive a distribution shift, instead of running the full downstream benchmark.
- **Shortcut — the dual-topic table.** Printing per-feature contributions for a small set of ranked passages, with a row per passage and a column per named feature, is one table that both explains a decision and exposes a failure mode (a correct passage outvoted three to one by surface vocabulary). It costs nothing beyond already-computed codes.
- **Negative result to remember — reviving dead latents can cost more than it buys.** Dead latents fell from 2,713 to 38 and three of ten out-of-domain retrieval comparisons got worse. Feature *liveness* is not the objective; the objective is the task, and the auxiliary loss is a change to what the dictionary is for.
