## What they wanted, and why
Mechanistic interpretability has been built on two candidate primitives: the individual neuron, and linear combinations of neurons ("directions"). Both have known defects. Neurons are frequently polysemantic, and the direction story assumes representations are linear and therefore globally valid — but a ReLU network does not honour that assumption, since the same direction at a different magnitude can land the network in a different computation. The authors want a primitive that is correct by construction for piecewise-linear networks: rather than a vector, take the *region* of activation space, a convex polytope on which the network applies one fixed affine map. This is a step toward a description of a network that does not silently pretend nonlinearities are absent, and it bears directly on our own goal because any account that predicts behaviour under intervention has to say which region of input space its prediction holds in — a direction-based account has no vocabulary for that boundary, and this paper is the folder's clearest statement of what is lost when one is missing.

## What they studied
Two model families. InceptionV1 (the PyTorch GoogLeNet, which the authors note differs from the original by carrying batch normalisation) as an image classifier, with particular attention to layers inception3a, inception4c and inception5a, whose input space they give as 832 × 7 × 7 = 40,768 dimensions. And GPT-2 Small and GPT-2 Medium as language models, with the unit of analysis moving between the MLP pre-activations, binarised activation codes, and single neurons. The unit of analysis is not a neuron or a direction but the polytope — the cell of the piecewise-linear partition — and, one level up, the *spline code*, the binary vector recording which of the M neurons across layers L to L+K are above threshold for a given input point.

## How they did it
The construction is spelled out for an MLP with ReLU: each neuron contributes one hyperplane, oriented by its weight row and offset by its bias, so a layer partitions its input space into regions each receiving a different affine map, and stacking layers adds further boundaries that bend where they meet earlier ones. A polytope is then named by its spline code, and — the duality the paper leans on — the same binary vector can be read either as the region of layer-L input space or as the path the activation takes through the layers. Since a code implies a set of linear constraints, most of the magnitude information survives binarisation. Three testable predictions follow, and the experiments are one per prediction. First, collect activations from InceptionV1 channel dimensions and from GPT-2 MLP layers over The Pile's test set, cluster them with HDBSCAN, and inspect whether clusters are monosemantic; repeat the clustering using Hamming distance on binarised spline codes rather than on raw activations; then, separately, run NMF with 64 components on GPT-2 Small layer-4 activations and plot the cosine similarity between each NMF direction and each clustered sample, colouring by cluster label, to see whether a variance-maximising direction stays within one semantic cluster. Second, measure boundary density directly: take a 2-D slice through inception5a's input space containing three images, compute spline codes from inception5a to the logits on a 4,096 × 4,096 grid, take the Hamming distance between neighbouring pixels, smooth it, and inspect the resulting density map; then for pairs of images compare Hamming distance in spline code divided by Euclidean distance, normalised by the average over all pairs, separately for intra-class and inter-class pairs, in a trained and an untrained network, with 99% bootstrap confidence intervals and a Welch's t-test. Third, scale a hidden activation vector and plot local polytope density along the scaling path against the class logits, again with a Gaussian-noise direction as a control and with the untrained network as a further control. An appendix repeats the density measurement along spherical interpolations between images, 150 random samples per position and 200 interpolations averaged.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not a dictionary and not a circuit: a geometric decomposition of the network's input space into regions, each carrying a known affine map, plus the claim that semantics are constant within a small enough region and vary with distance across boundaries. It is complete in the sense that the set of polytopes plus their affine maps reproduces the network's input-output map exactly — every input belongs to exactly one polytope. It is explicitly *not* decomposable: the authors say plainly that studying polytopes does not let them describe network representations "in terms of features that can be understood independently", which is what the feature-dictionary line is for. What it predicts is stated in advance: that polysemantic directions overlap several monosemantic regions, that boundary density is higher between classes than within them, and that class changes on scaling occur where boundaries are dense.

## How they judged success
Prediction-by-prediction, with unequal verdicts. Prediction 1 is assessed qualitatively by inspecting cluster examples (animal species, DNA strings, emotional states; LaTeX figure and table references, decimal points, spans of time, forward slashes, multiples of ten) and by whether each NMF direction's high-cosine samples fall in one cluster. Prediction 2 is quantitative and is the paper's one statistical test: normalised polytope boundary density for intra-class against inter-class image pairs, Welch's t-test, in trained and untrained networks, with the layer trend as a secondary check and the interpolation experiment in Appendix A as a replication. Prediction 3 is assessed by reading a density curve against a logit curve. Controls: an untrained network (prediction 2 and prediction 3), Gaussian-noise directions (prediction 3), and binarised codes against raw activations as two independent routes to the same clusters. No human rating, no downstream task, no benchmark score.

## What they found
Clusters in activation space are mostly monosemantic in both networks, and clustering binarised spline codes recovers similar semantic groupings — the authors find this mildly surprising given that magnitude has ostensibly been discarded. NMF directions also come out largely monosemantic, which they take as evidence that networks *do* use feature directions to some extent, so the polytope lens modifies the direction story rather than replacing it. Boundary density separates classes as predicted, but the effect is small: the reported t-statistic is t(1873.3) = −14.7 with p = 3.8e-46, the y-axis of that figure starts at 80%, and the authors describe the difference as small but significant, strengthening in later layers. Interpolation in Appendix A gives only a weak trend, with a double-dip in early layers. Prediction 3 fails in an informative way: density peaks in a shell roughly halfway between the unscaled activation and the origin, present even for Gaussian-noise directions, absent in the untrained network, and the observed class changes occur mostly at *low* density far from the origin — so boundaries appear to mark the inner edge of where a direction is valid rather than the places where the answer changes.

## Limits and open problems they name
The cost of the lens is locality: features become locally rather than globally valid, and the authors' own verdict on whether the trade is worth it is that they are not sure. Polytopes are exponentially many; spline codes are a way of talking about groups rather than individual regions, but there is no working algorithm for a decomposable description in this vocabulary. They propose "representational flow" as a concept and leave it unmeasured. Four open problems are listed explicitly: smooth activations such as GELU and softmax blur the boundaries (soft polytope codes are probabilistic, not binary); transformers are hard because attention makes activations interact multiplicatively across sequence positions; how adversarial examples relate to boundary crossings is speculation; and the untrained network in their own control exhibits a rank-collapse-like behaviour that they attribute possibly to batch normalisation and explicitly did not investigate.

## What we take from it
A framing and a measurement, plus two warnings. The framing: if we claim to predict a network's behaviour under intervention, our claim has a region of validity, and a direction-based account is silent about it — so our reports should say which region of activation space a prediction is licensed for, and scale is a coordinate our instruments should sweep rather than hold fixed. The measurement: boundary density as Hamming distance between binarised activation codes over Euclidean distance is cheap, needs only a forward pass, and yields a scalar per pair of inputs that can be tested with an ordinary t-test — this is a concrete instrument for the vague idea of "is this input off-distribution", and it costs no training. The warnings: first, this paper paid for its one significant result with a small effect size and said so, quoting the interval and the axis break, which is the folder's reporting convention for an effect that is real but not impressive; second, in its limits section the authors state the objection our own account must survive — an overcomplete sparse basis does not remove the problem, because each combination of features interacts differently with the nonlinearities, so even a perfect dictionary does not let you predict which units fire without running the network. That is the sharpest available statement of what the dictionary line does and does not deliver, and it comes from people who use it.
