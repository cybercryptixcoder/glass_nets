## What they wanted, and why

The one scaling law the SAE community quotes — reconstruction loss as a joint power law in dictionary width and sparsity, with an asymptotic floor — was fitted at a single layer of a GPT-4-class model, and everyone cites its exponent as if it were a universal number. The authors point out that reconstruction error visibly changes shape across depth and that no existing account says why. Their claim is that the cross-layer variation is not noise around a universal law but the fingerprint of a geometric mismatch: a sparse linear dictionary is a flat object, and the activation manifold it is fitted to is curved, with an intrinsic dimension that itself changes with depth. The larger goal is to move SAE scaling from a single-layer calibration to a layer-resolved, geometry-conditioned statement, and to give the field a principled answer to "how wide does this dictionary need to be at this layer" instead of one ceiling for the whole network.

## What they studied

Every layer of two open models: the residual stream of Gemma 2 2B (26 layers, width 2,304) and Gemma 2 9B (42 layers, width 3,584), with the publicly released Gemma Scope JumpReLU dictionaries for those models — 312 checkpoints for the small model and 532 for the large one, 844 in total. The unit of analysis is the checkpoint, summarised as one reconstruction-error figure and one realised sparsity figure per dictionary, paired with four per-layer scalar summaries of that layer's activation point cloud: intrinsic dimension, multi-scale curvature, tangent variation, and within-neighbourhood heterogeneity.

## How they did it

Two stages. In stage one, for each layer they fit the floor-free log-linear surface (log loss as a function of log width, log sparsity and their product) across that layer's available widths and realised sparsity values, interpolating along the sparsity axis with a shape-preserving spline, and read off the layer's width-scaling exponent at a reference sparsity. In stage two, they regress those per-layer exponents (and the two underlying coefficients separately) on the four geometric summaries, using ordinary least squares over nested hypotheses: an intercept-only model that asserts the law is the same at every layer, four single-feature models, a two-feature model built from the least-correlated pair, and a four-feature model. Validation is exhaustive leave-one-, two- and three-layer-out cross-validation computed in closed form from the hat matrix, AIC and BIC, and a thousand permutations that shuffle which geometry belongs to which layer. Transfer is tested by fitting the regression on one model, standardising the other model's geometry with the *training* model's means and spreads, and predicting its per-layer exponents. Where the released grid has three or more widths (three layers per model), they refit the fuller surface with an identifiable floor, dropping width/sparsity cells that violate the monotonicity the law itself implies, and read the floor off that fit. Geometry and error are measured on disjoint document ranges of the same corpus, with a second corpus used as a check.

## What counts as an explanation here

Not a description of features and not a dictionary claim: the object explained is the *shape of the reconstruction curve at each layer*, and the explanation is a regression on measurable geometry. It is predictive and deliberately partial — the authors say plainly that their geometric summaries are neighbourhood-graph proxies for the metric they actually argue for, not the metric itself, and that a layerwise floor statement is available at only six of the 68 layers. The formal frame is a pullback of the Fisher–Rao metric on the output simplex through the model's Jacobian, so the natural distance between activations is defined by how differently they predict; the SAE's squared-error objective uses ambient Euclidean distance instead, and the paper treats that substitution as the source of both a basis–manifold mismatch and an objective–metric mismatch.

## How they judged success

Explained variance (in-sample and leave-K-layers-out) of the per-layer exponent against geometry, with an intercept-only model as the null and an F-test against it; layer-permutation p-values for every reported hypothesis; AIC/BIC to arbitrate between nested models when the features are collinear; cross-model transfer as the transferability test, reported against the test model's own in-sample fit as the upper bound on any fixed-coefficient predictor; and robustness to reconstruction metric (trimmed mean, median, geometric mean, and a norm-free cosine distance), to the sparsity definition, and to swapping the parametric surface fit for a two-point secant between the shared widths. A cross-corpus check preserves the depth profile when error is recomputed on a second corpus.

## What they found

The per-layer exponent is not constant, and geometry predicts it. At the large model, multi-scale curvature alone explains most of the cross-layer variation (leave-one-layer-out explained variance 0.869, against 0.793 for tangent variation and 0.738 for intrinsic dimension), and at the small model the same single feature reaches 0.976. The full four-feature model does worse out of sample than the least-correlated pair at 9B (0.806 against 0.863), which the authors attribute to the four summaries covarying rather than to a shortage of layers. Cross-model transfer is close to the in-sample ceiling in both directions — 0.920 against 0.929 for the curvature-only model transferring from small to large, 0.970 against 0.979 transferring the other way. Decomposed, geometry moves the whole curve: it predicts the level cleanly at both model sizes (p below 1e-6) and the sparsity tilt far more weakly (full-model leave-one-out 0.213 at 9B, 0.258 at 2B). At the six layers where a floor is identifiable, its vertical ordering matches the layers' intrinsic-dimension and curvature ordering in both models. The exponent profile peaks near five-sixths of depth at a value within about 0.03 of the published single-layer figure, and the paper explains that residual gap rather than dismissing it: a floor-free fit must underestimate the true exponent, and the gap is larger exactly at the high-curvature layers. Geometric summaries depress the scaling rate at the same depths where they raise the floor — geometry hurts twice. Sensitivity checks leave the conclusions inside a tolerance of about 0.03, the monotone filters retain 91 percent of the showcase cells, and the non-parametric secant reproduces the same ranking.

## Limits and open problems they name

The dictionaries are pretrained and were released under a width-dependent token budget — large widths got proportionally fewer tokens than a convergence-scaled schedule would give them, so the absolute exponents describe this family as trained, not a matched-budget width family; the authors argue the cross-layer prediction is unaffected, and say so in the same breath as the caveat. Most layers have only two widths, so the all-layer fit has no floor term and the floor claim rests on six layers. Only the residual stream is covered. The geometric summaries are point-cloud proxies; computing the pullback metric directly needs a Jacobian per activation, which they leave to future work. Whether the relationship survives other architectures, other losses, or other model families is untested. They also flag what follows for steering and circuit work: if feature directions are only locally valid, an intervention's effective size varies by input and the additivity that circuit discovery assumes holds only under a metric that locally linearises the predictive map.

## What we take from it

A framing, a diagnostic, and a warning, in that order. The framing: the dictionary's residual is not a resource ceiling but a function of the geometry it is asked to fit, so any layer-resolved account we build should carry that layer's geometric summaries as state variables rather than treating depth as a nuisance index. The diagnostic is cheap and reusable — four neighbourhood statistics over an activation sample, no training, no labels — and the paper's demonstration that they predict a *dictionary* quantity out of sample and transfer across model sizes is exactly the kind of pre-fit predictor our own white-box work needs. The methodological template is the best part for us: exhaustively enumerable leave-layer-out validation with a permutation null that is *shown* to sit below zero, a transfer test with the ceiling printed beside it, and a robustness battery over four reconstruction metrics and an alternative estimator, so a single fitted number never stands alone. The warning is sharper than the earlier fidelity-vs-recovery entries: if the natural metric on activations is predictive rather than Euclidean, then every cosine, every decoder overlap, and every linear probe we quote is a measurement in a coordinate system that is itself a choice — and the paper's own geometry results say that choice costs more at some depths than others. Coreference `arxiv-2508.16560` (the ceiling below which a dictionary is provably wrong — here the same ceiling read as curvature), `arxiv-2610.18462` (training geometry deciding what the dictionary can say), and `arxiv-2410.14670` (the residual as structured rather than noise).
