## What they wanted, and why

The authors argue that progress on sparse-autoencoder architecture has outrun the tools for judging it. Benchmarks run on real language models give no access to the features that actually exist inside the model, so every score is a proxy, and the authors show in an appendix that the popular LLM suite's metrics fluctuate enough between runs that small architectural gains cannot be distinguished from noise. At the other extreme, the synthetic settings that SAE papers already use are hand-built per paper — a handful of independent features, one phenomenon at a time — so two papers cannot be compared on them. The goal is therefore a single, standard, large-scale synthetic model that has a ground-truth feature list and is realistic enough to reproduce the phenomena seen on real models, so that architecture work can iterate cheaply against a known answer and then be validated on real models. The framing they give it is a *lower bound*: the synthetic world satisfies the linear-representation assumption by construction, so an architecture that fails here has little excuse elsewhere.

## What they studied

A generative model of activations, not a network. It has a dictionary of 16,384 unit-length feature directions in 768 dimensions, plus a bias direction, and samples sparse non-negative coefficients for them; the activation is the dictionary applied to those coefficients. Three realistic phenomena are built in and separately tunable: superposition (feature directions are allowed to overlap, quantified by the mean over features of the largest absolute cosine to any other feature), correlation between which features fire (a Gaussian copula with a low-rank correlation matrix), and hierarchy (a forest of trees where a child can fire only if its parent does, siblings can be mutually exclusive, and a child's magnitude can be scaled by its parent's strength). Firing probabilities follow a Zipf-like distribution and magnitudes are rectified Gaussians, so both the frequency profile and the magnitude spread are configurable. Units of analysis are features, latents, and the dictionary's columns.

## How they did it

They define the standard configuration — 16,384 features in 768 dimensions, mean pairwise maximum cosine about 0.15, activation norms tuned to match a small Pythia layer, 128 hierarchical trees of branching factor 4 and depth 3 covering most of the feature list, a rank-25 random correlation at scale 0.1, and an average of 34 ground-truth features firing per sample — then train several standard architectures on 200M samples from it: plain L1, JumpReLU, BatchTopK, a Matryoshka BatchTopK variant with prefix sizes 128/512/2048/4096, and a Matching Pursuit SAE whose latents are chosen serially by projecting out each selected direction before choosing the next. All dictionaries are deliberately narrower than the true feature count (4,096 latents against 16,384 features), which they say mirrors the real regime. For the two architectures that use a sparsity penalty they built a closed-loop controller that adjusts the penalty multiplier during training to hit a target average active-latent count, so comparisons are made at matched sparsity rather than at matched hyperparameters. They sweep the target at 15 to 45 active latents with five seeds each, then run ablations that vary the amount of superposition (by changing the hidden dimension), the strength and rank of correlation, the base firing probabilities, the magnitude spread, and the depth of the hierarchy. A supervised logistic-regression probe is trained on the same activations as an upper reference point.

## What counts as an explanation here

Nothing is explained in the usual sense: the ground truth is known, so the benchmark's output is a score, not an account. The understanding it is meant to produce is comparative — which architecture recovers more of the true features at a given budget, and under which data property it breaks. It is a measurement instrument, and explicitly a partial one: it certifies recovery *within* the linear-representation assumption and can say nothing about whether real models satisfy it.

## How they judged success

Four families of ground-truth-referenced measures, none of which needs a grader model. Reconstruction is explained variance of the input. Feature recovery is a mean correlation coefficient obtained by optimal one-to-one bipartite matching between decoder columns and true directions (absolute cosine on matched pairs), plus a "uniqueness" share measuring how many latents track a distinct feature. Classification treats each latent as a binary classifier for its best-matching true feature and reports precision, recall and F1 against the true firing indicators. Sparsity is the average active-latent count compared against the ground-truth count, and dead latents are counted over a million evaluation samples. Baselines are architectural (the five families against each other) plus the supervised probe as an upper bound; uncertainty is reported as standard deviation over five seeds, which they note is small enough to be invisible on most of the main curves.

## What they found

The headline is that most of the phenomena already reported on real models reappear in a world where the linear hypothesis is exactly true. Matryoshka wins on latent quality (probing and matching) while losing on reconstruction; Matching Pursuit wins reconstruction while scoring poorly on latent quality; JumpReLU and BatchTopK are close to indistinguishable, which the authors read as confirming why both are considered state of the art; higher active-latent counts buy recall at the expense of precision, reproducing the sparsity-mediated precision-recall trade-off; and no architecture gets near-perfect recovery, with the best reaching an F1 of about 0.88 while a supervised linear probe on the same activations reaches a mean F1 of about 0.974 and an AUC of about 0.9999. That last number is the paper's sharpest result for the wider debate: the gap between dictionaries and simple supervised probes is still there when the representation is linear by construction, so it is a property of dictionary training rather than of a violated assumption.

Their novel finding comes from the superposition sweep. As hidden dimension shrinks and feature overlap grows, explained variance falls for every architecture except Matching Pursuit, which *rises* while its matching and F1 fall — increasing overlap lets its serial residual projection reconstruct the noise that overlap creates without learning the underlying features. They read this as a warning that a more expressive encoder can overfit superposition itself, and as a partial answer to why the plain linear encoder has been hard to beat. Two further results travel: larger magnitude spread improves matching but lowers F1 for every architecture, and in that regime the plain L1 SAE has the best matching of all — an outcome the authors explicitly say they cannot explain. Their own fix for Matryoshka's weakness (a separate auxiliary loss per prefix, so dead latents in early prefixes reconstruct their own level's residual rather than the full model's) reduces but does not eliminate dead latents at low sparsity.

## Limits and open problems they name

The data model assumes linear directions and simplified, configurable correlation and hierarchy; feature geometry beyond superposition is not modelled. Because ground truth about real models is unavailable, the *hyperparameters* of the synthetic world — how many features, how much correlation, how deep the hierarchy — are guesses, and there may be phenomena in real networks nobody thought to include. They position the benchmark as a complement to real-model evaluation rather than a replacement, and note that the capacity mismatch they build in on purpose (narrower dictionary than truth) is itself a driver of feature mixing rather than a neutral control. The open problem they state is direct: no evaluated architecture achieves clean recovery, and that is the target.

## What we take from it

Method and measure, mainly. First, this is the concrete answer to the null-arm question that has dominated recent entries: the folder now has an instrument where reconstruction and recovery are *separately* computable, and the paper demonstrates that they diverge systematically by architecture and by data property — so any of our own claims split along the fidelity-versus-feature axis should be reported against a ground-truth-referenced recovery column whenever one exists. Second, the superposition-vs-explained-variance sweep is a reusable adversarial test: take any account we fit, increase the overlap of the underlying directions, and check whether its reconstruction improves while its recovery degrades. If it does, our account is exploiting interference rather than representing it. Third, two reporting habits worth copying: matching sparsity with a controller before comparing architectures (so a sparsity difference is not silently the explanation), and printing the supervised-probe upper bound alongside dictionary scores, since without it a 0.88 looks like success. Fourth, a warning that prices our own predictive ambition: the paper's lower-bound argument cuts both ways — if a dictionary cannot recover features cleanly in a world that satisfies its own assumption exactly, then a dictionary-based white-box account cannot be assumed to generalise to a model that does not satisfy it, and our predictors need the same ground-truth ladder this benchmark provides plus a real-model rung on top.
