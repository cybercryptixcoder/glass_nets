# Case Study: Runtime Safety Verification of Neural Network Controlled System

- **Key:** arxiv-2408.08592
- **Authors / organisation:** Frank Yang, Sinong Simon Zhan, Yixuan Wang, Chao Huang, Qi Zhu; Northwestern University (ECE), with the University of Southampton (Electronics and Computer Science)
- **Date:** 2024-08-16
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.08592
- **Kind:** paper (case study)
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Nearly all the reachability work this thread has read answers an offline question: given an initial set, compute a tube. This paper asks the operational version - can the same tool run inside the control loop, every tick, and change what the robot does? The setting is a Turtlebot navigating a walled arena on a LiDAR-fed neural steering controller, where obstacles appear that the controller was never trained on. Their specific contribution claim is narrow: show that a published polynomial-arithmetic reachability tool can be called at runtime, and wrap it in a switch that hands control to a classical obstacle-avoidance law whenever the tool says the next few tenths of a second are unsafe, then hands it back when they are not. The larger goal - stated in the introduction and again in the future-work paragraph - is the Simplex idea: keep an unverified high-performance controller in charge, and pay for safety with a verified fallback plus a decision rule that invokes it.

## What they studied
A differential-drive Turtlebot 3 Burger simulated in ROS2 Flatland, state (x, y, θ), inputs (linear velocity, angular velocity), with maximum speeds 0.22 m/s and 2.84 rad/s. The learned controller is tiny - 3 inputs to 2 outputs, two hidden layers of 64 ReLU units - trained by supervised regression on 100 expert trajectories recorded at 20 Hz from a goal-following planner, with no obstacles in the training environment. The unit of analysis is a time-stamped reachable set of the robot's pose over a short horizon, compared for intersection against the union of detected obstacle footprints, which are treated as the unsafe region. Nothing here is a feature, a direction or a circuit: the unit is a set of states, and the only thing read from the network is its input-output map.

## How they did it
The controller is queried each callback with the current localized pose; AMCL from the Nav2 stack supplies that pose from the map plus LDS-01 scans, and obstacle detections are turned into rectangles representing unsafe states. Given the current pose as a point, the tool computes an outer approximation of the reachable set over a configurable number of verification steps, defaulting to ten, using degree-2 Taylor models for the neural network and for the ODE flow, with the non-differentiable activation approximated by a low-order Bernstein polynomial; the tool's symbolic-remainder trick - carrying the linear map symbolically and evaluating the remainder only at the end - is described as the means of resisting the wrapping effect across steps. If the reachable set meets an obstacle footprint the loop switches to the fallback, a geometric controller that steers around the obstacle counterclockwise at a fixed standoff, with its desired speed and heading clipped to the same physical limits, and keeps re-running the analysis; when the set no longer intersects the obstacles the learned controller is reinstated. The fallback's own safety is assumed rather than verified, and the suspension is asserted to land the robot at a point from which the learned controller can resume. Two sweeps are run: the number of verification steps (10, 15, 20, 30) and combinations of Taylor-model degree with Bernstein order at a fixed 10 steps, the latter averaged over 10 trajectories, with the 0.2 s callback period used as a hard admissibility cut.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not an explanation of the network in any descriptive sense - there is no dictionary, no probe, no direction. What the tool returns is a certificate of the form "over the next N sampling periods, the pose cannot leave this box"; the paper's understanding of the system is entirely a sequence of such certificates stripped to their decision content (intersects the obstacle or does not). It is partial by construction and conservative in a way nobody measures. The predictive content is real but shallow: a set that excludes a region supports the claim that the robot cannot be there, and says nothing about what it will do there.

## How they judged success
Qualitatively and operationally: a navigation task completing with obstacle detours (single and multiple obstacles, plus a no-obstacle baseline), trajectories drawn with switching points marked, and reachable-set boxes overlaid at two horizons. Quantitatively, the step sweep yields per-call runtime and three whole-task cost figures (total task time, time under the fallback, and fallback utilisation as a share of the task), and the parameter sweep yields runtimes as a bar chart with only the 0.2 s real-time threshold drawn on it. The hardware is a laptop with an i7. There is no comparison against another reachability tool, no replay of the same obstacle course with different settings to check that the safety verdict was ever correct, no measurement of how conservative the sets are, and no test in which a collision occurs - the paper's safety evidence is that none did.

## What they found
With default settings (degree 2, Bernstein order 2, ten steps) the loop navigates the single- and multi-obstacle scenes and switches controllers as expected, and the learned controller regains control after each detour. Raising the verification horizon costs runtime in a near-linear way - 0.18 s per call at ten steps rising to 0.53 s at thirty - and each additional horizon lengthens the whole task (77.73 s to 89.31 s) while the fallback's share of the task rises from about 27% to about 32%, which the authors explain by the accumulated over-approximation triggering switches earlier and more often. In the parameter sweep, higher Taylor-model degree costs time, Bernstein order barely does, and only Taylor degrees up to 3 stay under the 0.2 s budget, so the real-time-feasible corner of the design space is a narrow one at this step count on this machine.

## Limits and open problems they name
They list three: the framework does not handle system uncertainty or stochastic policies; the verifier is called unconditionally each tick and could be scheduled opportunistically instead; and the switching logic is fixed and can in principle hand the robot to a fallback state from which the learned controller cannot recover, which they name as a real gap. Implicit limits that the experiments themselves show: the safe operation reported depends on a particular horizon and polynomial degree, the better part of a second of delay is admitted by the horizon (the 0.53 s setting), and every obstacle footprint is known exactly at the moment of the query because it comes from the robot's own perception - so the tool is never asked to handle a region it has not already been told about.

## What we take from it
A warning and a framing, both about what it means for a certificate to be *used*. First, the real-time constraint here is a wall-clock budget per tick, and the paper's own table shows the fidelity dial (horizon) spending that budget and then turning into task cost - worse behaviour, not less of it - which is the folder's fidelity-dial defect in its most legible form: the dial is set by the deadline, and the consequences of setting it long are stated in the same table as the consequences of setting it short, with no width measurement anywhere. Second, the loop's decision uses only the *sign* of one set-intersection test, so any conservatism in the enclosure becomes an unnecessary handover to the fallback; the utilisation column is therefore the closest thing in the folder to a measured price of looseness - it is the fraction of the task spent because the tool was unsure - and it is reported as if it were a property of the horizon. Third, the case study makes plain that the surrounding system is where the guarantees actually come from: the fallback is unverified, the resumption claim is asserted, and the safety evidence is a completed run, so the honest description of the pipeline is "a verified check on when to defer", not "a verified controller". For our own account the transferable rule is that a predictive model used in a loop should be evaluated on the decisions it changes - here handovers per task and the cost each one incurs - rather than on its per-query time, because time is only meaningful against a deadline.
