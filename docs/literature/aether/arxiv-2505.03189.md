# Patterns and Mechanisms of Contrastive Activation Engineering

- **Key:** arxiv-2505.03189
- **Authors / organisation:** Yixiong Hao, Ayush Panda, Stepan Shabalin (Georgia Institute of Technology); Sheikh Abdur Raheem Ali (independent)
- **Date:** 2025-05-06
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.03189
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (LaTeXML HTML, 44,264 clean chars; body, references and the appendix's section headers and dataset recipes read — the appendix's per-behaviour figure pages carry no prose in the strip)
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful) — the folder's first entry that prices the distributional precondition of every steering result it already holds

## What they wanted, and why
Steering by adding a contrastive direction to the residual stream is usually sold on its two cheapest properties: no training, and inference-time control of a fixed model. The authors want a deployment manual rather than a new technique — what a practitioner should expect when the vector is fitted on one distribution and used on another, how many contrast pairs are worth collecting, whether the model's general competence survives the edit, and whether the edit can be talked around. The larger goal is to move contrastive activation engineering (CAE) from a demonstration to a tool with known failure conditions, since the same lineage is being wired into products and safety monitors. Their framing sentence for the whole exercise is that steering may be a property of the dataset at least as much as of the model.

## What they studied
Llama 3 8B-Instruct and Llama 3 70B-Instruct. Nine steering targets: the five OCEAN personality traits, political bias, and three safety-flavoured ones (honesty, corrigibility, self-awareness) alongside power-seeking inclination. The unit of analysis is a single mean-difference direction over last-token residual activations at one layer, plus the layer and the multiplier at which it is injected. Data: Anthropic's model-written-evaluations (MWE) pairs for fitting and for the in-distribution test, a one-pair "ActAdd" direction as a low-information contrast, a 540-question synthetic out-of-distribution set generated by DeepSeek R1, the MMLU dev split as a capability probe, and Pile text and model-written questions for a perplexity analysis.

## How they did it
A steering vector is the mean over a fitting set of (last-token activation on the desired statement) minus (last-token activation on the undesired statement), added to every layer-`l` residual at multiplier alpha; the paper writes both the single-pair and the mean forms. In-distribution, they sweep layers at fixed strength plus-or-minus one and sweep strength to plus-or-minus ten at the chosen layer, scoring answer-matching on an MWE test split with greedy decoding so the runs are reproducible, and they vary the fraction of the pair set used to build the vector (twenty through one hundred percent, and in a second sweep one, two, three, five and upward, converging past a hundred samples). Out of distribution, they apply the same vectors at the same layers to two new splits, a two-option choice set and an open-ended set, each with ten short, ten medium and ten long prompts per behaviour (questions of roughly ten to twenty, twenty to thirty, and over thirty words), and score behaviour and coherence with Llama 3 70B-Instruct as a judge, multiplying the two into a combined score. Capability damage is measured as MMLU change at a fixed strength, and as a per-question change in the negative log-likelihood of the model's own baseline answer, cross-tabulated by steering target against behavior — an approximation to the KL between steered and baseline models on those questions. Finally they attack the vectors with evolutionary prompt optimisation (EPO), seeded from the string of a reflective preamble, to find inputs that flip the steered answer, and they check a French-language sparse-autoencoder feature on Pile text as a sanity case that the likelihood instrument fires where it should.

## What counts as an explanation here
None, deliberately. This is an evaluation-of-methods paper with no feature dictionary, circuit, or surrogate; the object it reports on is the vector-plus-layer-plus-multiplier recipe itself, and the "explanation" is a set of operating conditions under which that recipe's measured effect exists. Its predictions are conditional and blunt: steering should move behaviour when the test text is drawn like the fitting text, and should not otherwise; degradation should shrink with more fitting pairs and with model size; steering should raise perplexity on nearly every question. The authors explicitly decline the mechanistic question their title raises, saying the OOD failure was strong enough that they could not get to it.

## How they judged success
Change in the percentage of MWE items where the response matches the steered behaviour (against the unsteered baseline, per behaviour, then averaged across behaviours); for OOD, the product of a judge-rated behaviour score and a coherence score, read as a distribution's skew rather than a mean; MMLU dev accuracy against an unsteered baseline; per-question NLL difference against the unsteered model's own answer for the perplexity matrix; and adversarial flipping under EPO at reported cross-entropy. No significance tests, no confidence intervals, no seeds and no null vectors are reported; the controls that carry the argument are comparative curves (vector fitted on 20 vs 40 vs 60 vs 80 vs 100 percent of pairs; one-pair versus many-pair vectors; 8B versus 70B) rather than matched-random baselines.

## What they found
In distribution both models respond: the best layer is early-to-mid (15 for the 8B, 29 for the 70B), answer-matching rises for strengths between zero and plus two on the 8B and between zero and plus six on the 70B and falls beyond that, and fraction-of-pairs curves are near-indistinguishable across the twenty-to-one-hundred percent range, i.e. diminishing returns by about a hundred and sixty pairs, with sample-size convergence stated at around a hundred samples (the abstract says about eighty). The one-pair ActAdd vector, by contrast, transfers badly even onto MWE, which the authors read as evidence that effectiveness is a dataset-level property. Out of distribution, on the 540-question set, neither CAA nor ActAdd produces the right skew a working intervention would — no obvious effect at any strength between minus two and plus two — and the authors report this as the central negative result. Capability cost: MMLU degradation against baseline in percent rises sharply as the fitting set shrinks, minus 30.7 for one pair, minus 8.9 for two, minus 4.8 for three, then a positive 3.2 at five and roughly flat at 2.1 to 3.7 from thirteen pairs on for the 8B; the 70B is worse in every cell, minus 60.0 at one pair, minus 31.6, minus 17.3, minus 9.0, and never leaves the negative range, best around minus 7.4 at eighty-nine pairs (the text names eighty-nine and fifty-five as the best sizes for the 8B and 70B respectively). Perplexity: steering at strength one raises perplexity on almost every target-by-behaviour cell of the MWE question set, and on the model-written question set there is no detectable beneficial cell at all, so the edit trades one question's likelihood for another's rather than adding capability; the French-feature sanity check behaves — highest loss rise on a Dutch passage, highest loss drop on a French one. Adversarial: EPO finds reflective preambles that invert a steered answer, but at cross-entropy around 5.1 to 5.5, which the authors say never occurs by chance and which is high enough that they treat the attack as a demonstration rather than a practical threat.

## Limits and open problems they name
Their in-distribution metric is a ceiling-bounded change (a model already honest ninety-five percent of the time can move five points at most), which they concede adds noise. The OOD judge rests on an unverified assumption that a LLM can substitute for a human rater, and the OOD set is synthetic and not grounded in real user queries. Because the OOD effect was so weak they could not deliver the mechanistic insights the title promises; the extensions they list are an optimal vector-norm-to-residual-norm ratio, multi-behaviour steering, data-collection pipelines, and sweeps over SAE-based steering.

## What we take from it
A warning and a measurement discipline, both cheap to adopt. (1) **In-distribution accuracy is not evidence of a transferable mechanism.** Every steering and ablation number the folder has collected was measured on text like the text used to fit or to select it; this paper is the first here to build the shift explicitly and get nothing, and it lands after a run (2604.03867) where per-prompt layer routing was tuned on the same datasets it was scored on. Our white-box model should carry a stated fitting distribution and a matched but disjoint evaluation distribution for every steering or removal claim, and report the pair. (2) **Capability cost is a curve with a floor, and the floor is model-dependent.** The MMLU table shows the cost falling monotonically to a plateau as pairs increase, but the 70B's plateau sits seven to eight points below its own baseline while the 8B's crosses into positive territory — a bigger model being steered with a vector fitted to the same behaviour is not a cheaper edit, and a one-pair vector costs thirty to sixty points of MMLU, which is the strongest single argument in the folder for never reporting a steering result at its best layer without its size-matched cost. (3) **Report the norm of the edit.** Their own proposed follow-up — the ratio of steering-vector norm to residual norm that trades effect against degradation — is the parameter our model must publish beside every intervention, because a vector that works at strength one and destroys coherence at ten is a recipe whose usable dose is invisible in a table of effects. Warning about the evidence tier: the paper reports no seeds, intervals or nulls, and its OOD claim is a visual judgement about skew, so it is cited here as a direction of travel, not as a measured bound.
