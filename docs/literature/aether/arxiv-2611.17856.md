# DECODE: Diagnosing Interpretability Failures in Automated Interpretation Process

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.17856

## What they wanted, and why
The automated half of feature interpretability has become self-referential: a language model writes the explanation of a sparse-autoencoder feature, and another language model scores that explanation for faithfulness, with nobody in the loop between them. The authors' worry is not that this pipeline is biased in some direction but that it has no way of telling a decomposition failure from an explanation failure — a low score is one number pointing at two very different culprits, and the two call for opposite repairs (retrain the SAE, or regenerate the explanation). They want to put a human back in at the point where the diagnosis is made, and they want that human's time spent where it changes the answer rather than on the features that are already fine. The larger target is a diagnosis that is *attributable*: every feature ends in one of a small number of named categories, and each category points at the module that produced it. The setting is deliberately the one with no ground truth, since that absence is what made full automation attractive in the first place.

## What they studied
One SAE, fully: Gemma-2-9b, residual stream at layer 31 (roughly four-fifths of depth), a 3,584-dimensional activation decomposed into 16,384 features, with weights and per-feature evidence taken from Neuronpedia. The unit of analysis is the **feature** (and, in the first stage, the **pair of features** whose decoder directions are close). Each feature arrives with 20 activating examples sampled across four activation-strength quantiles, three independently generated explanations — Llama 3.1 70B run locally on the quantile-sampled examples, plus GPT-4o mini and Gemini 2.5 Flash-Lite explanations as hosted on Neuronpedia — and three interpretability scores per explanation (fuzz, detection, embedding), computed with Llama as the scorer. So each feature carries nine scores and one phrase-level consensus measure before a human looks at it.

## How they did it
The diagnosis is a three-stage cascade, each stage asking exactly one question, with each stage's survivors labelled and passed on. **Stage 1, structural soundness:** features are paired by decoder-vector cosine similarity (the interface screens on a draggable similarity threshold, default 0.40), and a pair above threshold is inspected side by side and labelled *incoherent splitting* or *coherent separation* — the distinction being whether the boundary between two neighbouring features falls on a conceptual line or on an arbitrary one, such as two features splitting "sta"-prefixed words so that one of them holds everything except the word "state". **Stage 2, explanation adequacy:** single features are checked against their own activating examples. The expert first induces the pattern from repeated n-grams without reading the explanations, then compares that pattern against the consensus clusters, then cross-checks against the per-explainer scores with 0.5 marked as the random baseline; the label is *well-explained* or *need revision*. **Stage 3, failure attribution:** a need-revision feature is assigned *noisy activation* (the activations themselves have no coherent pattern), *missed syntax* (the explanation omits a surface pattern such as a shared affix) or *missed context* (it omits a semantic or topic-level pattern), with an escape hatch back to *well-explained*. At every stage the human labels a small seed set and an SVM — prototype-first seeding from TypiClust, then uncertainty-first near the decision boundary, then disagreement-first against an MLP and a random-forest committee — propagates the labels to the remaining features, gated on a confidence histogram and a stability chart of per-iteration flip rates, with automatically assigned pairs down-weighted to 0.2 in later retraining. Stage 1 and Stage 2 both have a "safe default" button that sends unresolved units forward rather than excluding them.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **diagnostic label per feature, with the failing module named** — a five-category taxonomy rather than a text description, and explicitly partial: it certifies (or refuses to certify) that an explanation faithfully describes a feature's activation pattern and says where the failure came from. The taxonomy is the load-bearing object. It splits failures by *source* (SAE-driven: incoherent splitting, noisy activation; explainer-driven: missed syntax, missed context) and by *granularity* (surface pattern versus semantic pattern), and each cell is chosen because it corresponds to a different corrective action. The paper adds a phenomenon to the literature's vocabulary — **incoherent splitting**, a boundary between two split features that is arbitrary rather than conceptual — and is careful about what it does not claim: "coherent separation" means only that no incoherent split was found under the pairs examined, not that the feature is monosemantic.

## How they judged success
Three domain experts (one master's-level researcher with 1.5 years of interpretability work, two PhD students with three and one year) each ran the full pipeline in a three-hour session — ten-minute pre-interview, thirty-minute tutorial, about two hours of independent labelling, twenty-minute post-interview — and were compensated at 70 USD per hour. The outcome measure is **macro-F1 against a reference set of 233 features** that at least one expert had manually given a terminal label; that reference set was itself labelled independently by one language model (Claude Opus 4.8) whose rationale an author consulted while adjudicating a final label, over roughly eight hours, with the experts blinded. Four conditions are compared: a score-only baseline that applies the interface's own default thresholds as labelling rules, each individual expert (manual labels plus propagated ones), the three expert pairs, and the group of three preferring manual labels and resolving overlaps by majority. Macro-F1 rather than accuracy because the two rare categories account for 16 and 11 of the 233 features. Inter-expert agreement is reported as Cohen's κ. Labeling effort is reported per stage as minutes per unit.

## What they found
**Automation alone certifies what a human refuses.** The score-only baseline reaches macro-F1 0.36 while the three individual experts reach 0.40, 0.37 and 0.41, and the aggregate hides opposite error profiles: the baseline terminates most features as splitting or well-explained (77% and 84% recall on those two categories) but recovers only 10% of noisy-activation features, filing 58% of them as well-explained; the experts do the reverse (23–44% and 55–75% on the first two, 67–87% on noisy activation). The failure mode that automation most reliably misses is therefore the SAE-side one — the features whose activations simply do not hang together — because such a feature can still carry a fluent and specific-sounding explanation.

**Anchor to the frame and the error relocates.** Stage 3 is where both the humans and the propagation fail. Manual labels alone scored 0.60, 0.65 and 0.63 macro-F1, while the labels the classifiers propagated scored 0.29, 0.23 and 0.25 — and because active seeding deliberately selects ambiguous features, the reference set is skewed toward exactly the features propagation is worst at. Aggregation helps and helps for two separate reasons: the pairs reach 0.53, 0.54 and 0.50 and the group of three reaches 0.62, with coverage replacing propagated labels by manual ones (changing 259 labels for a net gain of 158 correct) and majority voting on the 33 multiply-labelled features doing the rest (21 changed, net gain 9). Experts agreed at κ .481–.663 and diverged mainly in strictness along the well-explained/noisy-activation boundary rather than on what the categories meant.

**The scores are not what the humans use, and using them first hurts.** Across all three sessions, noisy activation was the dominant failure category (41–51% well-explained, the rest mostly SAE-side), which is the actionable conclusion: retraining the SAE addresses the plurality of failures, and prompting the explainers differently would leave them alone. Incoherent splitting was rare (57, 18 and 42 features after propagation). Two expert sessions ignored the interpretability scores almost entirely — one dismissing them as a measure of the examples' ambiguity rather than the explanation's faithfulness — and the one session that read the consensus clusters before looking at the examples overrode three of its own labels, an anchoring effect the authors attribute to evidence order rather than to the clusters being wrong. Time per unit rose monotonically across stages for every expert (0.47–0.83, 0.75–0.93, 0.83–1.08 minutes).

## Limits and open problems they name
The reference set is an author's adjudicated reading rather than ground truth, and the group of experts still reaches only 0.62 macro-F1, with propagated labels at 0.23–0.29; fine-grained attribution, especially between missed syntax and missed context, is called unreliable. Every case study is one SAE configuration — one model, one layer, one width — with the explainers, metrics, default thresholds and classifier all fixed and no measurement of how diagnoses change under alternatives. Cold start and class imbalance are unsolved: two labels per rare category keeps the category present, not represented. The taxonomy is not claimed to be exhaustive (hedging, polysemanticity and readability are outside it), the classifier assumes similar profiles share a category, and less experienced users were not tested. No corrective loop is evaluated — the paper diagnoses and stops.

## What we take from it
(1) **A diagnostic label with a named culprit is a weak claim that is still much stronger than a score, and it is the form our own write-ups lack.** The paper's central move is to replace "this feature scores 0.31" with "this failure came from the decomposition, not the explainer", and then to show the two are not interchangeable: the score-only baseline is the single best demonstration in the folder that a faithfulness metric can certify a feature whose *activations* are incoherent, because the explanation is graded against the same evidence that produced it. For us the analogue is cheap — every probe or feature we report should carry a source label (direction not present / present but not read / read but not causally used) beside its number, and the categories should be chosen so that each implies a different fix. (2) **The pair test is a read-out our battery does not have.** Incoherent splitting is invisible per feature by construction: each feature's explanation is faithful and the failure lives in the relation between two of them. Any claim of the form "feature A means X" that we make from a single dictionary entry inherits the same blind spot, and the test is mechanical — find the nearest neighbours in decoder space and check whether the semantic boundary between them is a line a human would draw. (3) **Evidence order changes the answer, and that is a measurement hazard rather than a UX detail.** The expert who read the consensus clusters first overrode three of their own labels; the two who induced the pattern from activations first did not. Our own labelling of features, and any human evaluation we run of an explanation, has the same exposure, so the rule to copy is structural: force the raw evidence before the summary, and treat a human pass that saw the model's verdict first as a different instrument. (4) **Consensus is a proxy, and this paper prices it.** Independent explainers and independent metrics agree for reasons that include shared training data, so consensus cannot be validated by accumulating more of the same kind of voter — which is why the reference set is built by one model plus one human, and why the authors name self-preference (Llama scoring Llama's explanations) themselves. (5) **Warning, on propagation:** the beautiful interactive loop degrades exactly where the claim is finest. Group macro-F1 is 0.62 while its constituent manual labels were 0.60–0.65 and its propagated ones 0.23–0.29, so scaling human judgement over 16,384 features is affordable only for the coarse question ("which module fails most") and not for the fine one ("which of these two explainer-side categories"). That is the honest shape of any active-learning-style shortcut in our own pipelines, and it is the second entry in the folder to show a propagated or selected set retaining its size while losing its content.
