# Contraction-Guided Adaptive Partitioning for Reachability Analysis of Neural Network Controlled Systems

- **Key:** arxiv-2304.03671
- **Authors / organisation:** Akash Harapanahalli, Saber Jafarpour, Samuel Coogan; Georgia Institute of Technology (School of Electrical and Computer Engineering)
- **Date:** 2023-04-07
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03671
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Interval reachability for a closed loop — a nonlinear plant driven by a neural network controller under disturbance — is cheap but loose, and the looseness compounds along the trajectory (the wrapping effect). The usual cure is to partition the initial set into many boxes and take the union of their enclosures, but the standard recipes partition *uniformly over the initial set*, which spends equal effort where the error is small and where it is large. The authors want the partitioning decision itself to be driven by a measurable property of the dynamics: cut only where the box is not shrinking fast enough, and only at the times when it is not shrinking. The larger goal is tight enclosures at runtime cost, since their stated target is online verification rather than offline certification.

## What they studied
Continuous-time or discrete-time nonlinear plants of the usual state/control/disturbance form, with a fully connected feed-forward network as feedback controller — any activation, not ReLU-only — applied through a zero-order hold over a control period. The unit of analysis is a box (an interval vector) of states, plus a tree of such boxes created by splitting. Their concrete instantiation pairs a mixed-monotone plant reachability routine (ReachMM, from the group's own earlier work) with CROWN-style linear bound propagation on the network through auto_LiRPA. Experiments: a 4-state kinematic bicycle with a 4x100x100x2 ReLU controller imitating an obstacle-avoiding MPC; and a 2-state discrete double integrator from the ReachLP literature with a 2x10x5x1 ReLU net.

## How they did it
The base machinery is a closed-loop *embedding system*: a 2n-dimensional interval dynamics built from a decomposition function of the plant, in which the network call appears only through an interval enclosure of its output — so the whole closed loop can be pushed forward by integrating one trajectory of a box, and that box provably contains the reachable set. The contribution is the loop around it. For each partition over a control interval, the algorithm integrates the embedding to a fraction γ of the interval and measures the width ratio against the partition's own starting width — that ratio is the partition's empirical contraction rate. If the projected width at the end of the interval (the rate raised to the power 1/γ, scaled by the starting width) still exceeds a user-set target width ε, the algorithm *rewinds* to the start of the interval and bisects the box into 2^n sub-boxes, recursing. Crucially, whether a new sub-box recomputes the network enclosure is a separate depth budget: the network verification is cached and inherited down the tree until a network-depth limit is hit, so most partitions inherit one enclosure instead of asking the verifier again. Integrations happen only at leaves; the parents' enclosures are built as the tightest enclosing box of their children — so there is no chain of Minkowski sums along the tree. Final output is the union over leaves. Because the recursion rewinds rather than continuing from the over-wide box, the same step's work is either accepted or redone at finer resolution, and the decision is made with a fraction of a step's integration.

## What counts as an explanation here
A certified box trajectory: an interval-valued over-approximation of the reachable set at every time, plus the partition tree that produced it. The explanation of *why the box is where it is* is the contraction rate — a per-partition scalar computed from a short partial integration — and the paper's theoretical section makes that scalar the subject: an accuracy bound in which the enclosure error is bounded by decaying terms driven by the initial-set width and by the network's bound gap, each multiplied by the contraction rate of the closed-loop embedding. There is also a second-order statement: an upper bound on that contraction rate composed from the plant's open-loop contraction rate and the network's input-output Lipschitz constant. So the account is predictive in a narrow, quantitative sense — it says how fast the enclosure error decays, and what it decays to — but it says nothing about the network beyond its Lipschitz summary.

## How they judged success
Runtime (mean and standard deviation over 100 runs) against set quality, where quality is measured both as the area of the final reachable set (computed exactly with geometry libraries, so the comparison is against ground truth rather than between approximations) and as the volume of the tightest enclosing box at the final time. Baselines on the double integrator are the four named partitioning schemes of the prior literature, each run in two hyper-parameter settings, plus their own non-adaptive ReachMM at matched depth budgets on the vehicle. Ablations are over the three knobs: target width ε, partition depth, network-verification depth. The theory side offers two theorems — the accuracy decomposition (from a Grönwall-type argument on the distance between the embedding's box and a true trajectory) and the contraction-rate bound.

## What they found
The theoretical claim is that the three design features correspond one-to-one with the three terms of their error bound: separating the verifier from the partition count improves the term that does not involve the network, spatial choice of where to cut improves the local coupling constants (each partition defines its own region, hence its own constants), and temporal choice improves the exponential growth factor. The contraction rate is literally the same object on both the embedding and the enclosure, so a negative rate means the initial-set part of the error decays to zero. On the double integrator the tighter of their two settings reaches a final-set area of roughly 7.5e-3 in about 0.83 s, while the strongest listed baseline needs on the order of 3.7 s for 1.2e-2 — a smaller set for a fifth of the time, which is the shape of the claim their abstract summarises as a sizable accuracy gain at a fraction of the runtime; their looser setting runs in about 0.08 s with an area of 1.0e-1, roughly double the loosest baselines' 1.5e-1. On the vehicle the adaptive variants are faster than the non-adaptive ones at matched depth limits (roughly 1.24-1.58 s versus 1.85 s at the shallow setting, and 2.64-3.33 s versus 4.27 s at the deeper one) and the tightest of them also yields the smallest enclosing volume at the shallow setting, though not uniformly across all rows.

## Limits and open problems they name
The guarantees are stated for a continuously applied controller, while the algorithm as run uses zero-order hold — the theory section says the piecewise-constant assumption was dropped for simplicity of analysis, which leaves a stated modelling gap between proof and experiment. The contraction-rate computation is itself an over-approximation obtained from a network Lipschitz bound, so the guarantee is only as tight as that number. Their own discussion concedes the third knob is a heuristic budget: partition depth and network depth are user-set, and there is no procedure given for choosing ε. The evaluation is two small systems with small networks; no large-network or high-dimensional benchmark, no comparison on a verification task (safety yes/no), and no measurement of how much the caching of network enclosures costs in tightness — which is the explicit trade the method makes.

## What we take from it
A method and a measure. The measure is the useful half: a *local* rate — a width ratio over a short forward push — computed from the object itself rather than from a global constant, used to decide where to spend compute. That is the first thing in this folder's closed-loop thread that turns a reachability diagnostic into an allocation policy, and it is exactly the quantity runs 627 and 626 concluded was missing from the field's reporting: fidelity printed beside the regularity constant. The method is the second half — separating the expensive sub-computation (network enclosure) from the cheap one (plant integration) with independent depth budgets, so accuracy can be bought in the currency that is actually cheap. Warning to carry: the caching means a partition's enclosure may be computed on a *larger* box than the partition itself, and the paper treats this as a pure saving without reporting what it costs, so our own account must state which region each enclosure was certified on rather than which region it is being used for. Cross-reference run 629's Type 1/2/3 rule — both say a summary is only valid on the region that produced it; this paper's tree is the concrete place where those regions differ.
