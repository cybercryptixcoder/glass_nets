- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Modern networks built from convex piecewise-affine nonlinearities are splines: each layer partitions its input space and applies a different affine map on each piece. Prior work had established this correspondence and had bounded how many pieces there can be, but almost nothing was known about the *shape* of the pieces - which points share a region, where the boundaries lie, and how one layer's partition composes with the next. The authors take a computational-geometry route rather than a learning-theory one: they want the closed-form geometry of the partition induced by a single unit, by a whole layer, and by the composed network, plus a formula for the classification boundary and a scalar measure of its curvature. The stated motivation is that if the partition is the mechanism ("the magic" of the architecture, in their framing), then describing it analytically is the prerequisite for saying anything mechanistic about the network at all. It is a foundations paper, not an empirical one, and its descendants in this folder inherit its objects rather than its claims.

## What they studied
Any network whose nonlinearities are piecewise-affine and convex: ReLU, leaky-ReLU, absolute value, max-pooling, and their compositions in fully connected, convolutional and residual architectures. No specific models are named as study subjects - the unit of analysis is the **partition region** (equivalently, in the network's terms, a pattern of which side of each unit's kink an input falls on), for one unit, one layer, and a full L-layer composition. The treated dimensionality is small: the worked visualisations use layers mapping R^2 to R^6 to R^6 to R^1 (Figure 3), and the only trained model mentioned is a ResNet on CIFAR-10 used to plot centroids (Figure 4).

## How they did it
The derivation proceeds in three steps. **(1) One unit.** View a max-affine spline unit as the maximum of R affine functions of the input; the unit's output is therefore the upper boundary of an intersection of R half-spaces - a convex polytope in the input-output space. Lemma 1 says the vertical projection of that polytope's faces onto the input space is a partition. Theorem 2 then gives the partition's parameters in closed form: it is a **power diagram** - a Voronoi diagram with a per-site radius (Laguerre distance) - whose centroids are the unit's slope vectors and whose radii are a simple function of the unit's slopes and biases. Corollary 1: cells are convex polytopes. **(2) One layer.** Concatenating K such units multiplies the region count to R^K, and Theorem 3 gives the layer power diagram's centroids as the sum over units of the selected slope vectors, with a radius built from the summed biases and the centroid norm. **(3) Composition.** They define a recursion: the first layer's partition; then each of its cells is subdivided by the second layer's diagram, pulled back to the input space through the first layer's affine map on that cell. Theorem 5 gives the recursion's parameters, which are recursively multiplied transposes of the per-cell affine matrices and recursively accumulated biases - so the composed partition is a multiscale power-diagram subdivision of the input space. They also show (Lemma 5, Theorem 6) that the network's own feedforward pass, read as a cell-classification problem, has cost linear in the per-layer budget rather than exponential in the region count. Finally (§5.3, Proposition 3) the centroid of the composed region containing a point is the Jacobian-transpose of the composed map times the all-ones vector - i.e. obtainable by backpropagation, and recognisable as a sum of per-unit saliency maps. From there they derive the decision boundary (Theorem 7, 8) as the level set of a product of unit pre-activations, and a boundary curvature as the dihedral angle between adjacent regions' hyperplanes.

## What counts as an explanation here
A closed-form geometric object: a partition of the input space into convex convex-polytope cells, given analytically in terms of the network's weights and biases, at every level (unit, layer, composition), plus a closed-form decision boundary and a curvature scalar. It is the replacement-model flavour of explanation - the network is re-described exactly, with no approximation and no data, as a multiscale power diagram plus region-dependent affine maps - and it is complete in the sense that nothing is left out of the re-description. Whether it *predicts* anything is the interesting edge: it predicts the shape of the classification boundary (which is testable against a rendered boundary) and it predicts that certain weight constraints produce certain partition geometries (Examples 1-2), and it predicts a monotone relation between ReLU firing density and boundary curvature (Proposition 6). It does not predict any trained network's behaviour on data from its weights alone beyond that.

## How they judged success
There is no benchmark, no baseline and no metric in the quantitative sense. The arguments are proofs (theorems, lemmas, corollaries with proofs in Appendix C, some resting on cited classical-geometry results such as the chordale property of power diagrams and the polytope-face projection lemma). The empirical component is illustrative: a rendered partition for a 3-layer visualisation showing the subdivision being built layer by layer with the newly introduced cuts highlighted; a centroid trajectory for one input through successive refinements of a trained ResNet on CIFAR-10; log-distance-to-nearest-boundary distributions through epochs and through layers for MLP and CNN models on CIFAR-10 and SVHN, with and without data augmentation; and a count of how many inputs share a region. There is a sanity claim that near-state-of-the-art performance is obtained by the networks used, but no numbers are tabulated, and no figure is quantified anywhere in the text.

## What they found
The main content is the derivation itself: unit, layer and network partitions are all power diagrams, the network's is a recursive subdivision, the recursion's parameters are explicit affine pulls-backs, and the classification boundary is the last layer's edge pulled back into input space with curvature given by a dihedral angle between adjacent regions' normal vectors. Several consequences are stated as results in their own right. Because a single unit's parameters determine many composed regions' centroids and radii, individual regions cannot be edited in isolation - they read this operationally as a recovery of weight tying and implicit regularisation. The boundary's curvature is invariant to rescaling of the parameters, which they use to argue that input-output sensitivity, gradient norm or Lipschitz constant alone cannot characterise boundary regularity. For ReLU they derive that curvature is larger where fewer ReLUs fire, and that absolute value gives a different angle range than ReLU. On the empirical side they report that training changes where region boundaries fall only mildly (the distribution of distances to the nearest boundary shifts slightly, with more inputs close to a boundary) and that the dominant effect of learning is on the *shaping* of regions through weights; that deeper layers appear to spend their subdivisions refining some regions more than others, which they read as effort concentrated on hard examples; and that in their standard trained networks at reported near-state-of-the-art accuracy, no two images ever shared a partition region, whereas shrinking the network produced sharing in early subdivision steps. The compositional cost result (Theorem 6) is their explanation for why such fine partitioning is affordable at test time.

## Limits and open problems they name
Explicitly: a preprint under review; the treatment is for piecewise-affine and convex nonlinearities only (softmax is handled by citing a companion paper, not proved here); the boundary curvature is derived for binary classification and R = 2 nonlinearities, with multi-class deferred by notation; visualisations are in two input dimensions because the object is not renderable otherwise. Beyond what they name, and worth recording because this paper is cited by later work for objects it does not itself measure: **(a)** the radius parameter is left without an operational meaning - this is precisely the gap the 2026 Linear Centroids Hypothesis entry (arxiv-2604.11962) inherits and names as its own limitation, so the "unstudied radius" problem is 2019-original, not a later oversight; **(b)** the two quantitative-looking empirical claims ("never more than one image per region", "curvature is larger where fewer ReLUs fire") are reported as prose over unquantified figures, with no model names, sizes, seed counts, or number of regions measured - the region counts are described as exponential in layer width and no effective or realised count is given; **(c)** the pre-training network used for the centroid/partition figures beyond the CIFAR-10 ResNet is described only in an appendix cross-reference that this version resolves to a placeholder ("appendix ??" in the submitted text), so its architecture and accuracy are not recoverable from this version; **(d)** the partition's exponential region count is never reconciled with the known empirical finding that trained networks use far fewer regions than the upper bound, which is the question that determines whether the object is a description or an idealisation.

## What we take from it
This is the definitional root of the object four recent entries in this folder were measuring - the linear centroid. Three things carried forward. **A formula to reuse:** the centroid of the region containing an input is `J^T 1` at that input (eq. 20, Proposition 3), which needs no region enumeration and is one backward pass. That is the only reason the centroid became a practical interpretability object at all, and every "centroid direction" result in this folder traces to this line. **A framing worth adopting:** a network is a partition plus per-region affine maps, so an intervention can be described as either *moving a region's boundary* or *changing a region's map*, and these are different causal objects. Every steering-vector and ablation result we hold collapses the two; naming which one an experiment moves is a cheap way to make our interventions comparable. **A warning that has already bitten the folder:** the composed region's radius grows with depth in a way the authors note pushes the centroid outside its own region, and the radius is left unstudied - so any method that reports centroid *directions* while ignoring radii is reporting half of a two-parameter object, and the half it drops is the one that decides whether the centroid is even inside the tile it is supposed to name. Two secondary takeaways: their curvature result (normalised directions, hence a boundary regularity measure that is invariant to parameter scale) is a template for reporting a shape statistic that cannot be inflated just by making weights bigger; and the compositional-cost theorem is a reminder that the network's feedforward pass *is* a cell lookup, which is the cleanest formal statement available that a white-box model of a network should be organised as a partition, not as a feature list.
