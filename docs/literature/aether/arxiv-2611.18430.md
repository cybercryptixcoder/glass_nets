# Validating Graph Neural Network Feature Attribution Through Local Perturbations: A Study on CoMoFeNiCu Decomposition Enthalpy

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.18430
- **Authors / organisation:** Miguel Tenorio, James Chapman — Department of Mechanical Engineering, Boston University
- **Date:** 2026-11-13
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18430
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (main body end to end; supplementary figures S1–S7 not fetched, so no number is taken from them)
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Materials scientists read graph neural networks by ranking input descriptors — element identities, pairwise bonds, bond angles — by how much of the prediction each carries, and then draw chemical conclusions from that ranking. The authors' worry is the one this survey keeps meeting: an attribution faithfully reports whatever the network actually relies on, including spurious correlations, and in real materials datasets there is no ground truth against which to check. Their proposal is to stop looking for ground truth and instead build a setting where a *known structural fact* — the model's own interaction range — must show up in the attribution if the attribution is reading the local environment at all. A methods-validation study is the deliverable, not a materials discovery.

## What they studied
One chemistry (Co–Mo–Fe–Ni–Cu high-entropy alloy) at three stoichiometries that vary only cobalt content (15, 35 and 55 at.% Co, with Fe, Ni, Cu fixed at 10% each), 500 random configurations per stoichiometry. The unit of analysis is the *descriptor instance* inside a message-passing graph: stoichiometric features (which element an atom is), second-body features (bond types), third-body features (bond-angle types). Targets are DFT mixing enthalpies computed along binary and ternary decomposition pathways (~877 binary and ~4,442 ternary pathway graphs, of which a random half of the ternary set was used). Models are 6-layer ALIGNN-style edge-gated GNNs, 100 channels, 3.5 Å radial cutoff, trained deliberately to overfit.

## How they did it
Structures are generated, embedded by an *untrained* 6-layer GNN encoder, projected with UMAP and sampled on a grid so the DFT budget covers the spread of the latent space rather than its dense centre. Mixing enthalpy is the parent alloy's total energy minus the sum of its decomposition products, and the network is fed the whole pathway as a union of disconnected subgraphs, so the model sees phases and parent together. The output head is constrained: decoder weights are forced positive and normalised so they sum to the predicted enthalpy, which makes each feature's decoder output a contribution that is additive by construction. Per-feature scores are divided by the predicted value, summed over all instances of a chemical motif, then normalised so a motif's fractional share of its descriptor class sums to one; that share is the quantity plotted throughout. Two models are trained, one on binary and one on ternary pathways, then cross-evaluated on the other pathway type. The validation move is the atomic swap: take one structure, exchange a high-scoring atom with a low-scoring one of a different species (Co with Fe in the Co-rich structure, Co with Mo in the balanced one, in both directions), re-run the model on the perturbed structure, and plot the mean change in score per neighbour shell measured outward from the swapped site. If the ranking is reading local chemistry under a 3.5 Å cutoff, the score change must be largest at the swapped site and die within a shell or two.

## What counts as an explanation here
A rank ordering of descriptor types by fractional contribution to the predicted enthalpy, read as chemistry: compute, then say which element or which bond-angle triplet carries the prediction. It is a weight-derived dictionary over hand-specified descriptor classes rather than a learned feature basis; nothing is discovered that a chemist could not have named in advance. Its predictive content is the claim that the ranking is a property of the *model's* local environment, testable by a stated decay law, and that the ranking of the dominant axis survives a change of training distribution even when the numbers do not.

## How they judged success
Fit on in-distribution test sets (R², mean absolute error in meV per atom, and a "hit rate" for correctly calling thermodynamic stability), cross-pathway extrapolation (R², best-fit MAE after allowing a slope correction, hit rate), and — the actual validation — the *shape* of the score change as a function of neighbour-shell index after a single swap, compared against the zero line and against the model's stated cutoff. Qualitative agreement between the descriptor rankings of the binary-trained and ternary-trained models on the same structures is offered as a second consistency check. There is no shuffled-score control, no randomly initialised encoder comparison, no random-swap baseline and no significance test: the decay is judged by eye against the cutoff it is supposed to match.

## What they found
Both models fit their own pathway type well (R² 0.99; MAE 14.26 and 7.70 meV/atom; hit rates 100% and 99.8%), and the cross-pathway transfer is lopsided: the binary-trained model on ternary data collapses (R² 0.308, best-fit MAE 665 meV/atom after a slope correction of −0.301, hit rate 13.6%), while the ternary-trained model on binary data keeps the trend (R² 0.943, best-fit MAE 110 meV/atom, hit rate 92.1%) — yet the paper's own discussion quotes the poorer figure for that same evaluation as R² 0.26, so the two statements in the main text disagree and the entry records both rather than picking one. Rankings: cobalt's share rises monotonically with cobalt content and overtakes molybdenum between the 15% and 35% subsets; Fe, Ni and Cu stay low at every composition; among bond types, Co–Mo and Mo–Mo lead at low Co and Co–Co leads at 55%; among triplets, mixed Mo–Co–Co and Co–Co–Mo dominate throughout, and three-body descriptors rank above two-body ones. The swap experiment gives the largest score change at shell 0 — the swapped atom itself — with the change reducing toward zero within two to three shells, and the sign flipping sensibly (a high-scoring atom replaced by a low-scoring one drops, the reverse rises). The prediction, meanwhile, barely moves: the Co-rich structure's DFT enthalpy shifts from 0.197 to 0.198 eV/atom while the model's own output moves 0.208 → 0.186, and the balanced structure's DFT enthalpy does not move at all (−0.707 to −0.707) while the model moves from −0.707 to about −0.693.

## Limits and open problems they name
Single chemistry, one alloy family, three stoichiometries, one architecture, and an output head whose positivity constraint the authors themselves flag as a possible reason the attributions look clean — they say they intend to test that. No ground truth for the ranking exists; the swap test validates *locality*, not correctness, and the authors concede extrapolation is out of scope. The paper has no separate limitations section, no controls, and no quantitative criterion for "the decay matches the cutoff"; the supplementary material promised for four of the seven figures does not appear in the version read.

## What we take from it
Three things, one of them a warning aimed at us. **A method:** a structural perturbation with a *predicted shape* is a cheap and strong validation of an attribution — swap one input element, re-run, and check that the effect is localised at the edited site and decays over the model's receptive field. It needs no labels and no retraining, and the prediction is refutable in advance; for our battery the analogue is perturbing one token or one activation site and testing that the internal readout moves locally where a probe claims it is computed. **A measure to invert:** the paper reports a large internal reshuffle against a near-zero output change and treats the output stability as reassurance ("the model captures global properties while retaining atom-specific details"), when the same pair of numbers is the folder's clearest small-scale instance of an attribution that moves while the prediction does not — composition-preserving swaps leave the global stoichiometry untouched, so a model keyed on composition barely changes, and the shell-0 concentration is what a purely per-atom additive decoder has to produce regardless of chemistry. The correct reading is that the test validates the *architecture's* locality, not the learning, and the missing control — a randomly initialised encoder, or a swap between two atoms of the same species, re-scored the same way — is exactly the one that would separate the two. **A control for our own read-outs:** because the decoder is additive by construction, a per-atom score that is largest at the atom you edited is guaranteed for any positive decoder; a battery that reports such a result owes the shuffled or architecture-only baseline beside it.
