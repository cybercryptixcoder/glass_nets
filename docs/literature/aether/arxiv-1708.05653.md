# Symmetric Rank Covariances: a Generalised Framework for Nonparametric Measures of Dependence

- **Key:** arxiv-1708.05653
- **Authors / organisation:** Luca Weihs and Mathias Drton (Department of Statistics, University of Washington, Seattle); Nicolai Meinshausen (Seminar for Statistics, ETH Zürich)
- **Date:** 2017-08-18 (v1, single version)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1708.05653
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (v1 HTML incl. body §§1–6, Appendix A's U-statistic theory, Appendix B's proof of the new proposition and the end of Appendix C on range-tree algorithms, and the reference list; the middle of Appendix B, the proofs of the remaining propositions and lemmas, was skimmed rather than read line by line)
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

The dependence measures an interpretability report reaches for — rank correlation between a probe's score and a label, a rank-based independence screen between two activation streams — are chosen from a menu that has never been organised. The authors want the organising principle. Their observation is that three unrelated-looking statistics (Kendall's tau, Hoeffding's D, and the Bergsma–Dassios sign covariance) turn out to be the *same construction* with different ingredients, and they want to say exactly what the construction is, which of the field's favourite measures fall inside it, which fall just outside, and what the construction buys in exchange. The larger goal is a taxonomy with proofs: every member is automatically invariant to any strictly increasing change of coordinates and automatically zero under independence, so any new measure invented by filling in one slot inherits both properties for free, and the question of whether a test built on it can detect dependence becomes a separate, concrete question about that slot.

## What they studied

No model, no data set: random vectors X in r dimensions and Y in s dimensions, and their joint law. The unit of analysis is a dependence functional of that law, estimated from n i.i.d. copies. The objects manipulated are *joint ranks* — the rank matrix of a tuple of observations, computed coordinate by coordinate — and permutations of a four- (or more) element tuple drawn from the sample. Dimensions r and s are arbitrary and are the paper's central generalisation: most of the classical measures it subsumes were defined for one coordinate on each side.

## How they did it

1. State three properties a dependence measure should have — zero under independence, nonzero under dependence, and invariance under strictly increasing maps — and note that the field's popular measures each satisfy only a subset (Kendall's tau and Spearman's rho are nonparametric and independent-zero but not dependence-detecting; distance correlation is dependence-detecting but not nonparametric).
2. Write Kendall's tau, Hoeffding's D and the sign covariance each as an expectation of a product, where each factor is a *signed sum of a 0/1 rank indicator over a permutation subgroup*. Identify the subgroup: the sign covariance and D both live on the four-element group generated by swapping the first pair and the second pair. This is the paper's central observation, and it is the content of its title.
3. Define the class by that shape — an indicator on each side, a common tuple order, and a subgroup with as many even as odd permutations — and prove that any such object is nonparametric and zero under independence. Show products stay in the class, which is what licenses squaring.
4. Recover Hoeffding's D and a variant the authors call R as members, and note that Spearman's rho is a *sum* of members rather than a member, because its signs do not follow the permutation parity.
5. Explain the class as testing independence of *binarized* versions of the two vectors: cut each vector at a point, look at the resulting 2^r by 2^s table of joint probabilities, and integrate the squared 2-by-2 minors of that table over cutpoints. The choice of integration measure is what separates D (weight by the joint law) from R (weight by the product of marginals), and R is proved D-consistent for every law, which D is not.
6. Derive multivariate extensions of the sign covariance by choosing new rank indicators, and exhibit one of them (the "joint" extension) failing dependence-detection on a parity construction while the other ("partial") passes every simulation — flagged as a conjecture, not a theorem.
7. Estimate every member by a U-statistic — unbiased by construction — and note the estimation cost is a sum over every four-element tuple, which is hopeless directly.
8. Replace the direct sum with counting: pretabulate a prefix-sum tensor so that "how many observations fall in this box" becomes a constant-time query, or build a range tree over the pairs when the dimension makes the tensor too big, and reduce the count-per-tuple to a handful of box queries by inclusion-exclusion.
9. Derive the null distribution: show that a group-theoretic condition on the indicator's invariance group forces the U-statistic to be degenerate of order two under independence, so its limit is a weighted sum of one-degree-of-freedom chi-squares rather than Gaussian, and compute the limiting law explicitly for D, R and the sign covariance — all three turn out to be the same random variable up to a constant (36/pi^4 for the sign covariance against 1/pi^4 for the other two).
10. Simulate power against the distance covariance on four families — two continuous, two discrete including parity, one mixed — and simulate run time against sample size in two and three dimensions.

## What counts as an explanation here

A taxonomy with certificates, not a model explanation. The form is: a parametrised class of functionals, a proof that every member has the two properties people want, a proof that a subclass detects all dependence, an estimator with a computational bound, and a null law. It is deliberately partial in one direction and the authors say so — detection guarantees are given for the subclass (and conjectured for the multivariate sign extension), and explicit null distributions are given only in the bivariate continuous case. Predictive content: the bias and null statements predict the distribution of the statistic on independent data, which is exactly the quantity a significance screen needs.

## How they judged success

(i) The two consistency properties, proved for the class and for the summing subclass; (ii) empirical power at the nominal 0.05 level against the distance covariance, 1,000 samples per point, on four named joint distributions, with one deliberate stress test that applies a monotone map to the response to show that only the nonparametric measures are unaffected; (iii) asymptotic run time in big-O for naive versus range-query versions at two and three dimensions, with the crossover sample size read off a plot (the authors report that at three dimensions the tree only wins above roughly 3,000 points); (iv) the distance between finite-sample and asymptotic null distributions, by total-variation distance between kernel density estimates at five sample sizes, with agreement judged good from about n = 60.

## What they found

- Kendall's tau, its square, the Bergsma–Dassios sign covariance, Hoeffding's D and D's product-weighted variant R are all members of one class, and the subgroup that appears in all of them is the one generated by the two pairwise swaps — the same one that arises when the squared deviation of the joint law from the product of marginals is expanded.
- R is proved to be dependence-detecting for every law, resolving what the authors say they could not find a reference for; D is not, on a two-point counterexample where the variables are perfectly dependent and D is exactly zero.
- Under independence the estimators of D, R and the sign covariance are degenerate of order two and share one limiting law up to a constant, which the paper derives rather than observes — Nandy and co-authors had observed the coincidence for two of the three.
- Spearman's rho is provably a sum of two members but not a member, because its negated terms do not respect permutation parity; the authors state they cannot prove conclusively that it is outside the class.
- One multivariate extension of the sign covariance is provably blind: on a parity construction with an even number of Bernoulli coordinates predicting their exclusive-or, it is identically zero, and its simulated power sits at the nominal level; with an odd number of coordinates the same construction gives it power. The other extension detects both, and the paper conjectures rather than proves that it is dependence-detecting in general.
- Power: no single test dominates. The nonparametric measures were often close to or better than distance covariance even though they tolerate a much wider class of transforms, and the monotone distortion of the response cut distance covariance's power substantially while leaving the others unchanged by construction.
- Cost: the efficient counting methods are asymptotically much better than summing over tuples (a fifth power becomes roughly n log-squared in the bivariate case), but the constants are large and the advantage shrinks with dimension; the authors print the cases where the naive method is faster.

## Limits and open problems they name

The null theory is bivariate only — the authors say the multivariate case is significantly harder because the marginals themselves now influence the limit, and show a plot in which the correlation between two response coordinates visibly changes the finite-sample distribution, so they leave it open. Every multivariate detection guarantee is a conjecture except for the summing subclass. There are no explicit asymptotic distributions for the multivariate estimators, so the simulations fall back on a reference distribution drawn from the estimated marginals rather than a true permutation null, justified only by "results generalise well" in small trials. The efficient counting methods do not generalise to arbitrary indicators — the class is wider than the algorithms. And the power study is 50 points per setting for the continuous cases, with the discrete cases at 16 to 48, which is small for the comparisons being drawn.

## What we take from it

- **A framing, and the strongest one in this arc: a dependence measure should be specified as a slot-filling choice, not as a formula.** Name the rank indicator (which ordering pattern counts), the permutation subgroup (with equal even and odd members), and the integration measure (joint or product of marginals). That triple fixes the invariance properties automatically, and then the only substantive question left is detection. Every "we correlated the probe with the label" number in this folder implicitly made that triple choice without naming it.
- **A warning that survives everything else: the invariance we want is exactly what makes a statistic blind.** The multivariate joint extension is invariant to coordinate-wise increasing maps *and* to a great deal more, and that extra invariance is what zeroes it on parity. The class has a monotone-robustness payoff (the distance-covariance stress test shows the price of not having it) and a detection cost, and the two are the same coin. For activations this is not hypothetical: exclusive-or-like constructions over coordinate sets are the standard thing a network learns, so a maximally invariant rank screen can be systematically blind exactly where structure is nonlinear.
- **A method worth copying in how they made the estimator cheap without changing the estimand.** Because the statistic depends on the data only through joint ranks, "how many points fall in this box" is the only quantity needed, and a prefix-sum tensor or a range tree answers that in constant or logarithmic time; inclusion-exclusion then turns each tuple's contribution into a fixed number of box queries. Any statistic written as a count over an ordering pattern can be computed this way, so the barrier to a rank screen over many simultaneous directions is engineering, not statistics — but the paper is also honest that the constants eat the asymptotics at the dimensions we actually live in.
- **A caution about our own nulls.** These statistics are degenerate of order two under independence, so their limit is a chi-square mixture and not a Gaussian; the authors confirm this both analytically and empirically. Any of our rank-based screens that standardises by a root-n variance and calls it a z-score is using the wrong null shape.
