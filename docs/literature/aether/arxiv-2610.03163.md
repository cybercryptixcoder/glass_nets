# Predicting Steering Vectors and Adapter Weights for Few-Shot Author-Style Transfer

- **Key:** arxiv-2610.03163
- **Authors / organisation:** Leonard Popp, Danni Liu, Supriti Sinhamahapatra & Jan Niehues; Karlsruhe Institute of Technology (KIT)
- **Date:** 2026-10-02
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03163
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

The paper asks whether an author's writing style can be conditioned into a frozen language model from three example texts, with no per-author fitting, no retrieval corpus and no stored per-user artifact. The cold-start personalisation setting is what makes it hard: a new author has no history, so per-user LoRA and history retrieval are both unavailable, and three abstracts are too few for in-context extraction to work. Scientific abstracts sharpen the problem, since formal conventions narrow the surface variation and authors write about their own topics, so anything extracted as "style" tends to carry content with it. The larger goal is amortisation: instead of computing one intervention per user offline and keeping it, train a network that maps examples to the intervention in a single forward pass. They compare three proposals against three baselines under that constraint, and the analysis half of the paper is really about whether the intervention a predictor finds has to be the intervention a manual contrast finds.

## What they studied

Base model: Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507, frozen throughout (36 layers, hidden size 2,560). Data: an arXiv snapshot restricted to permissive-licence cs.AI and cs.LG papers, publication year capped at 2022 so the ground-truth abstracts are provably human, first author treated as the writer. After filtering, 1,232 papers from 203 authors (6.07 papers each on average, range 5–14); one paper per author held out for validation and one for test, leaving 826 training entries. A second unseen-author set of 1,312 papers from 328 authors, each with exactly four papers — three as prompt examples, one as the test item — keeps every author out of training for both the generators and the author classifier. Each training item pairs a paper outline (generated by a Qwen3-4B model from the LaTeX source, four headings) with the abstract, the introduction, and three abstracts sampled from the same author's other papers. Unit of analysis: layers, residual-stream directions, and LoRA weight matrices on the query and value projections.

## How they did it

Three methods, all conditioned on the same author representation: three abstracts embedded by a frozen STAR authorship encoder, projected to 128 dimensions, averaged. Manual contrastive steering builds a vector per layer as the mean author activation minus the mean activation of the model's own zero-shot abstract for *the same paper* — the foil is the model's default style on the same content, not other authors' texts — averaged over the three references and injected at layers 18–20 with intensity 0.875 selected on validation. A learned steering network concatenates that author representation with a learned layer embedding, runs a four-block MLP backbone, and a zero-initialised linear head emits one shift vector per layer, so no intensity knob exists and per-layer magnitudes are learned; it is trained end-to-end through the frozen model on the author's real abstract. A hypernetwork is the same backbone plus a module embedding, with four heads emitting rank-8 LoRA A and B matrices for q_proj and v_proj, initialised to be a no-op. In both learned methods the base model receives only the outline — the three examples go to the predictor, never into the context — so style is carried entirely by predicted parameters. Baselines: zero-shot, few-shot with the three examples in the prompt, and a single rank-8 LoRA fitted across all training authors (not per author) and conditioned on the examples at inference. Evaluation uses (a) a fine-tuned distilbert k-NN authorship classifier at k=3, cosine, on fine-tuned embeddings with the head discarded, reporting accuracy and MRR; (b) a binary topic-preservation judge (GPT-OSS-20B) comparing abstract against introduction; (c) a pairwise quality judge against the real abstract, with each pair judged in both orders to cancel position bias.

## What counts as an explanation here

Not an explanation of the model, but a compact predictive surrogate for an intervention: a trained map from a few examples to the perturbation to apply, defined per layer and, in the adapter case, per module. The manual contrast vector is the "description" form — one direction per layer with a stated foil — and the whole analysis section is about whether that description is the mechanism. The paper's answer is that it is one of at least two working descriptions, so the surrogate predicts behaviour without claiming to recover the privileged direction. It is partial by design: a constant offset for steering, a weight-conditioned update for the adapter, applied to a narrow band of layers.

## How they judged success

Two primary metrics read as a pair, never alone: author-classifier accuracy and MRR for style, GPT-judge preference rate for quality, with topic preservation as a precondition gate. The style metric is explicitly calibrated rather than absolute: content-only abstracts (style-neutral, same outlines) are attributed at 31.0% on the seen set and 22.9% on the unseen set, and held-out human abstracts at 53.2% and 41.5%, so every result is read between an empirical content floor and a classifier ceiling. The classifier itself is validated by a four-row manipulation table (ground truth 53.2, target author's LoRA-styled 49.8, style removed 31.0, foreign content at 0.0–0.3 against chance 0.5), and the topic judge by a confusion matrix on 2,464 pairs at 99.3% accuracy. Trade-offs are described by Pareto dominance on point estimates with paired bootstrap 95% intervals, and the authors say the Pareto claims are descriptive rather than significance claims. Ablations: layer scope (18–20, 20–22, all 36) for both learned methods and both contrast constructions, plus per-layer vector geometry (L2 norm, L1/L2 sparsity, cosine between manual and predicted).

## What they found

A consistent style–quality trade-off across all six systems, reproduced on unseen authors with the same ranking. On seen authors: LoRA reaches the highest style accuracy at 49.8 (about 84% of the range above the content floor) but its preference rate collapses from 97.9 (zero-shot) to 38.1; the hypernetwork reaches 36.5 at 91.3 preference; manual steering 34.5 at 94.3; learned steering 35.0 at 89.6; and few-shot scores *below* zero-shot on both style metrics (28.1 and 0.36 against 31.0 and 0.40). On unseen authors the ordering is identical: LoRA 37.5 at 39.0, hypernetwork 31.4 at 95.5, learned steering 28.4 at 87.5, manual 26.2 at 95.1, few-shot 18.3 against zero-shot 22.9. Manual and predicted steering vectors reach comparable style while being near-orthogonal — mean cosine between them −0.03, −0.05, −0.04 at layers 18, 19, 20 — and the two differ by a single abstract on the seen test set. Their magnitudes are similar (L2 norm 18–36% of the hidden activation norm, hidden norms 39.9–49.4 against vector norms 8.2–15.1) but their structure is not: predicted vectors spread mass like a dense Gaussian (L1/L2 39.5–40.1 against 40.4 expected) while manual vectors are sparse (17.3–19.4). Layer scope is a property of the intervention, not of the task: manual steering at all 36 layers collapses to 2.5% style accuracy (below the content floor) while the same construction at 18–20 gives 36.0 and beats the inventory-based contrast (34.0 versus 31.0 at 20–22); learned steering at all 36 layers still scores 35.0 at a lower preference rate, and the hypernetwork drops from 36.5/91.3 to 34.5/71.4 going from band to full depth.

## Limits and open problems they name

The style metric is entangled with content: style-neutral generations are attributed well above chance, so every style number is a relative ranking inside this setup, and the reverse probe (varying content at fixed style conditioning) lands at or below chance, which cannot be attributed to the classifier or to the generators. The paper explicitly lacks the control that would separate an author-specific intervention from a generic shift toward human-written abstracts, and does not measure within- versus between-author similarity of predicted vectors or of the effective adapter product. Only two layer scopes were tried, so "narrow band beats full depth" is established but 18–20 is not shown to be optimal. One base model, one domain, English only; the authors say it is open whether the trade-off structure is a property of style conditioning or of this configuration. Manual steering also needs one neutral generation per reference, so it consumes more than the three abstracts and is a reference point rather than a deployable method.

## What we take from it

A **measure and a warning**, both mechanical. Measure: the near-orthogonality result is the folder's second case (after the seed-controlled contrast) where two directions with near-zero cosine produce the same behavioural effect — so cosine between a predictor's output and a hand-computed direction is not a validation criterion, and the paper says so directly. Warning: the layer-scope table is a clean demonstration that a fixed global intensity applied at all layers compounds along the residual stream and destroys the effect (2.5%) while the same vector at three layers works, which is the same accumulation risk our model must control for when it reports a per-layer predicted intervention as a single object. Also useful: the content-only floor — running the *untreated* baseline method through the evaluation metric to find the level attributable to the input alone — is a cheap, reusable calibration our white-box model's read-outs should carry before any style or behaviour accuracy number is reported.
