## What they wanted, and why
Multi-agent language-model systems are routinely justified by an averaging argument: if the members' errors are unbiased and independent, the error of their aggregate should shrink with the number of agents. The authors observe that this argument is an identity, so its empirical content lies entirely in whether the premises hold, and they want a task on which both premises can be measured rather than assumed. Fermi estimation suits the test because answers are continuous, are naturally combined on a log scale, and errors can cancel rather than merely outvote each other. The larger goal is to replace a vague worry about "correlated errors across models" with a decomposition that says on which items the shared error lives, how much of it a team can remove, and which interventions could touch it.

## What they studied
Fifteen open-weight instruction-tuned models spanning 3B–72B across nine lineages, with a sixteenth excluded because it hits the token limit on most generations (two of the largest are 4-bit AWQ checkpoints, the rest bfloat16 through vLLM). Data: 529 realFP items with positive finite numeric answers, 500 Science Olympiad Fermi questions, and 15 guesstimation items about counting objects in containers. The unit of analysis is a *draw* — one model's answer to one item in one team slot — with roughly 350 draws per model per item and 2.51M round-1 answers in total, and the estimands are variance components over the items of a benchmark: the between-item variance of a model's per-item mean error, the average within-item variance of its draws, and the intraclass correlation between them.

## How they did it
Every answer is written to a fixed `FINAL:` line and scored as the signed error in orders of magnitude against the true value. That error is decomposed into a model-wide bias, an item-level component shared by all of the model's draws on that item, and a residual; the components are estimated by a one-way variance decomposition by item with errors winsorised at a fixed OOM ceiling and corrected for sampling noise in the item means, and cross-checked against a second estimate obtained by fitting the variance of real team means. Because a team rule that shifts with its inputs passes the item-level term through unchanged, the item component is a floor on any such rule, and aggregation can only act on the residual. The counterfactual references are simulated rather than derived from the asymptotic rate: one resamples each member independently from the same model's draw pool on the same item, which is what a team with no dependence given the item would look like, and one takes the same resampled draws with random signs, which is what unbiased independent draws would look like. Teams answer round 1 in isolation, then revise in rounds 2 and 3 after seeing truncated peer answers; the second corpus runs a self-revision arm in which the same revise prompt is assembled with the peer field left empty, which separates exposure to peers from the extra generation passes. Interventions are then applied at four levels — model composition (all mixtures of 2 to 5 models over five slots), prompt-level arms (per-agent decomposition scaffolds, per-agent strategy anchors, calculator offload, reference constants, unit bases), sampling temperature, and a residual-stream screen on one model at layer 14 of 28 (contrastive strategy steering with opposite signs on paired agents, Gaussian noise, random attention-head masking, layer skipping) — and scored as the paired change in team error with an item-clustered interval, with the change in the item-level component reported alongside.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not an account of mechanism inside any network, but a statistical decomposition of error with an explicit floor. The explanation of why teams fail is that most of a small model's error is an error that *every* draw of that model makes on the item, and the decomposition says exactly how much: it is a variance-component estimate with two independent confirmations, not a story about what the model is computing. Its predictive content is quantitative and conditional — given the intraclass correlation of a benchmark and a model, the paper's decomposition predicts the size of the gain a team can deliver and the gain it cannot, and the authors use it to explain in advance why their own Guesstimate and SciOly numbers look the way they do, and why earlier reports of large gains from aggregating one model's samples came from benchmarks with the opposite variance structure.

## How they judged success
The headline metric is mean absolute error in OOM winsorised at 5, because the squared-error form is dominated by answers off by tens of orders of magnitude. Teams aggregate by the geometric mean so that the signed error of the team is exactly the mean of the members' signed errors and the decomposition applies directly, and every team-level result is repeated under the median rule in an appendix to show which conclusions are rule-dependent. All intervals are 95% item-clustered bootstraps with the item as the unit of inference. Success for an intervention is a paired reduction in team error that also lowers the item-level component, and the authors state the reason for the second clause: a degraded agent inflates the within-item variance and therefore lowers the intraclass correlation without any gain in diversity, so the correlation alone would credit noise as diversity. One family of contrasts — the six prompt-level arms — is confirmatory with its endpoint fixed in advance in an internal analysis plan (not publicly registered) and Holm-adjusted p-values; everything else is exploratory and the counts of models with significant effects are reported unadjusted and labelled descriptive.

## What they found
The decomposition is lopsided everywhere. The item-level component accounts for a median 0.906 of the error variance on realFP, 0.887 on SciOly and 0.763 on Guesstimate, and the design-effect-equivalent number of independent draws for a team of five is a median 1.08 — a team of five is worth barely more than one agent. Teammates are nonetheless independent *given* the item: their residuals against the item-and-run mean correlate at essentially zero, and real teams match the resampled-given-the-item reference to within a median 0.0004 OOM, so the shortfall is not dependence between members but the item term they all carry. Teams of thirty reduce single-agent error by a median 1.7% (at most 5.4%), which is 2.2% of the 74–79% reduction that unbiased independent draws on the same items would deliver; under the harsher reference that also removes item difficulty, the available reduction is 51–63% and at most 10.7% of it is realised. The structure of the shared error is measurable. Every one of the 15 models compresses: small quantities are overestimated and large ones underestimated, with negative slopes for 15 of 15 models, a median crossing point near 10^6 on realFP, and the compression common to all lineages. All models carry biases of the same sign — every point estimate positive in the main sweep, so a consensus of models inherits the mean bias and does not shrink it (realised shrinkage 1.00 against 3.87 for independent biases), and mixture weights cannot undo it because no magnitude band has both signs except the one where models are already near-unbiased. Cross-model per-item errors correlate at 0.827 before and 0.689 after removing the linear magnitude trend, and this is not a label problem: an answer-key audit flags 5.5% of items as candidates, four of which have independently checkable values and in each the models are right, and removing all flagged items barely moves the pooled median. Debate produces convergence without accuracy: three rounds cut within-team dispersion from 0.211 to 0.014 OOM (93%) and leave the item-level component at 2.64 OOM before and after, with a total gain of 2.1% over one agent at fifteen times the generation cost. The self-revision arm gets at least the same gain, which locates the convergence in peer exposure and the gain in the revision passes. Interventions behave as the decomposition predicts. Every arm that increases disagreement by perturbing the agents fails to convert it into accuracy — temperature to 1.0 changes team error by at most about 1%, contrastive activation steering changes neither the item component nor team error despite visibly moving the answers, and Gaussian noise makes the team worse. Composition is the one reliable diversity win: over all 4,928 mixtures of 2 to 5 models the median improvement over the members' own homogeneous teams is 0.139 OOM (6.3%, positive for 99.8%), four times a homogeneous team's own gain over one agent, but no mixture improves on the homogeneous team of the most accurate model — the closest, gpt-oss-20b with qwen3.6-27b, is worse by 0.169 OOM. Calculator offload makes the team significantly worse (catastrophic answers rising from 51.5% to 66.9%), which the authors read as evidence that arithmetic is not the bottleneck when the choice of factors is. Recalibration removes the overall bias without touching the item component, as it must, since a map from estimate to truth cannot separate item-specific error from magnitude.

## Limits and open problems they name
The evidence is conditional on open-weight 3B–72B models with thinking modes disabled and a 1,024-token budget, so frontier, closed and reasoning models and extended test-time reasoning are untested; the four checkpoints above 15B support consistency rather than a claim about scale. The main sweep mixes two prompt pairs unevenly across models, so per-model accuracy and bias levels and their rank correlations are prompt-dependent, though the second corpus with a fixed pair reproduces every structural statistic. Rounds 2 and 3 were generated by a harness whose parser misread mantissa notation, so in 22.5% of homogeneous and 32.6% of heterogeneous teams an agent saw a peer value different from the one written; recorded answers are re-scored but what agents saw cannot be repaired, and the authors withdraw their conclusion about debate in heterogeneous teams on this basis. The temperature sweep covers four models, one run and one temperature; the activation screen covers one model, 150 items, the median rule, its model recorded only in the job configuration, and it stored per-item errors from the earlier parser, so its layer-skipping result is a candidate and not an established effect. SciOly's answer key offsets all signed errors by an unknown common amount and Guesstimate has 15 items spanning less than two orders of magnitude.

## What we take from it
Four things. **A variance decomposition by item belongs in our own evaluation vocabulary, because the folder has repeatedly reported pooled effects without asking how much of the effect is a constant per item.** Anything we measure across a set of prompts — a probe's accuracy, a steering direction's success, a dictionary feature's coverage — can be split into a component shared by all runs on that item and a component that varies between runs, and the ratio between them is what decides whether more sampling or more intervention is the right response. Here the same statistic explains teams, debate, temperature, steering and recalibration in one frame. **The independence check and the correlation check are different tests and this paper runs both**: residual correlation *given* the item answers "do teammates add new noise", while the intraclass correlation answers "is the noise they share large"; the folder has treated these as one question whenever it argued from correlated errors to correlated mechanisms, and the split shows a system can be perfectly independent given the item and still gain almost nothing. **The random-sign reference is the right counterfactual for any "diversity" claim**: rather than asking whether disagreement increased, it asks what the gain would have been if the members' per-item error signs had been free, and reporting the realised share of that reduction (2.2%) is far more informative than reporting whether an arm moved the correlation. **And the paper's own instrument discipline is worth copying: a confirmatory family declared in advance and Holm-corrected, with everything else labelled exploratory.** The two arms that improved a team and lowered the shared error are the two the paper refuses to endorse, one for failing multiplicity correction and one for resting on a single-model screen — the correct disposition for results that point the right way but were not powered to establish it.
