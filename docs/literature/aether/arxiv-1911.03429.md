## What they wanted, and why

The authors argue that the interpretable-NLP subfield was accumulating methods that could not be compared, because each one came with its own datasets, its own annotation conventions, and its own metric. Their proposed remedy is a shared test bed: a standardised collection of existing corpora, each already carrying human-marked supporting evidence ("rationales"), re-released in one format with agreed splits, plus a starter set of metrics and baseline implementations. The larger goal is less about any single explanation technique than about making claims of the form "this method gives better explanations" decidable and repeatable. A secondary goal, stated in the introduction, is to push the field past plausibility: they separate rationales that look right to a human from rationales the model actually relied on, and they call the second property faithfulness.

## What they studied

Seven English NLP datasets, each at span- or sentence-level evidence granularity: Evidence Inference (evidence spans in full-text RCT reports, three-way effect labels), BoolQ (yes/no questions over Wikipedia passages), a movie-review corpus carried over from the older annotator-rationale literature, FEVER (claim verification against Wikipedia, restricted to supported and refuted), MultiRC (multi-sentence reading comprehension, converted to per-answer True/False instances), CoS-E (multiple-choice commonsense QA with extracted evidence snippets; the free-text explanations in the source corpus are deliberately not used), and e-SNLI (entailment with word-level evidence in premise and/or hypothesis). Table 1 gives sizes and mean document length, which range from tens of tokens (CoS-E, e-SNLI) to thousands (Evidence Inference, BoolQ). The unit of analysis is the token or the snippet inside one instance; the models studied are BERT-base (plus a bi-LSTM), GloVe-embedding LSTMs, GRU sentence encoders, and SciBERT for the clinical dataset.

## How they did it

Datasets were converted to a single format of input, label and human-marked evidence, with train/dev/test splits made disjoint at the level of source documents to prevent leakage (FEVER needed a strongly-connected-component pass over the document graph for this; BoolQ needed source articles recovered from a Wikipedia snapshot and matched by fuzzy string distance, dropping instances below a similarity cut). Because pre-existing annotations were usually sufficient but not complete, the authors hired annotators — two Upwork workers for the review corpus and for a subset of the question set, four medical professionals for the clinical corpus, Amazon Mechanical Turk workers with high approval counts for BoolQ — to produce *comprehensive* evidence annotations on parts of the test sets. Human agreement was then measured against majority vote with Cohen's κ and token-level F1/precision/recall, which establishes an upper bound on what any automatic rationale can score. For evaluation they then run two families of baseline: models that select snippets discretely (a mask-based rationaliser trained with policy-gradient estimation, with and without evidence supervision, and two-stage pipeline models where one network picks evidence and a second, independently parameterised network reads only that evidence), and models that score tokens continuously (attention over a bi-LSTM, input gradients, and LIME) where a single model must both predict and produce scores.

## What counts as an explanation here

A rationale: a discrete set of input tokens the model names as its support. For the discrete-selection family the rationale is faithful by construction, because a separate predictor consumes only the selected text — the explanation *is* the mechanism, not a read-out of it. For the soft-scoring family the explanation is an ordered list of tokens converted to a set by taking the top-k, and fidelity is not assumed but measured. The account is deliberately partial in the sense that it explains a single prediction by pointing at input text; nothing in the benchmark asks a model to represent internal computation.

## How they judged success

Four instruments. (1) *Agreement*: intersection-over-union against gold evidence with a 0.5 match threshold, token-level precision/recall/F1, and for soft scores the area under the precision-recall curve over token rankings; recall is treated as a direct measure of comprehensiveness only on the datasets that actually have comprehensive annotations, and is called noisy wherever they are missing — a distinction the paper makes explicitly. (2) *Comprehensiveness*: the drop in the probability of the originally predicted class when the named rationale is removed from the input. (3) *Sufficiency*: the drop when the model sees *only* the rationale. (4) *AOPC*: both faithfulness numbers recomputed over bins of the top 1 %, 5 %, 10 %, 20 % and 50 % of tokens by importance score, so that the curve rather than one operating point is reported — and, crucially, a **random-score arm averaged over ten runs** sits beside every soft-scoring number as the floor. Predictiveness on the task itself is reported separately so that an explanation score is never mistaken for a task score.

## What they found

Human agreement is substantial across the board: κ between roughly 0.62 and 0.85 and token-level F1 between about 0.62 and 0.87 depending on dataset, with the review corpus and the clinical corpus near the top on F1 and FEVER highest on κ; the clinical corpus has no κ because redundant annotation was not collected, which the authors explain by the annotators' expertise. Among discrete selectors, evidence supervision usually helps agreement (not always), and the effect does not track predictive accuracy; a BERT-to-BERT pipeline reaches the best agreement on FEVER (token F1 0.812) and the joint mask model collapses to exactly zero on several datasets when trained with policy gradient, which the paper attributes to a degenerate training outcome rather than a modelling result. Among soft scorers the divergence is the headline: attention has the highest AUPRC against human evidence in nearly every dataset slot (e.g. 0.606 versus 0.585 gradient and 0.544 LIME on the commonsense set, 0.395/0.416/0.513 on the entailment set) while gradient and LIME give more comprehensive and more sufficient rationales, and LIME is singled out as doing particularly well on faithfulness. The random floor is what makes those comparisons interpretable: on the long-document datasets the random arm's comprehensiveness is near zero (−0.001, 0.000), i.e. an explanation scores essentially nothing by chance there, while on the short-input datasets random removal is itself damaging, so the gap between a method and the floor narrows sharply and one attention-vs-random comparison inverts (sufficiency 0.097 for attention against 0.330 for random on the review corpus).

## Limits and open problems they name

They call the metric set a starting point and say plainly that measuring faithfulness is unsettled, hoping the release will generate better metrics rather than ratify these; the leaderboard is labelled a results board precisely to avoid privileging one criterion. The AOPC scores are not comparable across datasets, only across models on the same dataset and by construction the same model. Discretising a soft score requires choosing k, set here to the dataset's mean human evidence length — a choice that is itself an assumption about how long an explanation should be. The four baselines are described as not comprehensive, only one hard-selection model could be run at scale on the long corpora, and the pipeline family presumes sentence-level evidence, so no single architecture covers all seven datasets: they name "models that can rationalise at varying granularity" as the gap the benchmark exposes. Annotation cost and its ethics are noted too, including an explicit acknowledgement in the appendix that the crowd-work pay rate on one dataset came out low.

## What we take from it

Three things, in order of value. (1) **The paired deletion/sufficiency pair as the default faithfulness table**, computed with a random-score floor and reported as a curve over the fraction of the input removed rather than at a single k. This is the cheapest way to answer the question our own write-ups keep dodging — *does the named part carry the behaviour* — and it is the ancestor of the circuit complement tests and the unexplained-effect shares the folder has been collecting: comprehensiveness is what breaking the named part costs, sufficiency is what keeping only the named part preserves. (2) **Human rationales as an upper-bound ruler, with the rater's own agreement printed first.** Reporting inter-annotator κ on the same instrument before comparing any model to the gold evidence is the discipline that stops a 0.35 F1 from reading as failure or a 0.87 from reading as success; our accounts should publish reader agreement on the annotation task before any account-vs-account comparison. (3) **The "faithful by construction" framing, and its exact condition.** A two-module extract-plus-predict arrangement makes the explanation faithful because the predictor is barred from seeing anything else — but it is faithful only for an independently parameterised reader, and the paper flags that assumption in a footnote. That is the same claim shape as our own "matched no-account arm with a stated reader" requirement, and it is a warning: the guarantee comes from the architecture of the read-out, not from the quality of the extraction. The separations the paper finds also serve as a control set for us — attention wins agreement while losing faithfulness, and both facts are of the same size — so a method that reports only one column is uninformative regardless of which column it chose.
