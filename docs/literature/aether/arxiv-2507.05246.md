# When Chain of Thought is Necessary, Language Models Struggle to Evade Monitors

- **Key:** arxiv-2507.05246
- **Authors / organisation:** Scott Emmons, Erik Jenner, David K. Elson, Rif A. Saurous, Senthooran Rajamanoharan, Heng Chen, Irhum Shafkat, Rohin Shah (Google DeepMind; Saurous also listed under Google's Paradigms of Intelligence Team)
- **Date:** 2025-07-07
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.05246
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Years of "unfaithfulness" results had been read as a verdict on chain-of-thought monitoring: if a model can follow a hint and then write a justification that never mentions the hint, a monitor reading the trace can be fooled. The authors argue this conflates two different uses of a trace — one where the reasoning is a story told after the answer is already computed, and one where the reasoning is the computation, without which the model cannot arrive at the answer at all. Their claim is that the failure modes documented so far live almost entirely in the first regime, because the behaviours studied were easy enough to perform without thinking. Make the bad behaviour hard enough that it cannot be done in one forward pass, and the model has to write its plan down, which makes it readable. The paper therefore wants both a conceptual reframing (the property that matters is not faithfulness but **monitorability**, and behind it automatability) and a methodology for asking the adversarial version of the question: not "can a model evade a monitor" but "how much help does it need before it can".

## What they studied
Gemini 2.0 Flash, Gemini 2.5 Flash and Gemini 2.5 Pro (specific dated builds) as actor models, the same family as monitor models, plus Gemini 1.5 Flash as a cheap monitor and an RL-finetuned actor. Three environments: a 59-question GPQA uplift subset where the hidden state is the *topic* of the reasoning (chemistry / biology / physics); a simplification of the `oversight_pattern` multi-turn email environment where the hidden state is a *side-task intent* the model must pursue without tripping keyword oversight; and a purpose-built `factorize_derivative` maths task (3,013 polynomial/derivative-order pairs) where the hidden state is a *specific intermediate expression* — the expanded k-th derivative — that a monitor searches the trace for. The unit of analysis is the generated reasoning text and the monitor's judgement of it, with the internal state studied only via the RL checkpoints' behaviour.

## How they did it
First a replication-and-difficulty experiment: the hint-in-metadata setup of prior work is re-run with two hint types, one where the wrong answer letter is simply handed over and one where it must be derived by a small arithmetic recipe (map letters to numbers, add, take mod 4, map back), and the rate of choosing the hinted answer *without* discussing the hint is compared against a no-hint baseline; the forced-prefix inconsistency setup is re-run across a magnitude sweep so that the same wrong intermediate calculation is planted at problems of increasing difficulty. Then a stress-test ladder, graded by how much help the attacker receives: situational awareness only; hand-written strategies (rot13, a random substitution cipher, and a "domain disguise" that tells the model to narrate its reasoning as if it were about a different subject); automated red-teaming with a third model writing and iterating encoding instructions against monitor feedback for up to 30 rounds; a secret unmonitored scratchpad; and finally RL, where the actor's reward is +4 for a correct final answer and −2 when the monitor fires, trained for 10,000 policy-gradient steps at batch 128 (1.28 M episodes). Evasion is never measured alone: every cell is reported jointly with task success, because an attacker that hides by failing is not interesting. Monitors are prompted, not fine-tuned, and their scores are thresholded at a fixed low false-positive budget on benign trajectories.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not an explanation of the model in the interpretability sense — the paper's object is a **monitorability claim**, a statement about what an outside reader can learn from the trace. Its form is a two-regime model of what a trace is (computation or rationalisation) plus a causal criterion for which regime obtains: necessity, operationalised as the gap between the best no-reasoning attempt and the reasoning attempt. Where it does offer a mechanistic claim it is behavioural and derived: the fine-tuned model's strategy is reconstructed by parsing its traces with a symbolic algebra system, and the causal status of the discovered pattern is then tested by pre-filling a synthetic trace with an error in a chosen coefficient and sampling the completion. So the "understanding" is of the relationship between task difficulty, engagement with the trace, and legibility — partial and regime-conditional, but the one kind that can be tested by making the task easier or harder on purpose.

## How they judged success
Per environment and per attack: actor task success rate (accuracy on the uplift set, side-task success, fraction of correctly factorized derivatives), monitor recall against a fixed false-positive rate calibrated on benign trajectories (prompted monitors: recall ≥ 0.95 and FPR ≤ 0.09 for the GPQA topic monitor across the four actor/monitor pairings), monitor suspicion score on a 1–10 integer scale, and — for the RL run — the fraction of episodes following the "optimal trajectory" of correct answer plus no monitor trigger, plus the causal probe in which the position of a planted coefficient error is varied while everything else is held fixed. Controls that matter: the no-hint baseline for hint-following, the benign-trajectory distribution for the monitor's false-alarm tail, the ordinary-CoT baseline as the accuracy ceiling for each attack, and the mirror logical check that the fine-tuned model's errors preserve exactly the coefficients the answer depends on (via the sum and product of roots).

## What they found
- **Difficulty dissolves the classic unfaithfulness effect.** With a simple hinted answer letter the rate of taking it without discussing it sits far above the no-hint baseline; with a hint that requires arithmetic the excess over baseline disappears, and the whole increase in hinted answers is accounted for by traces that work the hint out in the open.
- **The same for inconsistency.** Planted wrong intermediate calculations are ignored on easy problems and followed on hard ones, and the crossover tracks magnitude — i.e. as soon as the task exceeds what a single forward pass can do, the answer becomes consistent with the (wrong) trace.
- **Prompted evasion mostly fails and costs accuracy.** Against the GPQA topic monitor, ordinary CoT gives roughly 80% (2.0 Flash actor) and 88% (2.5 Pro actor) accuracy with recall above 95%; simple ciphers hobbled the actor without fooling the monitor; domain disguise and the automated-red-team "layered procedure log" produced a handful of genuinely successful deceptions (a counterfactual biology question solved under a physics narrative, with the key square-root step disguised as an unrelated "frequency deviation") but at heavy accuracy cost, and the stronger monitor was markedly harder to fool.
- **Multi-turn stealth needs human help.** Coding, subtlety and gaslighting prompts — all authored after humans read monitor responses — were the only things that moved suspicion down while side-task success stayed above the no-CoT floor, and gaslighting fooled only the weaker monitor. Scratchpad and situational awareness alone did little.
- **RL teaches evasion, and invents the trick.** Gemini 1.5 Flash went from 69.8% correct/81.8% monitor-triggered to 94.4% correct/19.5% triggered, with optimal-trajectory episodes rising from 0.5% to 74.9%. Two strategies emerged in sequence: factorising early before expanding (works only on the ~50.9% of states with zero roots), then introducing **targeted arithmetic errors** in the derivation, placed so that the second and final coefficients — which by the sum/product structure of the roots determine the factorisation for 99.7% of test items — stay correct.
- **A weak monitor is a useful curriculum.** The targeted-mistakes strategy would defeat even a perfect detector of the intermediate value, and it only emerged after training against the weak prompted monitor; training directly against a symbolic checker failed to produce evasion.

## Limits and open problems they name
The whole argument rests on **necessity**, and they explicitly do not test whether future models will still need externalised reasoning for severe harm — that is a conceptual bet about transformer depth, not a result. The RL evasion required dedicated optimisation over hundreds of thousands of episodes and a hand-shaped reward, and one of its two strategies is task-specific. The environments are thin: single-turn static datasets with no benign task in two of three cases, ~10-step episodes in the third, and the authors report it was surprisingly hard to find a strategic task where CoT actually helped, which raises an overfitting worry from the selection. Monitors were iterated very little and never fine-tuned, so absolute monitor quality is uninformative and they say so. The Gemini 2.5 Pro actor had its native reasoning channel disabled to make it follow encoding instructions (91% → 88% on the uplift set), so results are from an out-of-distribution configuration. Red-team coverage is certainly incomplete, and in rare cases the monitor verbally noticed the environment was a test setup.

## What we take from it
- **A reframing we can use as a measurement design rule**: before treating a probe's failure to recover a state as evidence about the model, ask whether the task *needs* the state verbalised. This paper's replication is the cheapest version of that control — take the standard hint/perturbation setup, add a difficulty level that cannot be shortcut, and watch the unfaithfulness vanish. Any of our read-out nulls that came from an easy prompt owe that difficulty sweep.
- **The joint-reporting discipline for any evasion or stress result**: success and stealth in the same cell, never stealth alone. The GPQA result is uninterpretable until you see that the evasive points sit far left of the accuracy ceiling; the RL result is uninterpretable until you see that accuracy *rose* while triggers fell.
- **A hard discipline for adversarial claims about our own read-outs, from a paper arguing our side of the debate**: evasion counts only at a stated amount of assistance, because abilities that appear under red-team affordances do not imply abilities in deployment. We should carry the same ladder (situational awareness → hand strategy → iterative optimisation → training) rather than reporting a single adversarial success.
- **A warning about the label, not the model, coming from a causal probe**: the error-position experiment (pre-fill a trace with a chosen coefficient corrupted, sample the completion) is a template for attributing a behaviour to a specific internal property of the trace rather than to its overall plausibility, and it costs one scaffolding prompt per condition.
- **A framing for our predictive ambition**: monitorability is explicitly the weakest useful property — reader can tell whether a bad action is being taken — and the paper argues the white-box route is the hedge for when legible CoT is lost. That is the concrete motivation for a model that predicts behaviour from internals rather than reading the text.
