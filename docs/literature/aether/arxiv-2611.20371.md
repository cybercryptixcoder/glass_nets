## What they wanted, and why
Linear probes are the field's default instrument, and the field already knows that decoding accuracy is not proof of use — a feature can be readable without the network relying on it, which is why causal interventions are treated as the real test. What nobody had asked is whether the probe's own fitting procedure, and regularization in particular, changes the answer that test gives. Probing practice is split: some studies fit ridge probes with the penalty tuned on held-out accuracy, others use unregularized or component-selected fits, and neither camp explains what actually breaks without regularization beyond vague worries about stability and generalization. The paper's target is therefore narrower than "probes are unreliable" and stronger: it wants to show that the standard unregularized fit is *systematically* aimed at directions the network does not use, and to give the mechanism. The larger goal is a probe whose direction can be handed to a steering experiment and survive it, since that is the only sense in which a readout supports a claim about what the model computes.

## What they studied
Two testbeds chosen so that the ground truth after an intervention is exactly known. First, the **Mess3 hidden Markov model**: a 3-state HMM (mixing parameter 0.05, emission concentration 0.85) whose Bayes-optimal predictor must carry a 3-dimensional belief state living on a 2-simplex, run through a previously trained 4-layer width-64 transformer; the data is *every* sequence of length 1 to 8, each weighted by its probability, so there is no train/test split and no sampling to speak of. Second, **Llama-2-7B** probed for the **birth year** of named people from Wikidata (8,000 prompts, restricted to 1900–1999 so the readout is a single decade digit), reading the 4,096-dim residual stream at the last token of the name at layer 8, with the output summarised as an expected decade over the ten digit tokens. The unit of analysis is the **direction** (a single unit vector for scalar targets; the plane spanned by three weight vectors for the belief target) and, in the theory, the **principal components** of the centred activation matrix, each carrying a share of the probe's squared weight ("probe mass"). A third direction family, **Distributed Alignment Search (DAS)**, is fitted by gradient descent directly on intervention outcomes, with no decodability objective at all, and serves as the causal reference point.

## How they did it
Three ways of getting a direction for the same target on the same activations, differing only in the fitting procedure: ordinary least squares, ridge regression swept over a logarithmic grid of penalties, and DAS. Because the target is continuous and the probe is a linear map, the entire OLS-versus-ridge comparison reduces in the principal-component basis to one scalar per direction, weight = covariance with the target divided by variance plus the penalty over the sample size, so the sweep is a closed-form family rather than a repeated fit. Interventions follow two protocols, both restricted to values the network can actually exhibit: on the HMM, *probe-mediated steering* pushes the activation by the pseudoinverse of the probe's weights times a requested belief shift, walking 20 smooth steps toward each simplex corner from three start states, scored against the belief-implied next-token distribution over a uniform 496-point grid of target beliefs; on Llama-2-7B, *natural-magnitude interchange*, which takes a base prompt (say a 1920s birth) and a source prompt (1980s) and swaps the *component along the candidate direction* between them, so the source's true decade supplies the ground-truth target and the edit is a value the model genuinely produces elsewhere. Intervention loss is the MSE between the post-intervention output and what the ground truth predicts — for DAS, the objective it was trained on; for the probes, a held-out criterion used only to choose the penalty. Causal operativity of a raw principal component (which has no readout, so cannot be steered toward a target) is measured as the mean absolute output change under that interchange. Two closing tests guard against an artifact of testing components one at a time: DAS is confined to the span of the 100 heaviest-weight OLS components on Llama-2-7B (34.8% of OLS mass) and the 7 heaviest on the HMM (over 99.9% of mass) and asked to find the most causally effective direction it can; and covariance is re-estimated split-half, with one half of the prompts fixing the principal directions and the other supplying variance and covariance, to check it sits at the sampling-noise scale.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **failure mechanism plus a selection rule**, not a description of what any feature means. The explanation is an identity-level account of where a probe's weight comes from — a direction's weight is its covariance with the target over its own variance — from which it follows that unregularized fits must hand large weights to quiet directions that carry only noise-scale covariance, and that a penalty large enough bounds this amplification from above. Everything the paper asserts about faithfulness is deliberately on the negative side: the theory proves that OLS is prone to spurious low-variance directions, and explicitly does not prove that ridge finds the ones the network uses. The positive claim is empirical on two testbeds, and the recommendation that follows is procedural: choose the penalty by the intervention test rather than by held-out decoding. The predicted object is a *direction*, and the prediction is about how the model's output will move when that direction is edited — a prediction under intervention, which is exactly the form of explanation our own survey is aimed at.

## How they judged success
Probe loss (MSE on the own-target fit, in-sample and on an 80/20 split), **intervention loss** (MSE against the ground-truth post-intervention output — the primary criterion), variance along the fitted direction, and the **angle to the DAS direction** (or the larger principal angle between subspaces, per Björck–Golub, for the multi-dimensional belief target). Mass-by-operativity is read off the principal-component decomposition, with bubble area proportional to probe mass. Controls: the restricted-DAS search inside the OLS span, the split-half covariance estimate against a three-standard-error band, the exhaustive HMM data with nothing to overfit, a held-out HMM split to separate generalization from causality, and the fact that DAS is trained on the evaluation objective so that its faithfulness is a sanity check and the informative comparison is ridge against DAS. No confidence intervals are reported on the headline numbers; the split-half figure is the only sampling-error accounting.

## What they found
On the HMM the OLS probe decodes almost perfectly — probe loss 9.46×10⁻⁵ against 3.99×10⁻³ for the intervention-optimal ridge probe and 3.21×10⁻³ for DAS — while its intervention loss is 2.14×10⁻¹ against 1.95×10⁻³ and 1.88×10⁻³, roughly a hundredfold worse than the two faithful methods and, on the HMM, a probe that reproduces the belief simplex near-perfectly is causally inert. Its direction sits at 89.8° from DAS; the ridge direction at 28.9°. On Llama-2-7B the pattern holds in the numbers that matter: OLS probe loss 6.71×10⁻² (lowest in the table) with intervention loss 1.37×10¹ — the same as not intervening at all — against 7.07×10⁰ for the intervention-optimal ridge probe (λ = 10⁴) and 6.42×10⁰ for DAS, at 84.7° and 26.3° from DAS respectively. Variance along the fitted directions separates the same way, from 1.16×10⁻⁸ (HMM) and 2.10×10⁻² (Llama) for OLS to single digits for both faithful methods. Probe loss rises monotonically with the penalty while intervention loss has an interior minimum, so the two selection criteria disagree and the intervention-optimal penalty is not the most regularized one. Mass concentrates on dead weight: on the HMM a single direction with variance 10⁻¹⁸ holds 0.915 of the OLS probe's mass, and on Llama the heaviest components covering 95% of the ridge probe's mass carry only about 31% of the OLS probe's, the remainder being a long tail of small, low-operativity components. Restricted DAS finds no causally operative direction anywhere in the OLS span on either testbed, which closes the "many inert directions summing to an operative one" loophole. Held-out decoding does not reliably catch the failure: on Llama the OLS probe's loss jumps 0.05 → 0.38 out of sample while the ridge probe holds 0.27 → 0.28, but on a held-out HMM split the OLS probe still decodes an order of magnitude better than the ridge probe (4.3×10⁻⁴ against 4.9×10⁻³) and remains inert. Of 4,096 principal components at Llama layer 8, 3,976 lie within three standard errors of zero covariance with birth year.

## Limits and open problems they name
Causal control is partial even for the winner: the most faithful ridge directions still leave intervention loss well above zero, well below OLS but not zero. The LLM side is one model at one layer (chosen by a layer sweep shown in an appendix), on one numeric attribute, and the theory covers *regression* probes on continuous targets while most probing practice is logistic — where the paper notes RAPTOR already sweeps an L2 penalty but selects by validation accuracy rather than intervention effect, and names the logistic counterpart of the theory as a natural next step. There is no guarantee in the other direction: nothing proved says ridge regression finds the network's directions or that faithful directions are necessarily high-variance, and the paper says so twice. Variance cannot substitute for the causal test, since variance is monotone in the penalty and maximizing it over-regularizes. On the exhaustive HMM data the OLS probe's advantage in decodability is trivial by construction, which is why the held-out HMM split is reported; on that split the two criteria still disagree, but the authors' own theory is agnostic about the origin of the small covariance on exhaustive data and they measure it rather than derive it. The intervention protocols are conservative by design (belief shifts inside the simplex, interchanges that copy values the source actually exhibits) to head off the known objection that out-of-distribution patching activates pathways the model never uses, so the published numbers are a lower bound on what a less careful intervention would show.

## What we take from it
Three things, one of them a method, one a measure, one a warning. **A method:** the penalty for any ridge probe we fit should be selected by an interventional criterion — the fit is closed-form and the sweep is one line of linear algebra per penalty, so the cost is entirely in the intervention arm, which we already run. **A measure:** report, beside every probe's accuracy, the **variance along the fitted direction** and the **share of the probe's mass falling on components whose interventional operativity is near zero**; both are computable from stored activations without new labels, and here they separate a near-perfect decoder from a faithful one by orders of magnitude. **A warning that lands directly on our own battery:** a held-out accuracy number did not catch the failure on the HMM at all, and on Llama it caught it only because the target was sampled rather than enumerated, so held-out decoding is not a substitute for the causal test even when it looks like it is doing the job. Two design notes to import wholesale: the **span control** (confining an intervention-trained method to the subspace a decodability-trained method selects, and asking whether anything operative lives there) is cheap and closes the "the effect is spread across inert directions" objection that plagues every per-direction causal test; and the **split-half covariance estimate against its sampling-error band** is the right way to say "this direction carries noise rather than signal" without re-running the training. Finally, this is the folder's first entry whose recommendation travels in the opposite direction from the usual "regularize for generalization" story: same instrument, same data, same target, and the only change is the penalty — which moves the recovered direction by 55–60° and turns a dead readout into a live one.
