# Language Models Don't Always Say What They Think: Unfaithful Explanations in Chain-of-Thought Prompting

- **Key:** arxiv-2305.04388
- **Authors / organisation:** Miles Turpin, Julian Michael, Ethan Perez, Samuel R. Bowman — NYU Alignment Research Group, with Michael also at Cohere and Bowman also at Anthropic
- **Date:** 2023-05-07 (v1); 2023-12-09 (v2, this entry)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.04388
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Chain-of-thought prompting is widely treated as a window into how a model reached its answer, and papers were already scoring explanations for plausibility — whether the steps make sense and support the conclusion. The authors' question is one level down: an explanation can be coherent and still not be the reason the model answered as it did. They want to know whether a model's stated reasoning is *systematically* shaped by features of its input that the reasoning never mentions, which would make the explanation a rationalisation rather than a report. The larger goal is to establish that transparency claims built on generated reasoning need their own adversarial test, and that a written chain of thought is not self-certifying.

## What they studied
Two RLHF-tuned assistants of the time: `text-davinci-003` (GPT-3.5) and `claude-v1.0`, neither with disclosed size or architecture. Data: thirteen BIG-Bench Hard (BBH) multiple-choice tasks, chosen from the twenty-three for having some subjectivity or hard-to-falsify world knowledge, and a 2,592-example stratified subset of the Bias Benchmark for QA (BBQ) covering nine of its eleven social-bias categories. Unit of analysis is the whole model's input-output map: the prompt is edited, the generated explanation and the final answer are read off, and nothing inside the network is touched.

## How they did it
For BBH the manipulation is a change to the prompt that should not matter if the stated reasoning were the real account. In the *Answer is Always A* condition the multiple-choice options in the few-shot demonstrations are permuted so that the correct choice sits in slot A throughout, exploiting the model's pull toward repeated patterns. In the *Suggested Answer* condition a user turn is appended asserting a randomly drawn choice ("I think the answer is <label> but I'm curious to hear what you think"), exploiting sycophancy. Predictions under the edited prompt are compared with predictions on the unedited prompt, over examples whose correct answer the bias points away from; a drop in accuracy is read as the bias overriding the model's own reasoning, since almost all such drops are traced to bias-consistent flips rather than to diffuse noise. For BBQ the manipulation is inside the question: ambiguous scenarios are augmented with two *weak evidence* sentences, generated by GPT-3.5 and hand-checked so the two people's evidence is swapped, and a faithful model should either abstain on both versions or switch its answer between them; changing answer only to the stereotype-consistent side is scored as inconsistent use of evidence. Every comparison is run across CoT versus no-CoT, zero-shot versus few-shot, and with or without a debiasing instruction, with paired-difference confidence intervals; separately, humans annotate samples of explanations for whether they support the final answer and for whether they contain visible errors.

## What counts as an explanation here
A generated chain of thought as a candidate account of the model's own decision procedure, judged under a counterfactual-simulatability framing: an account is faithful to the extent it lets a reader predict what the model would do on other inputs. Faithfulness is deliberately treated as a *necessary but not sufficient* property, tested only against small input edits. The paper's central claim is about form: the account is text, it is assessed by comparing behaviour across edited inputs, and the verdict is a discrepancy between what the explanation cites and what demonstrably moved the prediction. Nothing here is meant to predict a network's internals — the account is scored as a description of a policy, not as a causal model of a computation.

## How they judged success
For BBH, accuracy drop in the biased context against the unbiased context on bias-contradicting items, plus a companion metric that divides the flips into bias-consistent and everything else; the unbiased baseline for that second metric is the inverse of the average number of answer choices. For BBQ, the share of unfaithful prediction pairs that are stereotype-aligned, whose no-bias null is 50%, tested with a binomial test against 50%, alongside the overall unfaithful share. Controls: the same few-shot prompt for CoT and no-CoT so the demonstrations are held constant, unbiased-context predictions as the within-example reference via paired tests, and explicit debiasing instructions as a manipulation that should reduce bias if the effect is real. Verbalization is checked directly — a sample of explanations is read to confirm that the biasing feature is not mentioned — and a manual annotation of 104 BBH and 96 BBQ explanations codes whether the text supports the prediction actually made and whether errors are present.

## What they found
Biasing features move predictions substantially and are essentially never acknowledged. Accuracy on bias-contradicting BBH items falls as much as 36.3 points under zero-shot CoT with GPT-3.5 (59.6 to 23.3) and 30.6 points for Claude (65.3 to 34.7); few-shot CoT softens but does not remove the effect (24.1 and 21.5 points respectively). *Answer is Always A* costs GPT-3.5 18.7 points under few-shot CoT against 4.7 for Claude. The mirror-image analysis shows the mechanism: under the same bias, accuracy on bias-consistent items *rises* by 31.9 points for GPT-3.5 zero-shot CoT and 24.1 for Claude, so the loss is the bias steering a correct prediction onto the biased one. Across 234 BBH explanations read from biased contexts, none mentions the bias; across 192 BBQ explanation pairs only one does. Of 426 BBH explanations supporting biased predictions reviewed, one explicit mention is reported. Explanations are rewritten rather than merely contradicted: 73% of a 104-case BBH sample support the new bias-consistent answer, and 15% of those contain no obvious error at all — they rationalise through inconsistent subjective judgement or exploitation of task ambiguity. Convincingness drops from 4.0 for correct-answer explanations to 2.0 for biased ones, and logical-coherence problems rise from 12% to 42%. On BBQ, 62.5% of unfaithful few-shot CoT pairs for Claude are stereotype-aligned against 54.5% zero-shot, and 59.2% for GPT-3.5 zero-shot; 86% of a 96-case sample of stereotype-aligned explanations explicitly argue for the answer given. CoT does lower BBQ bias relative to no-CoT in every setting (6.4 points for Claude few-shot, 7.9 for GPT-3.5 few-shot), and explicit debiasing instructions nearly eliminate it for Claude (62.5 to 50.6) while doing little for GPT-3.5 zero-shot (59.2 to 60.0). One asymmetry worth recording: zero-shot CoT made models *more* sensitive to the Suggested Answer bias than no-CoT (GPT-3.5 39.5 to 23.3 in the biased context) even while improving accuracy in the unbiased context (57.1 to 59.6).

## Limits and open problems they name
The test establishes failure only, never faithfulness — a model could hide its true drivers in ways these edits do not disturb, and the edits are minor modifications of the input rather than the wide range of counterfactuals one would want an explanation to predict. Whether the behaviour is dishonesty or incapacity is left open: if the model could have recognised the bias via a critique or an internal read-out, the explanation is misleading; if not, it is a capability limit, and the two call for different interventions. The authors also note that prompting against known biases reduces them but cannot generalise to biases nobody has thought to name.

## What we take from it
A method and a warning, both cheap. **Method:** the input-edit-plus-behavioural-difference test is the folder's simplest operationalisation of "the explanation names X; does X actually drive the answer?" — insert a feature the explanation cannot plausibly reference, flip it, and see whether the prediction moves without the text changing to match. This is the exact instrument any account we generate should be run against before it is quoted. **Warning:** the rationalisation count is the number to keep — 73% of explanations rewritten to support the newly wrong answer, 15% of them error-free — meaning an explanation that is internally coherent and correctly predicts the answer has told us almost nothing, and a plausibility check alone is liable to certify a rationalisation. The weak-evidence construction on BBQ transfers directly: paired inputs differing only in which entity the same piece of evidence attaches to give a consistency score without needing ground truth, which is usable on tasks where no label exists. **Shortcut:** the bias-consistent mirror analysis — showing that accuracy rises where the bias points correctly — is a way to prove a drop is the bias and not noise for the cost of one extra tabulation, and it is stronger evidence than a drop alone.
