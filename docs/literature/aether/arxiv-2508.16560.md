# Sparse but Wrong: Incorrect L0 Leads to Incorrect Features in Sparse Autoencoders

## What they wanted, and why
Almost every SAE paper plots reconstruction against sparsity and treats the sparsity level — L0, the average number of latents firing per token — as a free dial: pick a point on the curve, and any point with low enough sparsity is as legitimate as any other. Two recent findings unsettled that picture without explaining it — low-L0 SAEs do worse on downstream probing and on sparse-probing benchmarks — and the authors wanted the mechanism rather than the correlation. The larger goal is to establish when a dictionary is actually disentangling the model's features and when it is merely fitting activations, and to leave practitioners a cheap diagnostic they can run without labelled probing benchmarks (their motivating case is SAEs trained on biology or chemistry foundation models, where no such benchmark exists). The paper is also an argument about evaluation method: its second target is the sparsity–reconstruction plot itself, which it tries to convict of rejecting the correct dictionary.

## What they studied
Two things. First, synthetic toy models: orthonormal ground-truth feature sets whose firing is drawn from a correlated Bernoulli process with per-feature probabilities and magnitudes, the smallest being 5 features in 20 dimensions with a true L0 of 2, the main one 50 features in 100 dimensions with firing probabilities falling linearly and a true L0 of 11, plus variants with superposition noise (40 dimensions for 50 features) and with an SAE narrower than the feature set (25 latents). Second, pretrained LLMs: Gemma-2-2b at layers 5, 12 and 20 and Llama-3.2-1b at layer 7, each with 32,768-latent BatchTopK SAEs trained across an L0 sweep, and a Gemma-2-2b layer-12 suite comparing BatchTopK against JumpReLU. The unit of analysis is the dictionary latent, judged by its cosine similarity to a ground-truth feature in the toy model and by decoder geometry in the LLM. They are careful to reserve the word *feature* for the concept and *latent* for the SAE unit, which the entry follows.

## How they did it
Toy models are generated by summing ground-truth directions weighted by correlated magnitudes, so the true answer is known and a "ground-truth SAE" can be constructed by setting encoder and decoder to the true basis. SAEs are then trained across L0 values, including fractional L0 below the true value, and the decoder's cosine similarity to the true features is read off as a per-latent map. Crucially, in the small model the low-L0 SAE is *initialised at the ground-truth solution*, so any mixing that appears afterwards is gradient pressure and not a bad starting point. The reconstruction argument is made by comparing the MSE of a trained low-L0 SAE against a ground-truth SAE forced to fire at the same reduced L0. A training-dynamics arm starts L0 too low or too high and linearly transitions to the correct value over 25k of 30k steps. For LLM SAEs the authors sweep L0 and score each dictionary with a single statistic of the decoder matrix — the mean absolute cosine similarity across all distinct latent pairs — and compare the shape of that curve against k-sparse probing F1 from a benchmark of over 100 tasks. A decoder-projection histogram (training activations projected on each decoder column) is used to inspect what the mixing looks like at the level of individual latents. An appendix tries to close the loop by treating L0 as a learned parameter and optimising it against the same metric during training.

## What counts as an explanation here
A dictionary corrected by a diagnosis, plus an argument about the training objective. The explanation form is unusual for this folder: it is *not* a description of individual latents, and it is not a predictor of model behaviour either. It is a statement that the SAE training problem has a right answer — the ground-truth feature set — and a structural reason (correlated co-occurrence plus a sparsity cap below the true rate) why the objective will prefer a wrong answer to it. That reason is delivered as two theorems: one showing the disentangled dictionary is not the MSE minimiser at low L0, and one strengthening it to show the disentangled dictionary is not even a local minimum, because there is a strict descent direction away from it. The diagnostic is a scalar property of the whole dictionary, offered as a proxy rather than as a definition, and the authors are explicit that it rules out clearly wrong settings better than it pinpoints the right one.

## How they judged success
Ground-truth cosine similarity per latent in the toy model (visualised as decoder-versus-feature matrices), and the position of the metric's minimum relative to the known true L0, across five seeds. In the LLM, the agreement between the L0 at which the decoder-similarity metric bottoms out (or the elbow before it climbs at low L0) and the L0 at which k-sparse probing F1 peaks, over three seeds per L0 point. Supporting checks: the L0 sweep is run separately for two architectures so that an architecture-specific artefact cannot carry the result; a superposition variant tests whether the metric survives an imperfectly orthogonal basis; a narrow-SAE variant tests whether the signal survives feature hedging and predicts a lower optimal L0 in that regime; and a training-dynamics study distinguishes an unrecoverable low-L0 basin from a recoverable high-L0 one. The strongest single control is the MSE comparison, because it makes the failure of standard evaluation a numeric statement rather than an argument.

## What they found
Correlation plus a sparsity cap below the true rate corrupts essentially the whole dictionary. In the 5-feature model at L0 = 1.8 against a true L0 of 2, every latent tracking a correlated feature picks up a piece of the shared feature, with the sign of the correlation deciding the sign of the mixed component; inverting the correlations flips the mixed components; removing the correlation removes the mixing altogether; and a sweep over L0 from 1.7 to 2.0 shows the mixing growing as L0 falls away from the true value. When the small SAE is *initialised at the correct answer*, training still moves away from it. In the 50-feature model (true L0 of 11) the asymmetric picture is the important one: at L0 = 5 every latent is affected, while at L0 = 18 many latents are still correct and the damage is confined to a subset — so too-low is the worse error, contrary to the usual intuition that too-dense is the dangerous end. The MSE comparison puts a number on the incentive: at L0 = 5 a trained SAE with mixed latents reaches an MSE of 2.73 against 4.88 for the ground-truth SAE, meaning a sparsity–reconstruction plot would discard the correct dictionary in favour of the corrupted one, and the authors note that a hypothetical training method which returned the true features would be rejected by the field's standard plot. The training-dynamics run shows the asymmetry is a property of the loss surface: correcting an L0 that started too high recovers the true features, while correcting one that started too low does not fully escape. The proposed diagnostic behaves as intended — decoder pairwise cosine similarity is minimised at the true L0 in the toy model (five seeds), still roughly minimised under superposition noise, and in the narrow-SAE arm it lands at the reduced expected L0 rather than at the wide-SAE value. In the LLMs, both the peak probing F1 and the elbow in the decoder metric fall around L0 = 200 for Gemma-2-2b layer 5 and Llama-3.2-1b layer 7; for Gemma-2-2b layer 12 the BatchTopK minimum sits near 200 and the JumpReLU minimum nearer 250-300. The JumpReLU comparison produces the most interesting anomaly: JumpReLU curves rise far less at high L0, its thresholds *increase* with L0 while BatchTopK thresholds fall, and across a wide range of sparsity coefficients the realised L0 sticks near the correct value rather than scaling with the coefficient. At L0 = 750 the projection histogram shows a narrow core plus a heavy tail of large projections simultaneously, which the authors read as some latents over-firing while others under-fire — an SAE that is too sparse and too dense at once, which a per-latent threshold can relax and a single global TopK cap cannot. A survey of public SAEs listed on Neuronpedia with an L0 recorded in SAELens finds most below 100, well under the authors' estimated optimum, so their headline claim is that much of the public dictionary corpus is in the too-sparse regime.

## Limits and open problems they name
The theory assumes linear features and simple correlations, both stated as simplifications, and unbalanced correlation structure is named as unstudied. Only a few LLM layers are swept, since full-layer sweeps were too expensive, and TopK SAEs are not tested separately — BatchTopK is argued to stand in for them. The decoder-similarity metric has real weaknesses the authors lead with: several curves have broad shallow minima (Gemma-2-2b layer 5's global minimum sits inside one), so it reliably rules out bad settings more than it locates a precise optimum, and its agreement with sparse probing is imperfect especially at high L0 — hence the recommendation to treat it as one signal among several. The attempt to learn L0 during training works in toy models but needed heavy hyperparameter tuning in real LLMs, because the metric is shallow near the optimum, its response to an L0 change is delayed, and overshooting downward can permanently damage the dictionary; it is reported as future work rather than as a result. The authors also suggest the finding may explain why SAE steering is often disappointing — a latent that mixes features sends the model toward combinations it never represents — but this is offered as a hypothesis, not tested.

## What we take from it
Three things, in order of use. First, a *diagnostic we can adopt verbatim*: the mean absolute pairwise cosine among decoder columns, which costs one matrix multiplication on the dictionary and no forward passes over the model at all, with PyTorch in an appendix. For a white-box account this is exactly the kind of frozen, pre-intervention dictionary statistic our own screening work wants, and it is the same geometry signal that appeared as "decoder crowding" in the steering-side-effect entry — here with a job attached (detect mixing) and a stated direction (mixing raises it). Report it per layer with the L0 sweep attached, and never at one L0 point only. Second, a *prohibition*: a sparsity–reconstruction curve cannot rank dictionaries or architectures, because the corrupted dictionary wins on reconstruction at the too-sparse end — the folder now has three independent papers saying reconstruction is not the quantity of interest, and this one gives the constructive proof in a setting where ground truth exists, which is stronger than the correlational versions. Third, a *warning for the folder's own feature-level claims*: a latent's decoder direction is not a single concept whenever L0 sits below the true firing rate and features co-occur, and the mixed-in component's sign follows the correlation. That means a "direction" we read out of a dictionary is a bundle whose contents depend on a hyperparameter nobody reports — so whenever we use a latent direction as a coordinate, the account should carry the L0, the dictionary width, and the decoder-similarity score. The most transferable single idea is that the *right hyperparameter is the one where the dictionary stops needing to cheat*, i.e. where an internal-geometry statistic bottoms out, and that this can be found without labels. The corollary to keep in mind when reading others: an SAE that sticks near the correct L0 across a range of its own sparsity coefficient is displaying a self-correcting property, which is an argument for per-latent thresholds over a global cap.
