# Navigating Disagreement Among Criteria for Evaluating Explanation Faithfulness

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.06846
- **Authors / organisation:** Grant Shanklin (Max Planck Institute for Security and Privacy, Bochum; Yale University), Jiří Němeček (MPI-SP, Bochum), Asia Biega (MPI-SP, Bochum)
- **Date:** 2026-11-04
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.06846
- **Kind:** paper (conceptual / measurement-modeling analysis with a legal case study)
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Explanations are routinely called "faithful", but the checks used to certify faithfulness routinely disagree: the same pair of explanations can be ranked one way by one criterion and the opposite way by another, so whether an explanation passes depends on which check the auditor happened to run. The authors argue this is not sloppiness to be fixed by building a better metric, and not two camps arguing about what faithfulness means, but a general requirement that was only ever partially specified. They want three things: a notation in which the existing criteria become visibly comparable, a diagnosis of *where* along a measurement chain the disagreement enters, and a way to justify picking one criterion over another for a concrete use. The larger goal is the one that governs our own reporting: an evaluation should be published with the commitments it makes, so that the commitments can be audited rather than concealed inside a metric's name.

## What they studied
Not models and not neurons — the *criteria* themselves. Scope is local explanations of a prediction model: feature-attribution vectors and selected rationales, which are the two forms whose faithfulness checks are known to disagree. Eight published operationalizations are analysed (infidelity, faithfulness correlation, comprehensiveness, AOPC, prediction gap on important features, sufficiency, insertion AUC, prediction gap on unimportant features), all of which reduce the model to a real-valued score oriented so that higher supports the explained output. The unit of analysis is therefore an *evaluation procedure*, decomposed into the three things it must decide. A second unit is the audit setting itself: US adverse action notices for credit denials, where the disclosed "principal reasons" are legally required to describe the factors actually scored.

## How they did it
First they make the disagreement concrete: they define two criteria as *rank non-equivalent* when some model, input and pair of explanations is strictly ordered one way by the first and the other way by the second, and prove that infidelity, sufficiency and comprehensiveness are pairwise non-equivalent with a single worked counterexample on four binary features (a monotone score over eleven of the sixteen inputs, with two reasons named out of the factors). Second they build a common form: a *faithfulness functional* that writes any such criterion as the aggregate of a per-comparison assessment over a distribution of comparisons, each comparison being a perturbed copy of the input at which the model is re-scored. A choice of the three parts is a *faithfulness specification*. Admissibility conditions keep the parts separate: the comparison distribution may not consult the model, the assessment may see the model only at the two compared points, the aggregation may see only the distribution of assessments, and either comparisons or assessment must depend on the explanation. A criterion counts as reconstructed only if the functional reproduces its ranking of every pair of explanations exactly, up to a positive rescaling and shift. Third they read a measurement-validity taxonomy onto the reconstructions: is the plurality competing conceptions of the concept, different specifications of one concept, or different indicators of one fixed specification? Fourth they apply the machinery to a bank-proposed notice generator that ranks fixed-length notices by mean baseline-Shapley contribution, derive the specification that reproduces that ranking, and ask which of its choices the regulatory setting actually supports.

## What counts as an explanation here
The object explained is not a model's internals but the *meaning of an evaluation*. What the paper produces is a structural account: each faithfulness score is a triple of commitments (which comparisons, what counts as a favourable response, how to pool), and a prose argument about which level of a measurement hierarchy a disagreement lives at. It is explicitly partial — the authors say an evaluation's commitments cannot be read off its numbers, only off a defensible specification of what the explanation is being taken to claim. It has one predictive consequence and it is a strong one: any criterion that reconstructs to a different comparison distribution, assessment or aggregation is *by construction* capable of reversing another's ranking, so the possibility of disagreement follows from the structure rather than from any empirical accident.

## How they judged success
Three standards, none of them benchmark accuracy. (1) **Exact rank preservation**: a reconstruction is accepted only if the functional reproduces the source criterion's ordering of every pair of explanations, allowing a positive affine transform whose constants may depend on the input and score but not on the explanation. This is a derivation-level test, verified per operationalization in an appendix that also exhibits the constants. (2) **Fit to the taxonomy**: the reconstructions must land predominantly in one of the three measurement-disagreement levels rather than spread across them, and the paper argues the specification level is the right one; the competing readings are then rejected with reasons rather than by majority vote. (3) **Case decompression**: a real procedure must reduce to a specification whose parts can be named, and the setting must be shown to justify some parts while leaving others open. Two of the eight criteria cannot be reconstructed at all under the paper's scope — a procedure that retrains the model after deleting features needs structure beyond input comparisons — and that exclusion is stated rather than hidden.

## What they found
The three most-quoted criteria are pairwise rank-reversing on a shared monotone score over four binary features, so the choice among them decides who wins, not just by how much. All eight remaining criteria reconstruct exactly into the functional, and they fall into three families by what they reward: *correspondence* (attribution values should track observed score changes — infidelity, faithfulness correlation), *dependence* (disturbing the named features should move the score — comprehensiveness, AOPC, prediction gap on important features), and *retained support / invariance* (keeping the named features should hold the score, or disturbing the others should not change it — sufficiency, insertion, prediction gap on unimportant features). Several criteria share an assessment and differ only in comparisons: comprehensiveness and AOPC are the same per-comparison quantity with a different perturbation schedule, and the two prediction gaps differ only in sign and in whether the named or the unnamed features are perturbed. Because each reconstruction recovers its source exactly, the differences between criteria are differences in the quantity each defines, not errors in measuring one quantity — which is what rules out the "bad indicator" reading. The diagnosis that survives is incomplete specification: faithfulness fixes *the standard of accuracy* (the model's own behaviour) and leaves open what the explanation claims about that behaviour, which alternatives the claim ranges over, and how strictly it must hold.

In the credit case the ranking used by the bank's notice procedure reproduces as a correlation between the score drop when a group of factors is swapped to an approved reference profile and the fraction of the notice's named factors in that group; with the Shapley kernel weighting, that correlation is a positive affine transform of the mean baseline-Shapley contribution of the named factors, so the procedure's selections are exactly the maximizers. The regulatory setting supports one choice and challenges another. It supports tying the reference profile to the approval standard, and better, anchoring it near the decision boundary in the denied applicant's direction, because the notice explains a denial and is meant to teach the applicant where they fell short. It does not settle the treatment of interactions: a two-reason notice can omit a factor whose correction alone would remove a large joint effect while naming factors with smaller individual effects, which conflicts with the notice's error-correction purpose, and the authors show the allocation rationale and the education rationale pulling in opposite directions without a rule to break the tie.

## Limits and open problems they name
Scope is local attributions and rationales for prediction models scored through a single oriented real-valued score; criteria that retrain the model under feature removal are outside the framework. Only the comparison distribution of the case-study specification is argued through — the assessment and the aggregation are explicitly deferred. On interactions, the case identifies a genuine conflict with no resolution, only a demand that the ranking's use be justified. The paper also leaves open whether some choices (for instance the reference against which features are replaced) belong to the specification level or to the indicator level, which would shift the disagreement into something validation could settle.

## What we take from it
A reporting standard, a filing rule, and a warning. The reporting standard: any faithfulness number our white-box model reports ships as an explicit triple — the comparison distribution, what counts as a favourable model response, and the aggregation — because those three are the only places two honest evaluators can differ, and naming them converts an argument about whose metric is right into an audit of a stated commitment. The filing rule: when two of our checks disagree, before rebuilding either one we ask which of the three parts differs; a difference in the comparison distribution or the assessment is a *specification* difference, which no amount of better estimator will close, while a difference in how the same quantity is estimated is an indicator difference and is settled by convergence testing. The warning, and the most useful single sentence in the paper for us: a criterion can be internally exact, reproducible and rank-preserving while still being the wrong thing to measure for a given use, so "our metric is faithful by definition" is never an argument. Concretely, this is the evaluation-suite analogue of the previous entry's rule — validate the emitted solution, not the fit — and it supplies the vocabulary (correspondence vs dependence vs retained support) for saying which of the two families a given check belongs to, since the two families disagree by design.
