# From Concepts to Actual Causes: Toward Closing the Concept–Malfunction Gap in Robot Foundation Models

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.00155
- **Authors / organisation:** Gihyuk Ko; NC AI (Korea; work funded by a NIPA/MSIT physical-intelligence action-model programme)
- **Date:** 2026-10-30
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00155
- **Kind:** essay (position paper, 4 pages with one figure and one table; SPAIS workshop at CoRL 2026)
- **Read from:** full text (arXiv HTML v1, ~26k clean chars — the entire submission: abstract, §1–§5, the reference list of 29 entries; no appendix and no experiments exist, and the only figure is a schematic of the toy model, so every number below is stated in prose or in Table 1)
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central) — the folder has plenty of entries about *measuring* a component and about *attributing* an outcome, but almost none that write down the logical form a causal claim has to take before an intervention is allowed to support it; this one does that in four lines, and then uses it to say in which precise way each familiar method falls short.

## What they wanted, and why
The complaint is that interpretability answers a question nobody asked at the moment of a failure. A robot policy built on a foundation model misbehaves, the regulator wants to know which internal component caused *this* incident, and the available toolkit reports which concepts the policy has learned across a distribution. The author names this the concept–malfunction gap and argues that it is mostly a specification problem rather than a tooling problem: the methods compute real effects, but they answer a query that has been silently fixed to one time step, one kind of counterfactual value, no backups and an immediate read-out. Larger goal, stated plainly in the introduction: targeted repair rather than retraining, and compliance with incident-reporting duties on high-risk autonomous systems. The philosophical move is to import actual causality — but-for dependence plus a searched contingency — and treat a closed-loop rollout as the structural model over which the counterfactual is evaluated.

## What they studied
No model was run. The object of analysis is the *query*, not a network: a policy π maps an observation history and an instruction to an action chunk, its computation at each step is decomposed into components `Z` that an investigator could intervene on and blame (attention-head outputs, MLP neurons, dictionary latents, a world model's predicted-state latents), and the collection of all such components over all steps is the variable set `V`. An incident is a binary predicate on the rollout — the gripper inside a safety distance of a hand, a held knife dropped. The unit of analysis is therefore a *subset of components at possibly different time steps*, and the paper's whole contribution is about what makes a question about such a subset well-posed.

## How they did it
1. **Build the structural model.** The exogenous context (initial state, seed, instruction), a deterministic simulator and the policy determine a rollout; the unrolled rollout is treated as a structural causal model whose equations are the policy and the simulator, so an intervention `[X ← x']` is a re-run of the whole rollout with those components set.
2. **Adopt Halpern's modified definition of actual cause**, with its three conditions: the assignment and the failure both hold in the observed rollout; there is a *contingency* `W` (a set of other components held at their observed values) and an alternative value `x'` such that fixing `W` and setting `X ← x'` averts the failure; and no strict subset of `X` already satisfies the first two.
3. **Note that holding `W` at its factual value is exactly activation patching**, which is how the paper connects the formalism to practice — the method the field already uses is the witness clause of a definition it usually does not state.
4. **Add Halpern–Chockler degree of responsibility** = 1/k, where k is the smallest total number of components that must be changed or held fixed for the failure to depend on the component in question, so a lone but-for cause scores 1 and a cause with one backup scores ½.
5. **Define the fault-isolation query** as the tuple of intervened set, counterfactual value, contingency and read-out, and derive four well-specification conditions from it: the intervened set may span steps; the counterfactual value must be inducible by some observation history; the contingency must be *searched per incident* rather than fixed in advance; and the read-out must be the failure predicate on the full counterfactual rollout.
6. **Score six method families against the four conditions** (probing and sparse dictionaries, visual attribution, activation and path patching, causal scrubbing, activation steering, causal abstraction) in a table, then run one hand-built example to show the misspecifications producing conflicting verdicts.
7. **Derive four open tasks** from the four conditions.

The toy model is the paper's entire empirical content: a robot closing on a person 2 m away at a 1 m/s reference, simulated at 10 Hz for 5 s, with the incident defined as coming within 0.3 m. Acceleration is set by three components — a hazard latent that fires when a person is visible and would brake, a suppressor in [0,1] that blocks that braking, and a target-reaching drive that accelerates whenever speed is below reference. The suppressor is stuck at 1, so the hazard is active but blocked and the drive is idle, and the robot breaches at 1.7 s. Ground truth: the suppressor is the actual cause; the drive is its backup, because repairing the suppressor alone leaves the drive pushing the robot into the person.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **causal verdict about an individual event**, not a description of the model: a named subset of components, the contingency under which the failure depends on them, and a graded responsibility between 0 and 1. It is explicitly singular rather than distributional — the paper's sharpest line is that the field optimises for claims that hold on average while an incident needs a claim about one rollout. Completeness is not claimed and is not needed; the verdict is meant to predict *one* counterfactual (that fixing this and holding that averts *this* failure), and the grading is meant to rank candidates for repair. The paper also names a distinct verdict class it thinks the field is missing: a component whose repair is undone by the policy itself later in the loop, which it calls a persistent fault and which no single-step analysis can see.

## How they judged success
There is no metric, baseline or benchmark — the paper says so in its limitations. The evaluative device is a **table of four binary conditions against six method families** (no family meets all four; steering is the only one spanning all steps and the only one reading out the full rollout, and every intervening family violates the realizability condition), plus a **single worked scenario** in which the correct answer is known by construction and the methods are applied one at a time to show they disagree. The controls that stand in for experiments are: a known ground truth (suppressor is the cause, drive is the backup), the requirement that a sound method name the suppressor and not the hazard or the drive, and the observation that even the patching methods which intervene at every step still fail once the backup is left free.

## What they found
- **Patching one step looks decisive and is not.** Setting the suppressor to zero at the first step yields the full braking effect, a change in acceleration of 2 m/s² — because the backup never gets to act inside one step — and the counterfactual rollout then shows the breach still happens, merely 0.1 s later.
- **Patching every step still fails**, because the drive restores speed and settles the robot at 0.5 m/s, breaching at 3.3 s. Causal scrubbing and causal abstraction inherit the blind spot, since both fix the contingency in advance.
- **Steering cannot express the repair at all**: no realizable setting within the swept range averts the failure, because the drive always re-accelerates.
- **Only the query with the backup frozen recovers the truth** — the suppressor is an actual cause with responsibility ½ — and the author immediately notes the cost, that this witness forces a state the policy would never produce, so the verdict weakens as the counterfactual drifts away from the factual rollout.
- **Probing and visual attribution fail in the two opposite directions** one would expect: probing shows the person is represented but cannot say why braking did not follow, and masking the person in the image changes nothing because the suppressor blocked the brake regardless.
- **Every intervening family violates the realizability condition**, which the paper argues is worse than missing causes, because it means even the causes they report may be spurious. Off-manifold counterfactual values are the mechanism: a masked image, another input's activations, a steered state.
- **Granularity is a prerequisite for responsibility to mean anything**: 1/k counts backups, so it is only interpretable if one backup is one component, which is the paper's argument for dictionary latents over individual neurons.
- The four open tasks are: finding candidate faults (with an *action lens*, the action-chunk counterpart of the logit lens, as the suggested instrument); certifying that a counterfactual activation is inducible; scaling contingency search over millions of latents, needed because deciding actual causation is DP-complete even in explicit models; and validating fault isolation, which needs planted faults with known causes *and* backups, with interchange-intervention training named as a possible source of ground truth.

## Limits and open problems they name
No experiments on real robot foundation models at all; the argument rests on one toy example and on a faithful deterministic simulator, so every conclusion is conditional on replayability — which is exactly the assumption that fails for a system deployed in the world. The witness construction can force combinations the policy would never produce, and the resulting verdict becomes less meaningful the further the counterfactual is from the observed rollout, with no measure given for how far is too far. Contingency search is combinatorial, and the paper offers shortlisting by attribution as a heuristic without evidence that it finds the right backups. Planting faults risks producing faults easier to isolate than natural ones. And the four tasks are named, not started.

## What we take from it
A **framing**, and it is the most directly transferable thing in the folder this run. Five uses, in order of how quickly we could adopt them.
1. **State the query tuple before the intervention.** Every experiment we run should carry four labelled slots — what we intervene on and over what horizon, what value we substitute and whether any real input induces it, what we hold fixed and how we chose it, and what we read out and whether that read-out is the outcome we care about or a per-step proxy. The paper's table is the demonstration that a paper can appear methodologically complete while three of those slots are filled by default.
2. **Search the contingency; do not fix it.** This is the entry's central claim and it reorganises a habit this folder has recorded repeatedly without naming: every ablation that leaves the rest of the network free is measuring a *but-for* effect, and the response to a small effect has been to soften the ablation (mean-ablate, add covariance directions, sweep a magnitude) rather than to ask which components are compensating and hold *them* fixed. Those are different experiments. The first estimates the effect with backups live; the second estimates it with backups removed. A predictive model needs both numbers, labelled.
3. **Responsibility as a graded number, with its denominator printed.** 1/k has the property the folder keeps asking for — it is cheap, it ranks candidates for repair rather than certifying one, and it *degrades* honestly as backups accumulate. The methodological consequence worth carrying is the granularity requirement: if a backup may be a mixture of components, k is not interpretable, so the choice between neurons, heads and dictionary latents is a choice about whether responsibility can be computed at all.
4. **A read-out must be the outcome, not the next token.** The folder's own entries have converged on this from the other end (a component that changes a mid-layer axis and never flips the answer; a patch that moves a probability but not the behaviour). This paper supplies the reason it happens: the intermediate quantity is a one-step proxy, and in a loop the intermediary's effect can be undone downstream.
5. **A warning about the realizability clause in disguise.** Much of this folder's most careful work is spent on exactly this problem — the admissible region for sampler draws, the steering magnitude at which generation collapses, the prompt that the model would never see — and each of those is the WS2 condition rediscovered per-experiment. A standing question in our own write-ups: *would any real input produce this state?* If the answer is unknown, the verdict is provisional.

**Counterweight, so we do not over-borrow.** This is a position paper with no measurement, and its toy example is favourable to its own thesis: the backup is a hand-written additive term, the rollout is deterministic, and the ground truth is handed to the reader, so the demonstration that naive patching fails is a demonstration on a system built to make it fail. The formalism's cost is also unquantified — searching contingencies is stated to be DP-complete, the suggested shortlist is untested, and the "off-manifold witness weakens the verdict" problem has no measure. Take the four conditions as a checklist for our own experiments and take the responsibility grade as a proposal, not as an instrument we have seen work. It also points at a gap in the folder worth naming: every entry here about a language model gets its counterfactual for free from a prompt edit, and this is the only one that asks whether the counterfactual state was reachable at all.
