# The Singular Values of Convolutional Layers

- **Key:** arxiv-1805.10408
- **Authors / organisation:** Hanie Sedghi, Vineet Gupta, Philip M. Long; Google Brain, Mountain View
- **Date:** 2018-05-26
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1805.10408
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

A convolutional layer is a linear map, so its Jacobian is the map itself, and the singular values of that map are exactly the factors by which the layer can amplify or shrink a signal travelling forward or a gradient travelling backward. If those singular values sit near one, gradients neither explode nor vanish; if the largest one drifts up, the layer can blow up activations. Methods that regularise for this already existed, but they approximated: one line of work took the operator norm of a *reshaped* kernel tensor rather than of the actual linear transformation, and another scaled a layer by a per-minibatch estimate of input-to-output ratio, which moves the entire spectrum rather than the offending part. The authors wanted the real spectrum of a convolutional layer — the singular values of the true linear map — and an algorithm cheap enough to compute it often enough to be useful during training. The paper is therefore aimed at removing a practical obstacle rather than at a new claim about what networks do: once you can see the whole spectrum of a layer at realistic sizes, regularising for it exactly becomes a one-line consequence of a classical projection fact, and observing trained networks becomes possible at all.

## What they studied

Two-dimensional multi-channel convolutional layers, single stride, square feature maps of side n with m input and m output channels and a k×k kernel, with the boundary treated as wrapping (a torus) so that the linear operator is a structured circulant object. The unit of analysis is the **layer as a linear operator** — its full singular value multiset — and then, downstream, the layers of one trained network. There are no neurons, features, circuits or directions in the interpretability sense: the objects are the kernel tensor, the enormous matrix that encodes its action on a vectorised feature map, and the family of small matrices derived from the kernel by Fourier transform. The empirical objects are a 32-layer residual network with 2.4M parameters trained on CIFAR-10, and the official TensorFlow ResNet-v2 pre-trained model, whose convolutional layers (kernels larger than 1×1 only) are profiled. The authors deliberately study the wrapping variant rather than zero-padding, on the grounds that the wrapping case is the one with clean spectral theory and is the standard object of theoretical analysis.

## How they did it

The chain of reasoning is short and complete. First, a single-channel filter embedded as an n×n array generates a doubly block circulant matrix acting on the vectorised image, and such matrices are diagonalised by a two-dimensional Fourier basis, which makes them **normal** — and normality is the key step, because for a normal matrix singular values are just the moduli of the eigenvalues. That collapses the single-channel spectrum to the moduli of the entries of the Fourier transform of the kernel. Next, the multi-channel case: the layer's matrix is an m×m block matrix whose blocks are each doubly block circulant, a conjugation by a unitary that mixes only the spatial part leaves the channel part untouched, and the whole object becomes block-diagonal with a per-frequency family — one m×m matrix for each of the n² frequency positions, with the layer's singular value multiset being the **union of the singular values of all those small matrices**. The proof of that union statement goes through a block-diagonal intermediate and then an explicit polynomial-time verification that each small matrix's singular vectors, lifted by a basis vector and paired with their frequency, give a valid singular decomposition of the full matrix. Everything reduces to a two-line computation: transform the kernel along its two spatial axes, then take singular values of each of the resulting small channel matrices. They verified this against the brute-force construction-and-SVD route in unit tests, timed it against full-matrix SVD and against a TensorFlow implementation across channel counts and feature map sizes, and then used it in two experiments: (i) a training loop that, every 100 steps, projects each convolutional layer onto an operator-norm ball by clipping its transform coefficients' singular values and then zeroing all coefficients outside the original k×k neighbourhood, alternating the two since both sets are convex, and (ii) a read-out of the singular spectra of the pre-trained ResNet-v2 layers, ordered by magnitude and colour-coded by depth.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **characterisation**: a closed form for a layer's entire singular multiset in terms of the kernel, proved, plus an algorithm whose cost the authors state explicitly. The object the understanding takes the shape of is the per-frequency family of small channel-by-channel matrices — the layer decomposed into n² independent m×m linear maps — and the derived spectrum. It is complete in the sense that the multiset is exactly recovered, not bounded: the theory is not an approximation and there is no slack to report. But it is not an explanation of behaviour. The paper never asks what a particular singular direction corresponds to in the data, never identifies which input pattern a large singular value responds to, and never uses the decomposition to say anything about a unit, a channel, or a feature. Its predictive content is a pair of bounds — the layer's operator norm is at most the sum of the absolute kernel entries, with equality when the kernel is non-negative — which are properties of the weights alone and computable without data, but which the authors themselves report as loose in practice.

## How they judged success

Four separate currencies. **Correctness of the characterisation**: unit tests comparing the fast route with the explicit-matrix SVD. **Cost**: measured wall-clock seconds against both the full-matrix method and the NumPy/TensorFlow implementations, on a 3×3 convolution over a 16×16 map and an 11×11 convolution over a 64×64 map, with the brute-force route unable to complete at the larger setting — and asymptotic counts given alongside (roughly n²m²(m + log n) against n⁶m³ for the brute-force route and n²m²k² just to run the convolution). **Regularisation effect**: CIFAR-10 test error of the same 32-layer residual network under a sweep of clip values from 0.1 to 3.0, plus training-loss and test-error-versus-time curves, against an unclipped baseline; and a comparison against a lasso-style penalty on the sum of absolute kernel entries, motivated by their own bound, which performed worse and which they also use as evidence that the bound is loose. **Descriptive**: the plotted spectra of the pre-trained network. There is no control for a constrained *initialisation* trained unconstrained, and no seed variation reported for the clip sweep.

## What they found

The two-line implementation matches the brute-force SVD in unit tests, and the timing difference is large enough that the full-matrix method could not be run at all on the 11×11, 64×64 setting — the fast route is the only practical one at real sizes, and the TensorFlow version exploits the fact that the m² transforms and then the n² small SVDs are independent. On CIFAR-10, the 32-layer residual network baselines at 6.2% test error after 250 epochs, and clipping the convolutional layers' operator norms every 100 steps improves this to 5.3% at a clip of 0.5 and 5.5% at 1.0; clipping at 2.5 or 3.0 does essentially nothing because the trained layers' norms already sat between 2.5 and 2.8; and clipping hard at 0.1 makes it *worse*, 6.7%. Training time is only slightly increased. The read-out of the pre-trained ResNet-v2 is the part with the most content for us: the largest operator norms sit nearest the input; the number of non-negligible singular values rises through the middle of the network and then tapers off; and the later layers have low effective rank, which the authors suggest means they could be compressed. Their interpretation of the pattern is deliberately hedged — a few strong directions near the input "such as edge detectors", richer features in the middle, fine-grained adjustments at the end — and they present it as one possible reading rather than a finding.

## Limits and open problems they name

The wrapping assumption is a modelling approximation: real layers zero-pad or shrink the map, and the entire characterisation depends on the circular structure. The projection step is honest about a cost they acknowledge — clipping the singular values leaves the eigenvectors fixed so the projected matrix is still a convolution, but its neighbourhood can widen to the full n×n, so a second projection back onto the k×k neighbourhood is needed, and the alternation gives the nearest point in the intersection only in the limit. Their own bound is admitted loose and its penalty version underperformed. Beyond what they name: the spectrum is of a linear map that the network never applies in isolation, since every convolutional layer is followed by a nonlinearity, and the paper does not check whether these spectral properties survive composition through the activation or across layers — it explicitly frames its target as the layer's own contribution, leaving the network-level question open. Also, the projection is applied as a hard constraint at intervals; there is no reported test of what the network's behaviour is in the steps between projections, and no measurement of whether the clipped spectrum stays clipped. The interpretability read-out is four sentences of hedged suggestion accompanying one figure, with nothing tested.

## What we take from it

A **method**, a **measure**, and one sharp **warning**.

The method is the one we should treat as a standing instrument: **any layer whose operation is a convolution can be read out exactly, from its weights alone and with no data, as a family of small channel-by-channel matrices indexed by frequency, whose singular values collectively are the layer's singular values.** This is the ancestor of the state-space realisation and the Fourier-domain certificate computations that later members of the survey reach for, and it is the cleanest statement of it: no approximation, no solver, and the operator norm falls out as the maximum over a modest number of small matrices. It also gives us the composition we need for a per-layer attribution: the frequency index is a *label* on each small matrix, so a reading of a layer is not one object but n² labelled objects, and questions like "which frequencies does this layer amplify" are well posed and cheap. Nothing in the paper asks them.

The measure is the spectrum itself, reported as an ordered curve per layer and aggregated across a trained network — the effective-rank-by-depth profile. This is the cheapest layer-level summary of a trained network in the whole survey so far, and its finding (large norms near the input, peak non-negligible count in the middle, low effective rank at the end) is a hypothesis about where a network's capacity actually lives that costs one pass over the checkpoint.

The warning is the sharpest part: **a regulariser that binds the true operator norm helps, and one that approximately binds it does not help as much, but the effect is narrow and non-monotone** — 6.2% down to 5.3% and back up to 6.7% as the clip tightens through and past the trained layers' natural norms, with the useful window sitting just below where the unclipped network already lives. That is the same shape as the constraint head's tightness-versus-operating-point finding, arrived at from the empirical side with no certificate in sight: the intervention does something only when it is close to binding, and if it binds hard it hurts. For our account, the practical rules are (a) report the spectrum of a trained layer before proposing any per-layer intervention on it, because the natural operating point silently defines the useful range; and (b) include the trivial kernel-norm product bound alongside any spectral measure, since this paper prints a case where the trivial bound is two orders of magnitude loose and says so.
