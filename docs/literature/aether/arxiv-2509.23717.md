# Measuring Sparse Autoencoder Feature Sensitivity

- **Key:** arxiv-2509.23717
- **Authors / organisation:** Claire Tian (The Harker School), Katherine Tian (Independent), Nathan Hu (Stanford University)
- **Date:** 2025-09-28
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.23717
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Every standard way of characterising a sparse-autoencoder feature works from its top-activating examples: a human browses them, or a model writes a description from them, and the description is scored against more activation. The authors point out that this tells you what the feature does fire on and says nothing about what it *misses*. A feature that fires on a handful of harmful requests out of many has been characterised only over a narrow slice of behaviour, and any claim about the model's general handling of the concept built on it is correspondingly narrow. Their goal is a cheap, scalable measure of exactly that gap — how reliably a feature fires on new text that is semantically the same as its examples — and one that does not route through a generated description, because an automatic description is an extra failure source that cannot be separated from the feature's own failure. A secondary goal is to use the measure to compare SAE designs rather than individual features. The framing is squarely in the SAE-evaluation literature: reconstruction and interpretability were the two classic axes, and this is proposed as a third.

## What they studied
Unit of analysis: one SAE latent (a feature in a trained dictionary), plus aggregates over all features in one SAE. Feature-level study on GemmaScope's canonical JumpReLU SAE for the Gemma 2 2B layer-12 residual stream at width 1M and sparsity near 10^7 — 10,000 features sampled, 2,061 surviving the filters. SAE-level study over the 29 GemmaScope JumpReLU SAEs on the same site (74-ish KB to 1M widths, several sparsity levels) and over the SAEBench collection of 112 SAEs spanning seven architectures (BatchTopK, Matryoshka variants, TopK, JumpReLU, ReLU, Gated, a per-feature annealing variant) trained on Pythia-160M and Gemma-2-2B. Texts come from a 2M-token sample of OpenWebText (SAEBench runs) and of the Pile uncopyrighted subset (GemmaScope runs), scored in 128-token sequences.

## How they did it
Four steps per feature. (1) Collect its activating texts: 2M candidate tokens, score the feature, and for each firing token take a window of ten tokens either side; per feature keep 15 examples, ten top by activation plus five sampled with probability proportional to activation magnitude. (2) Prompt a language model with those 15 examples alone — no feature description, no concept summary — and ask it to produce new passages sharing the same properties; the prompt tells the generator to preserve every consistent trait across the examples, to match their diversity level rather than homogenise or expand it, to vary along the same dimensions the examples vary along, and to avoid new kinds of variation, and to mark the span where it expects the feature to fire. Generation used GPT-4.1-mini in one query per feature (a single query produced more diverse outputs than several independent ones). (3) Score the new passages with the same feature. (4) Sensitivity is the share of generated passages on which the feature fires. Two filters are applied before anything is counted: features that cannot produce 15 activating windows in 2M tokens are dropped (rare features), and features whose own truncated example windows fail to re-activate the feature at least 90 per cent of the time are dropped. The second filter is large and grows with width — it removes 35 per cent of features on average in the 4k–65k SAEBench SAEs, and 51 per cent at 65K up to 79 per cent at 1M in GemmaScope. Validation is a blinded human study and a count of verbatim overlap between generated and original text; the SAE-level comparison re-weights features to a common frequency distribution so width and frequency cannot be confused.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not a description at all. The artefact is a single per-feature number measuring the boundary of a feature's behaviour, deliberately taken *without* an intermediate label — the authors argue that a generated description makes sensitivity estimates ambiguous, since a low score could mean the feature is insensitive or the description wrong, and this ambiguity is worse for SAE features than for neurons because the dictionary itself is a learned approximation. The rhetorical claim is that the score behaves like the sensitivity you would measure if you had a perfectly precise description: one that can generate near-neighbours of the activating set and nothing broader. It is a partial account by design — it says how wide the feature's triggering region is around its observed examples, not what causes the firing, not what the feature does downstream, and not how it composes with others. The authors are explicit that it complements rather than replaces interpretability scores.

## How they judged success
Validity of the artefact: a blinded human study over 102 items where annotators rate a new text against a feature's activating examples on a four-point scale (indistinguishable / closely related / weakly related / unrelated). Three hidden conditions per item: a genuine held-out activating example as a positive control (20 per cent of items), a passage generated for a *different* random feature as a negative control (20 per cent), and a passage their method generated that failed to activate the feature (60 per cent). Only highly auto-interpretable features were used, which biases the sample toward cases where a low sensitivity score is most surprising. Novelty: longest-common-substring distributions between generated and original texts against the original-to-original baseline. Distinctness from existing metrics: Spearman correlations of sensitivity with automated-interpretability score, feature frequency, and maximum decoder-vector cosine to any other feature. Confound control at SAE level: re-weighting to equalise the feature-frequency distribution across SAEs, plus repetition of the width result at several filter cutoffs. Position-bias check: where in the generated passage the intended activation span falls, and whether early spans fail more.

## What they found
Human validation is the paper's strong point: 79 per cent of the passages that failed to activate were still rated indistinguishable from real activating examples (62 items), against 83 per cent for genuine held-out activating examples; exactly one of the 62 was rated unrelated. Controls behaved — 96 per cent of positive controls were rated indistinguishable or closely related, and every negative control was rated unrelated or weakly related. So low sensitivity is not a generation defect: the text really is in the neighbourhood of the examples and the feature still does not fire. Overlap analysis shows generated text copies long spans slightly less often than original examples copy each other (3.1 against 3.7 per cent at five or more tokens) while sharing short spans slightly more (20.8 against 18.0 per cent at two or more), and pairs of generations overlap modestly more than the baseline (27.9 per cent at two tokens, 4.3 per cent at five) — flagged as a mild diversity shortfall, not pathological copying. On the feature-level GemmaScope sample, sensitivity is distinct from the standard metrics: ρ = 0.24 with auto-interpretability, −0.06 with frequency, 0.06 with maximum decoder cosine. The headline qualitative result is the *disagreement*: of 1,347 features with auto-interpretability at least 0.9, 82 score at most 0.5 sensitivity and 23 at most 0.2 — interpretable and insensitive. At SAE level, average sensitivity falls with dictionary width: 0.92–0.94 at 65K against 0.85–0.87 at 1M on GemmaScope, and at fixed width it rises with sparsity (more active latents relative to a sparser setting gives higher sensitivity), both surviving frequency re-weighting and cutoff changes. Across the seven SAEBench architectures the width trend is consistent and vanilla ReLU SAEs are the weakest family; Matryoshka SAEs, built to mitigate scaling problems, show the same negative scaling.

## Limits and open problems they name
Only frequent features are measurable (15 or more firings in 2M tokens), so rare features — arguably the most interesting ones for auditing — are outside the method. The truncation filter biases toward features that do not need long context, and the excluded fraction rises steeply with width (79 per cent at 1M), which means the width trend is computed on a differently-selected population at each width even though the cutoff sweep shows the trend surviving. Activation magnitude is ignored: a feature that fires weakly on every neighbour and one that fires strongly are scored alike. Position bias exists in a mild form — the intended span lands within five tokens of the start in about 30 per cent of generations and in 1.5 per cent it is the very first token, and passages with short prefixes fail more often, which the authors attribute to prompting rather than to the feature. Generation diversity is somewhat low between samples for the same feature. The SAE-level architecture comparison is underpowered — noise prevents claims about differences between specific architectures beyond the ReLU observation. They note the method generalises to any component that fires on input text (neurons, transcoders) but do not run it there.

## What we take from it
A measure and a warning. The measure: **an explanation-free sensitivity score — regenerate near-neighbours from the activating examples alone, with no description in the prompt, and count the firing rate** — is a cheap, automated, description-free way to bound how much of a feature's apparent concept the feature actually covers, and it can be computed for thousands of features with one generator call each. The methodological detail worth copying is the validation design: give the human annotator a *failed* item with no label, hide which condition it came from, and include both a genuine positive and a cross-feature negative control. If the failed items are rated as similar to real examples as the positive controls are, the score is measuring the feature and not the generator. The warning is more important for us than the measure: **an interpretability score and a coverage score can disagree at exactly the features that look best.** Eighty-two of the 1,347 features with near-perfect automatic descriptions fired on fewer than half of their own semantic neighbours, which means a dictionary entry that reads beautifully may cover a sliver of the concept — and any predictive account of ours that names a feature as *the* representation of a concept is making a coverage claim it has not measured. Second warning, about scale: sensitivity worsens monotonically with dictionary width across seven architectures, so "the wider SAE is better" is true of reconstruction and of auto-interpretability and false of coverage simultaneously. Third, a design pattern to reuse: when a metric is confounded by selection, the paper's own handling is the template — print the excluded fraction per condition, sweep the cutoff, and re-weight to a common nuisance distribution. The excluded-fraction column is the thing that would be missing from most papers, and at 1M width it is 79 per cent, which is not a footnote.
