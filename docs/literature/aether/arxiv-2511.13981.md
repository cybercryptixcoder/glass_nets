## What they wanted, and why

Sparse autoencoder training is treated as a sparsity–reconstruction trade-off: add an L1 or a TopK constraint, tune the penalty, and read quality off the fidelity frontier. The authors' starting observation is that every one of those designs still trains on *correlated* activations, so the input geometry is anisotropic and the optimisation landscape is stretched along the high-variance directions. Their proposal is to reuse the preprocessing step that classical sparse coding and ICA have always used — PCA whitening — and to check whether the correlations that make the problem ill-conditioned are also what misalign "sparse" with "interpretable". The larger target is a claim about the field's objective: if a preprocessing transform that changes nothing about architecture or loss moves interpretability metrics substantially while costing a little reconstruction, then the fidelity frontier is not a proxy for interpretability and should not be treated as one. The framing is borrowed from efficient coding in the visual system, where early sensory circuits are thought to decorrelate their input before anything is learned.

## What they studied

Two open models, one layer each: Pythia-160M at layer 8 (residual width 768) and Gemma-2-2B at layer 12 (width 2304), both activation sets taken from the standard SAEBench protocol. The unit of analysis is the SAE latent, and the manipulation is at the level of the *input distribution* rather than the dictionary: paired runs that differ only in whether activations were whitened before the encoder. Architectures spanned ReLU SAEs (soft L1 sparsity at penalties 0.012, 0.02, 0.06) and TopK SAEs (hard sparsity with target L0 of 80 and 160), at dictionary widths 2^14 and 2^16, giving 40 configurations — 24 ReLU and 16 TopK — each trained twice, once standard and once whitened.

## How they did it

Before training, they fit a PCA whitener to activations sampled from the target layer in batches of 2048, estimating the mean and the covariance from a fixed number of batches (10 batches for Pythia, giving a 20,480 × 768 matrix; 16 for Gemma, giving 32,768 × 2,304). From the eigendecomposition they build the whitening matrix as the inverse square root of the eigenvalues composed with the eigenvector rotation, with a small epsilon for numerical stability, plus an explicit dewhitening matrix; both are computed once and frozen for training and evaluation. During training the encoder sees whitened, mean-centred inputs and the sparsity penalty is computed in whitened space, while the decoder's output is dewhitened before the reconstruction loss is taken — so the reconstruction term is still scored against the model's real activation distribution and only the *geometry the encoder optimises over* changes. Evaluation wraps the trained SAE in an interface that whitens on the way in and dewhitens on the way out, so downstream metrics see what they would have seen from an ordinary SAE. Training followed the open dictionary_learning framework at SAEBench's hyperparameters: 500M tokens of the Pile, learning rate 5e-5 with 1,000-step warmup, sparsity-penalty warmup over 5,000 steps, decay over the last 20%, batch 2,048, context 1,024, with identical data ordering across paired runs. Separately, they ran a 2D sparse-coding simulation: sample 10,000 points from heavy-tailed sparse sources, mix them through a random 2×2 dictionary, then exhaustively score every candidate inverse dictionary on a 1,024 × 1,024 grid of angles for two quantities — an inverse-mean-L1 sparsity score and a mean-correlation-coefficient recovery score against the true dictionary — and plot both as surfaces over that grid, before and after whitening.

## What counts as an explanation here

This is not a new form of explanation; the artefact remains a sparse feature dictionary, and understanding is carried by its latents. What the paper contributes is a geometric account of *why* a dictionary trained on whitened inputs should be more interpretable, expressed as a claim about landscape shape: whitening equalises the eigenspectrum of the covariance, which makes the basin wider and more isotropic, moves the sparsity optimum into the same place as the recovery optimum, and imposes second-order independence as an inductive bias toward disentangled latents. The 2D exhaustive landscape plots are the paper's demonstration object — they show, at every point in dictionary space at once, whether "more sparse" and "more correct" coincide. It is explicitly a partial account: the authors concede the simulation is intuition-building and that a quantitative treatment of conditioning and gradient dynamics on real high-dimensional activations is future work.

## How they judged success

Five SAEBench metrics, chosen because the authors argue they are the ones robust at sub-2B scale: CE loss score (how much next-token loss the reconstruction preserves), explained variance (fidelity of reconstruction), sparse probing top-1 (linear probes on SAE activations across 35 binary diagnostic tasks), SCR top-20 (spurious correlation removal — ablating features tied to a confounder such as gender and measuring whether the downstream prediction stops using it), and TPP top-20 (targeted probe perturbation — ablate latents for a target class and measure how selectively the class's performance drops, a causal-specificity test). The design is paired throughout: each configuration is trained with and without whitening on identical data ordering, and results are reported as mean ± SEM across all configurations of an architecture, with paired t-tests and p-values. The two reconstruction metrics are the deliberate counterweight — they are what the standard frontier would tell you to optimise, so any gain on interpretability has to be read against the loss there.

## What they found

The pattern is uniform across architectures: interpretability metrics rise, reconstruction metrics fall slightly. For ReLU SAEs averaged over all 24 configurations, sparse probing improves from 0.757 ± 0.008 to 0.812 ± 0.008 (+7.15%), SCR from 0.176 ± 0.015 to 0.271 ± 0.019 (+54.03%), and TPP from 0.021 ± 0.004 to 0.098 ± 0.010 (+372%), all at p < 1e-4; meanwhile CE loss score drops from 0.980 to 0.954 (−2.64%) and explained variance from 0.813 to 0.772 (−5.02%), both also significant. TopK SAEs show the same qualitative shape but a narrower one: sparse probing 0.754 → 0.809 (+7.30%, significant), CE 0.990 → 0.968 (−2.27%) and explained variance 0.837 → 0.794 (−5.22%), while SCR (0.311 → 0.304, +p 0.23) and TPP (0.141 → 0.152, p 0.24) do not move significantly. The architecture asymmetry is their most interesting interpretation: soft L1 sparsity allows many weak co-active latents, which whitening apparently stabilises into disentangled features, whereas a hard TopK budget discards exactly the weak distributed activations that higher-order disentanglement needs — so whitening still aligns the *active* features semantically (probing goes up) but cannot repair what the sparsity constraint throws away. In the 2D simulation, without whitening the high-sparsity peaks sit away from the high-recovery regions, so climbing the sparsity objective leads into a region of poor feature recovery; after whitening the surface becomes isotropic and the brightest recovery values sit on the peaks, so sparsity and correctness point the same way. They connect this to Karvonen et al.'s report that Matryoshka SAEs lead several interpretability metrics while sitting worse on the fidelity frontier, and read both results as the same phenomenon.

## Limits and open problems they name

Middle layers only, both models under 2B parameters, text modality only — early and late layers, larger scales, vision and audio, and interactions with gated SAEs and transcoders are all listed as untested. The theory is a 2D exhaustive simulation, not an analysis of real high-dimensional conditioning. They name a specific mechanistic worry about the reconstruction drop: whitening assigns equal weight to all directions, some of which are noise, so compression budget is being spent on noisy dimensions; denoising or replacing the reconstruction term with the downstream CE loss are suggested as fixes. They also do not determine at what point the reconstruction drop becomes consequential for downstream tasks. One further thing to hold in mind when reading the headline numbers: whitening rescales the activations the encoder sees and the latent scale the sparsity penalty acts on, so "whitened vs standard" is not only a change of basis — the effective penalty strength differs between arms, and the paper does not report a penalty sweep that would hold effective sparsity constant across the pair.

## What we take from it

Three things. First, a preprocessing baseline that is almost free and that any dictionary study of ours should carry as a standard second arm: fit the mean and covariance once, transform, train, dewhiten before the reconstruction term, keep evaluation wrapped so it matches training. If our numbers move this much from a linear rescaling of the input, we need the whitened arm in the report whether or not it helps, because the difference tells us how much of our "feature quality" is conditioning rather than structure. Second, a directly usable *diagnostic*: the exhaustive 2D landscape plot over all dictionary angles is a cheap way to see whether the objective we are optimising and the quantity we care about have their optima in the same place — and it is the same move our own work needs when we claim a probe or a surrogate predicts behaviour rather than merely fitting it. Third, the sharpest warning for prediction-under-intervention work: sparsity and interpretability are not one axis, and pushing the fidelity frontier harder does not buy interpretability — here it costs it in the other direction, since whitened SAEs are *worse* reconstructors and *better* features. For our white-box goal that reframes what a faithful surrogate has to preserve: fidelity to activations is not the same as fidelity to the computation, and a reconstruction-optimal decomposition can be the one that abstracts the wrong way.
