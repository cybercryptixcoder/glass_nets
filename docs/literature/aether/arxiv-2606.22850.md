# To select or not to select: predictively consistent priors instead of model selection

- **Key:** arxiv-2606.22850

## What they wanted, and why

The standard Bayesian workflow advice is to guard against overfitting by comparing candidate models of different sizes and selecting a simpler one. The authors want to know whether that advice is even necessary, and they suspect the answer depends on a choice that is usually made before the comparison starts — the prior. Their target is a property a sequence of nested models can have or lack: as components are added, the distribution the model predicts before seeing data should stay sensible rather than drift toward nonsense. If a prior sequence has that property, the authors find, the biggest model is usually as good as or better than any selected smaller one, and selection can be dropped. The larger goal is a workflow recommendation: replace component-level sparsity and parsimony with priors that remain honest in predictive space, so a modeller can expand a model safely instead of pruning it afterwards.

## What they studied

Nested sequences of additive models — linear regression, a logistic variant, forward covariate search, single-predictor nonlinear models (raw and orthogonal polynomials, thin-plate splines, Hilbert-space approximate Gaussian processes), and one randomised-trial setup. The unit of analysis is not a neuron or a direction but a **model in a nested family and the prior that family is given**. Two prior families carry the argument: R2D2-style priors that put a distribution on the share of outcome variance the model explains and split it across coefficients by Dirichlet weights, and function-space priors whose marginal variance is bounded by construction. The comparator throughout is the seemingly harmless choice of independent normal priors on each coefficient, with a fixed scale. Data are simulated with known generating coefficients, so the true predictive performance of every candidate is computable on a large independent test set.

## How they did it

They first define the quantity that has to stay under control: the prior-induced distribution of the scalar systematic component (the linear predictor at a single reference input), and its variance as model size grows. Two properties are then formalised. *Second-order predictive consistency* requires the variance of that signal to be bounded across the whole sequence and to converge to a finite limit — allowing non-monotone paths, and stated on the signal rather than on the outcome because some likelihoods have infinite marginal variance regardless of prior. *Predictive parsimony* requires the summed Kullback-Leibler divergence between signal distributions of successive models to be finite; for Gaussian signal families this reduces to a function of consecutive variance ratios. They prove the two properties are independent — neither implies the other, with explicit counterexamples — and that the independent normal prior satisfies parsimony but not consistency, because its signal variance is model size times a fixed coefficient variance. They then specialise to global-local scale mixtures, where the signal variance is the prior expectation of a global scale times the sum of local weights, giving a necessary-and-sufficient condition; and they exhibit two consistent families: R2D2, where consistency becomes a finite-moment condition on a Beta-prime global scale that holds whenever the Beta prior on explained variance does not put too much mass near one, and the Hilbert-space Gaussian process, whose truncated basis expansion has marginal variance bounded by the prior mean of the kernel marginal scale for every truncation order. Having the theory, they run four simulation experiments in which they fit every model in the sequence, score each on held-out data, and average the difference from the best model over repetitions.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **property of a prior sequence**, stated as a mathematical condition and checked against a closed-form variance expression, plus a decision rule that follows from it: if the property holds, do not prune. The form of the understanding is a proof obligation on the prior rather than a description of a model's internals, and it is deliberately partial — it says when model expansion is safe, not what any component is doing. It does predict, in a strong sense: from the prior alone, before any fitting, one can say whether the signal variance diverges with model size, and the divergence prediction is then confirmed in the held-out results.

## How they judged success

Expected log predictive density on independent test data, reported on the leave-one-out scale so differences are comparable across sample sizes, averaged over repeated simulated datasets, with a margin of four flagged as the point at which two models are substantially different. The baselines are the independent normal prior and, in the selection experiments, forward stepwise search by leave-one-out score and a projection-predictive search. For the randomised-trial experiment the criteria shift to posterior recovery of the treatment coefficient: interval length, frequentist coverage, and root-mean-square error, with an analytic prediction of the shrinkage factor available for each prior. The consistency property itself is checked against prior predictive simulations — the prior-implied distribution of explained variance is plotted as model size grows.

## What they found

The independent normal prior fails the property in the way predicted: its implied signal variance is the number of coefficients times a fixed variance, so with thirty or a hundred covariates the prior predicts that the model explains almost all of the outcome's variation. In the linear experiment with 30 candidates, models under R2D2 hold test performance flat out to the full model size while those under independent normal priors decline once enough covariates are in, and the two are comparable only in the uncorrelated, larger-sample corner. The logistic version reproduces this, with the mechanism stated cleanly: as the linear predictor's variance grows, the inverse-logit pushes predicted probabilities to the boundaries, the average Bernoulli variance collapses, and the implied explained-variance measure is forced toward one. In the sparse forward-search experiment, with 15 of 50 covariates real, any selected model with more than about five predictors does better under R2D2 than any selected model under normal priors. In the nonlinear experiment the divergence is visible directly in the prior-predictive variance plot for raw polynomials, since the sum of the even moments of the input grows with degree, and only penalised splines and the Gaussian-process basis hold test performance steady as complexity rises. The randomised-trial experiment is the counterweight and the most useful result: there, making the prior consistent by folding the treatment coefficient into one joint explained-variance prior becomes actively harmful, because the treatment's prior precision grows with covariate count, the shrinkage factor collapses — approximated at roughly 0.16 in the weak-signal configuration — and the treatment effect is biased toward zero, with coverage well below nominal. Splitting the prior, leaving the treatment coefficient on its own fixed prior while the covariates share a consistent one, recovers the coefficient with near-nominal coverage and the lowest error, slightly better than the treatment-only model.

## Limits and open problems they name

Everything is additive and, in the experiments, at least as many observations as covariates; the authors expect the failures to get worse when there are fewer rows than columns but did not test it. They compare only a few prior configurations, and the omitted alternative — scaling normal priors by the number of covariates — would also bound the signal variance but at the price of heavy shrinkage on every coefficient. They are explicit that predictive consistency does not license causal claims: the fourth experiment shows a prior can be predictively certified and still bias the quantity of interest, and that adjusting the prior to be consistent can make inference worse, so desirable prior properties for causal targets are left open. Whether the property can be extended to non-additive models is named as future work.

## What we take from it

Three things, one of them a warning aimed at us. **A measure of how far an account set has drifted, computable before any fitting.** Their signal variance is a prior quantity — a number you can write down from the specification alone — and it is what predicts which model sizes will degrade. For us the analogue is a *prior-predictive* check on the account set: fix a reference input distribution, propagate each account's own uncertainty through to its prediction at that input, and report how that variance changes as accounts are added to the readout. If it grows like the account count, the ensemble is not a hedge over a bounded space; it is an unbounded family whose extra members are manufacturing confidence. That check costs simulations, not fits, and it is the cheapest item in this folder to run before any held-out comparison.

**A single named quantity that explains why "weakly informative" defaults are not weak jointly.** Independent normal priors on many coefficients imply a near-certain claim that the model explains almost all of the variance — the joint prior is far stronger than the marginals suggest. Our default account sets are built the same way: each account is specified separately with a modest claim, and the union of them implies a joint predictive claim far stronger than any single one. Whenever we aggregate accounts, the joint implication should be computed and reported, not inferred from the members.

**The warning is the fourth experiment, and it is the sharpest thing in the entry for us.** A property that guarantees good prediction can be exactly the property that biases the estimand we care about. Folding a treatment coefficient into a joint explained-variance prior made the prior predictively certified and made the treatment effect wrong: interval length shrank from about a full unit to half of one while coverage fell from roughly nominal to around one in ten in the weak-signal configuration, which is the signature of a confident wrong answer. The split prior kept the treatment coefficient on its own scale and got both. Mechanically: **any prior or shrinkage scheme we adopt to stabilise a readout must be applied at the level of the quantity we are not claiming about, never to the coordinate we are, and the report prints the coverage of the coordinate of interest alongside the predictive score that motivated the shrinkage.** This is the folder's clearest statement that predictive quality and inferential validity are separate axes, and it names the exact construction by which optimising one wrecks the other.
