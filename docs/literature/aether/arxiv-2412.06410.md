## What they wanted, and why
Sparse autoencoders decompose a model's activations into a dictionary of directions, and the two architectures that dominate the field each pay a different price for their sparsity rule. TopK keeps exactly k latents per sample, which fixes the per-sample budget before anyone knows how complex the sample is. JumpReLU lets each feature keep a learned threshold and gets a better fidelity curve, but its L0 is only reached by sweeping a penalty coefficient and hoping one point lands near the target. The authors want the flexibility of the second without the tuning cost of the first — a dictionary you can ask for a specific average number of active features and get it back, with fidelity at or above what the best tuned architecture achieves. This matters for us because the sparsity budget is the one hyperparameter any dictionary-based account has to declare, and a dictionary whose budget cannot be set directly cannot be compared fairly to those of other architectures at a matched price.

## What they studied
The residual stream of GPT-2 Small at layer 8 and of Gemma 2 2B at layer 12, trained on OpenWebText truncated to 128-token sequences. Unit of analysis: the dictionary direction (one decoder column per latent). Dictionary sizes 3072, 6144, 12288 and 24576 for GPT-2 Small, 16384 for Gemma 2 2B; target active latents k of 16, 32 and 64. Comparisons against TopK SAEs and JumpReLU SAEs, where the JumpReLU penalty coefficients were chosen so the resulting sparsity would match the target k.

## How they did it
The change is one line in the architecture: instead of keeping the k largest latents of each sample, keep the n*k largest latents across a batch of n samples, and zero the rest. That pool is shared, so a sample whose activations carry little information can use one latent while a dense sample can use far more, and the average is pinned by construction at k rather than approached through a penalty coefficient. Training is a reconstruction loss on the batch plus the same auxiliary dead-latent loss TopK uses. The batch-level coupling is a problem at inference, when a single activation arrives alone, so they estimate one global threshold as the expected value of the smallest positive latent activation across batches, and at test time run JumpReLU with that fixed threshold — meaning the deployed object is a thresholded dictionary even though training was a sorted one. They train to a fixed token budget, then compare normalised mean squared error and the increase in language-model cross-entropy obtained by splicing the reconstruction back into the forward pass. The paper's second experiment is a diagnostic of their own premise rather than a benchmark: plot the histogram of how many latents each sample actually activates, and check that the flexibility is used rather than nominally available. A fixed random-seed-free design choice worth noting is that no human or automated interpretability rating is run at all — the argument rests entirely on reconstruction, cross-entropy and the per-sample sparsity distribution.

## What counts as an explanation here
A feature dictionary, same form as the TopK and JumpReLU entries: a set of directions, an activation rule, and a decoder that approximately reconstructs an activation from a sparse combination. The only structural addition is that the activation rule is not a constant per sample. There is no claim about composition into circuits and no causal account of any direction; the dictionary predicts a reconstruction, and its predicted object is the per-sample count of active features, which is now a quantity the model states in advance rather than one it is tuned to hit approximately.

## How they judged success
Headline metric is normalised mean squared error against the cross-entropy degradation under splicing, reported as curves across dictionary size and across k, with a fixed-k comparison (L0 = 32) and a fixed-dictionary comparison (12288) run separately so that the two axes are not blended. The per-sample sparsity histogram is the mechanism check. Controls are cross-model replication (GPT-2 Small and Gemma 2 2B) and sparsity-matched baselines, including the explicit note that JumpReLU cannot be held to a fixed L0 during a sweep and so the baseline units are only approximately matched. No interpretability metric, no human rating, no simulation score.

## What they found
BatchTopK beats TopK on both measures at every dictionary size and every k, on both models — better reconstruction and less cross-entropy degradation at the same average sparsity. Against JumpReLU the result splits by model and by budget: on GPT-2 Small BatchTopK wins on both measures at all sparsity levels tested, while on Gemma 2 2B it reconstructs better everywhere but only has the lower cross-entropy degradation at the lowest sparsity setting (k = 16). The per-sample histogram is clearly wide — some samples activate a single latent and others more than 80 — with a visible spike on the beginning-of-sequence token, which the authors read as the case of "not much information here" being served by "not many features", exactly what a fixed per-sample k cannot do.

## Limits and open problems they name
Two models, residual stream only, and one layer per model — no MLP or attention-output sites and no depth sweep. No interpretability evaluation at all: the authors say they did not directly assess it and lean on the architectural similarity to TopK to argue the latents would be comparably interpretable, which is an assumption and not a measurement. The inference threshold is a single global scalar estimated by averaging over batches, so the per-sample adaptivity is trained but not deployed — at test time the dictionary is indistinguishable in form from a JumpReLU dictionary with a fixed threshold.

## What we take from it
Three things. First, a **measure** worth copying directly: the per-sample active-feature histogram is the cheapest possible falsification test for a sparsity claim, because a constant-budget instrument produces a delta function by construction and a claimed variable-budget instrument that still produces one has not learned anything. Any account we build whose budget is per-item should ship that histogram. Second, a **warning** about where the gain comes from: the cross-entropy column overturned the reconstruction column on Gemma 2 2B at all but the loosest setting, so fidelity and downstream usefulness are again separable columns and the ordering between two instruments can invert with scale — the same report-don't-collapse rule the JumpReLU and sparse-probing entries arrived at, now with a model-size flip as the instance. Third, a **framing** point about train-versus-deploy: the object that was trained (a batch-level sorted rule) is not the object that is deployed (a fixed threshold), and the paper's justification for swapping them is an average. A white-box account of ours must state which of the two its guarantees are about, since a property proved of the training rule does not automatically survive the inference rule that replaces it.
