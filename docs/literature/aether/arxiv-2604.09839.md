## What they wanted, and why
Steering — adding a fixed vector to the residual stream — is used both as a control primitive and as evidence about what a model represents. The common inference is that if a behaviour can be induced by a white-box edit, it is "in" the model and reachable by prompting too. The authors ask the sharper question: is any steered internal state actually producible by some text prompt? They frame prompt-reachability as surjectivity of the map from discrete prompts to residual-stream states, prove that steering lands off that image almost surely, and use the result to argue that white-box controllability and black-box exploitability are separate quantities that evaluations should report separately. The larger aim is a principled account of what an intervention can and cannot license us to conclude about the model.

## What they studied
Three small open-weight chat models — Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct, Qwen-2.5-0.5B-Instruct and gemma-3-1b-it, plus gemma-3-270m-it, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Llama-3-8B-Instruct and a latent-adversarially-trained Llama in the attack table, and one INT4-quantised Llama-3.2-1B for a robustness check. Two steering vectors: a refusal direction taken from an existing open implementation, and an "evil" persona vector extracted with an LLM judge. Ten prompts per steering category drawn from the source papers' own test sets. Unit of analysis: the residual-stream activation at a chosen layer and token position, treated as a point in R^d, and the steer-vs-natural *distance* between activations rather than any behaviour label.

## How they did it
Three steps. (1) Theory: treat the transformer as a function mapping the activation history and the current token to the next activation, invoke the known result that such maps are real-analytic in the parameters and therefore almost surely injective over discrete prompts, and then define a "steering collision function" — the squared distance between a candidate prompt's natural activation and the steered activation — and show it is not identically zero, so its zero set has measure zero. Three variants: random steering vectors, difference-of-means vectors (which are themselves a real-analytic function of the same parameters, once the contrast set is fixed), and an adversarial vector chosen specifically to force a collision at one position, which then diverges at the next. Two mild conditions on the contrast set are needed for the difference-of-means case — the two classes must not be uniformly the same length, and no token position may be shared across all prompts of a class. (2) SipIt inversion: feed the steered activation to a token-by-token inversion routine that recovers the prompt from natural activations, and check whether it matches anything; it fails at the first token for every model and prompt. Then project the steered activation to its nearest vocabulary token per position to get a lossy prompt and generate from it. (3) Many-shot ICL: build prefixes of 1 to 64 (harmful query, steered answer) demonstrations, which is the black-box route known to work, and measure position-aligned L2 distance between the prefix-conditioned natural activations and the steered ones, with the zero-shot case as the baseline. Three further prompt-search attempts are reported in an appendix: a prompt optimiser, instructing the model to copy the steered answer verbatim, and prefix tuning with 1–15 soft tokens.

## What counts as an explanation here
Neither a dictionary nor a circuit: a *negative* structural result about the representation space. The claim is that the set of prompt-reachable states is a countable, measure-zero subset of activation space, that steering moves the state into the holes between those points, and therefore that "the model can be steered to say X" carries no implication that any prompt makes it say X. The explanation is meant to be complete for the exact-match question and explicitly silent about approximate reachability, which they list as future work.

## How they judged success
Exactness is the standard, and the paper is careful about it. A collision is a zero-distance match between two activation vectors; the theory gives probability zero for every prompt pair and position pair. Empirically: SipIt recovery succeeds on natural activations (used as the positive control that the inversion machinery works at all) and fails on steered ones; per-token L2 distance to the nearest vocabulary token, absolute and under a sweep of the steering coefficient; ICL distance as a function of shot count alongside the attack success rate recovered by substring matching against standard refusal phrases. The distance-0 criterion is relaxed for batched inference because batching is non-deterministic — a match is counted when one token's activation is an order of magnitude closer than the runner-up, with a single-example check that distance really is 0.

## What they found
On the theoretical side, non-surjectivity holds for random vectors, for difference-of-means vectors extracted from any realistic contrast set, and against an adversary who is allowed to force one collision — the sequence diverges at the very next token almost surely. Empirically, SipIt recovers the original prompt from natural activations in all models, and fails at the first token from steered activations in all models, all prompts and both vector types. The most informative single result is the projection: when the steered activation is forced onto its nearest tokens, the reconstructed prompt is *the original prompt* (exactly in most cases, off only at a trailing special token or a few positions otherwise), and generating from that reconstructed prompt gives the ordinary unsteered answer. So the steered state is close to the natural state of the same prompt, and yet is not the natural state of that prompt or of any other. On the black-box side, more ICL demonstrations make the distance *grow*, not shrink, while the attack success rate rises to the point of working at 32–64 shots — the two routes reach similar behaviour by different internal means. The attack table makes the evaluation point concrete: a one-word "Here" suffix jailbreaks most of these models as reliably as steering (for example 0.997 versus 1.000 on Qwen-2.5-0.5B, 0.960 versus 0.980 on Llama-3.2-1B), while the latent-adversarially-trained model resists the suffix attack at 0.000 and still fails under steering at 0.910 — white-box failure without black-box failure, in one row of one table. The appendix prompt-search results are all failures with the same signature: the optimiser produces a prompt that recites the steered answer rather than eliciting it, verbatim copying reproduces the text while the activations stay unaligned, and soft prefix tokens do not reproduce the generation either (and the theorem does not cover soft tokens).

## Limits and open problems they name
Existence of a matching prompt cannot be disproved by search over an exponentially large space, so the empirical work is corroboration, not proof — their word. Only small models, ten prompts per condition, and two vector families (refusal and persona); the authors note that coefficient choice for the persona vectors needed manual inspection (an automated choice produced unusable generations on two of three models), which is a quiet admission that steering dose is still tuned by hand. Quantisation is outside the theory, though an INT4 check shows the empirical pattern persisting. Approximate reachability — whether a steered state is ε-close to some prompt's state — is left open, as is the geometry of quantised activation spaces. The comparison is exact-match only, so "no prompt reproduces this state" is not the same as "no prompt produces similar behaviour", which they acknowledge by separating the two threat models rather than claiming behavioural uniqueness.

## What we take from it
A warning, and a cheap instrument. The warning is the load-bearing one for us: any predictive claim built on an intervention — that a direction is the model's representation of X, that editing it reveals what the model would do — is a claim about an off-manifold state unless the reachability question is asked. This is the same defect the folder keeps meeting from other angles (probe directions whose equivalence class is huge, features that fire but do not matter), and here it is proved rather than measured: the intervention set and the prompt set are different objects, and success in one is not evidence about the other. The instrument we can actually steal is SipIt-style inversion as a *position-locator*: because natural activations are almost surely injective, inverting them recovers the exact prompt, which makes "where does the steered trajectory leave the natural manifold" a measurable per-token quantity. Their projection result is the diagnostic to copy — project the steered state back to the nearest tokens and see whether it lands on the prompt you started from. If it does, your edit is a small nudge away from a real computation; if it lands somewhere unrelated, the behaviour you induced is manufactured. Notes I add and mark as mine: the whole empirical case rests on ten prompts per condition with no error bars and no distributional statement, the ICL distance curves are described qualitatively (trends are said to differ across model families in the appendix), and the "Here" attack is a single-token suffix used as a black-box foil whose success rate is scored by substring matching — a measure the same paper's appendix notes is a heuristic, so the strongest-looking row in the table is the least rigorously measured. Also worth flagging for our own write-ups: their claim is about *exact* collision, and a reader could easily over-read it as "steering is unnatural", which is false — the nearest-token projection shows the steered state sitting close to the natural state of its own prompt.
