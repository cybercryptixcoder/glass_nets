# Interpretability without actionability: mechanistic methods cannot correct language model errors despite near-perfect internal representations

- **Key:** arxiv-2603.18353
- **Authors / organisation:** Sanjay Basu, Sadiq Y. Patel, Parth Sheth, Bhairavi Muralidharan, Namrata Elamaran, Aakriti Kinra, John Morgan, Rajaie Batniji; University of California San Francisco, Waymark, University of Pennsylvania, Virginia Commonwealth University, Stanford University
- **Date:** 2026-03-18
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.18353
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (v1 HTML: main text, all five arms' methods, Tables 1-3, Appendices A and B including Tables 4-9; figures are images and were not inspected)
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
The motivating premise of a large part of interpretability is that reading a model's internals tells you both *that* it will err and *how* to fix it: if the knowledge is in the activations, then a direction or a feature or a concept should be adjustable to convert a wrong output into a right one. The authors think this premise has been repeated for years but never stress-tested head-to-head in a setting where being wrong has real consequences, and that safety and regulatory frameworks are being built on it anyway — they cite EU and FDA transparency language as resting on the assumption that interpretability enables correction and not merely detection. So the target is narrow and empirical: measure the gap between what a model internally knows and what it says, then take the four most common families of inference-time intervention and see which, if any, closes it. Their stance is deliberately that explanation and control are separate goods, and that the field has been quietly treating the first as if it delivered the second.

## What they studied
Two models in parallel: **Steerling-8B** (Guide Labs), an 8.4B concept-bottleneck model whose forward pass routes through 33,732 supervised medical concepts with sigmoid activations and a test-time override interface, and **Qwen 2.5 7B Instruct**, a 28-layer, 3,584-dimensional general instruction-tuned model. Data are 400 clinical triage vignettes with physician adjudication: 200 physician-authored cases across 18 hazard categories (132 hazard, 68 benign) and 200 real de-identified Medicaid encounter messages (12 hazard, 188 benign), 144 hazards in total. The unit of analysis shifts by arm — the concept layer and its individual concepts, per-token SAE features at one layer, the last-token residual stream at one layer, and a linear probe direction per layer — so the study is really four different units measured against one common outcome.

## How they did it
Four arms, all on the same 400 cases with the same prompt, generation settings and response parser, so the arms are comparable by construction. Baseline behaviour is free-text triage advice classified by fixed emergency/urgent keyword lists. **Arm 1** picks, per case, the 20 concepts most associated with that case's hazard category using leave-one-out selection, then overrides concept activations to target values (the steer_known interface) across five alpha levels plus random-concept controls plus a prompt-engineering condition — 3,200 inferences — and additionally tries two in-distribution targets (the leave-one-out true-positive mean and its 95th percentile). **Arm 2** trains a 16,384-width L1 SAE from scratch on per-token layer-14 activations, selects features associated with hazard cases by Mann-Whitney U with FDR correction, finds 3,695 of them, and clamps the top 20 to 1× and 2× the true-positive mean during generation, with a random-feature control. **Arm 3** uses the logit lens to track the vocabulary rank of six hazard tokens across all 28 layers, takes the layer with maximal Cohen's d between true-positive and false-negative cases, builds a correction direction as the normalised difference of mean hidden states, and patches the last-token residual at four strengths. **Arm 4** trains L2 logistic-regression probes at all 28 layers under 5-fold stratified CV, takes the best layer, forms a truthfulness separator vector (TSV) as the normalised true-positive-minus-false-negative mean difference, and adds it to the residual stream at five strengths against a random-direction control. Statistics: Wilson intervals for proportions, BCa bootstrap for MCC and AUROC, McNemar with continuity correction for paired condition comparisons; reporting follows TRIPOD+AI, and code and vignettes are public.

## What counts as an explanation here
Nothing in the usual sense — there is no dictionary, graph or description on offer. What the paper produces is a **causal test of read-outs**, i.e. an accounting of which read-outs license which interventions. The probe is treated as a measurement of internal knowledge (its AUROC is the claim about what is represented); the TSV, correction direction and concept targets are treated as candidate *causal handles*, and the paper's judgement is that only one of them, at one strength, in one model, behaves like a handle at all. So the explanatory object is a two-place relation — read-out, and the effect size its intervention actually has — rather than a description of a mechanism.

## How they judged success
Two numbers per condition, always reported together: **false-negative correction rate** (missed hazards turned into detected ones) and **true-positive disruption rate** (correct detections broken), the second being the cost. The primary comparison is net effect in percentage points, with Wilson confidence intervals; the crucial control is **random perturbation of the same magnitude** at the same site (random concepts, random SAE features, random direction vector), tested by McNemar. Secondary measures: probe AUROC and accuracy per layer under cross-validation, Cohen's d between true-positive and false-negative hidden states, vocab rank of hazard tokens by layer, and a parser sensitivity re-analysis that reports the same gap under a more generous response classifier.

## What they found
The baseline gap is large and clean. Linear probes discriminate hazardous from benign cases at **AUROC 0.982** (95% CI 0.968–0.993) at layer 23 — above 0.95 at every one of the 28 layers — while Qwen's own output sensitivity is **0.451**, a 53-point gap; Steerling-8B manages 0.354. The TSV also separates the model's *own* true positives from its own false negatives at AUROC 0.814, so the internal state carries information about whether it will act, not only about what is true. Against that, the interventions mostly do nothing or do harm. Concept steering corrects 20.0% of missed hazards but disrupts **53.2%** of correct detections — net −33.2 points — and is statistically indistinguishable from random-concept perturbation (McNemar p = 0.84); random-concept suppression is, if anything, more disruptive (61.7%). SAE feature steering produces **exactly zero** corrections and zero disruptions in every condition despite 3,695 significant features. The logit lens shows hazard tokens never approach the prediction (best mean rank 13,380 at the final layer; no true positive reached top-100) even where the hidden states diverge strongly (Cohen's d 1.39 at layer 22), and patching along the correction direction nets zero at α = 1.0 and +11 cases at α = 5.0. The TSV is the only arm with a real positive net: 5 corrected / 5 disrupted at α = 1.0 (net 0), and **19 of 79 corrected (24.1%) against 4 of 65 disrupted (6.2%)** at α = 10.0 — net +15 — which is nevertheless *76% of missed hazards left uncorrected*. The authors' own sensitivity analysis is honest about the measuring instrument: a refined parser raises Qwen's baseline sensitivity from 0.451 to 0.729 and shrinks the gap to 25.3 points, still leaving 39 of 144 hazards missed.

## Limits and open problems they name
Two models, one task, 400 cases, with the real-world half carrying only 6% hazards; they say explicitly that generalisation across models, tasks and scales is untested. The keyword parser is coarse (the gap narrows substantially under a better one, which they report rather than bury). The SAE had to be trained from scratch on 400 cases because no pretrained one exists for Qwen, and they grant that a better-trained or cross-layer-transcoder version might work. Only inference-time methods were tried — training-time interventions are listed as future work. One control is missing outright: the random-direction comparison for the TSV at α = 10.0 was never run, for a stated 4-hour GPU budget reason, so the paper's headline positive result lacks a matched control at the strength where it appears; they note the random direction loses at α = 5.0 (net −6) but cannot complete the paired test at α = 10.0. They also flag that a probe used as an *external monitor* rather than a steering handle is an untested alternative that their own results make look promising.

## What we take from it
This is the direct empirical challenge to the probe-and-steer arc, and the most useful thing in it for us is not its conclusion but its **instrumentation**. Four things to take. First, **a two-number reporting rule for every intervention we run**: correction rate and disruption rate, plus the random-perturbation control at matched magnitude at the same site. Single-number "steering works" claims are exactly what the concept arm would have produced if the disruption column had been omitted — it corrected 20% of errors and looked like a modest win until the cost was printed. Second, **net effect in points, not counts of successes**, because the disruption cost is what separates a causal handle from noise, and in three of four arms the cost met or exceeded the gain. Third, a warning about a specific kind of read-out we rely on: a probe with near-perfect AUROC is evidence of *information*, not of *causal sufficiency*, and this paper contains the cleanest demonstration of the separation — the probe is at 0.98, and the direction built from its own class means achieves a 24% correction ceiling. The corollary for us is that probe AUC should never be our headline metric for a predictive model; the headline has to be net correction under intervention against a random control. Fourth, an architectural diagnosis worth carrying as a hypothesis: single-site, single-layer intervention failing here is attributed to residual-stream compensation and distributed routing, and the authors' proposed fixes are multi-layer intervention and training-time shaping — which is the same "readable-in-patches / native-not-assigned" direction that `arxiv-2609.13529` argues from architecture and `arxiv-2410.16443` argues from layer statistics. Also worth the reader's time: the parser sensitivity table is a model of how to report a measurement artefact that shrinks your headline without destroying it.
