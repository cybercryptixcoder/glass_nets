# Can Mechanistic Interpretability Help Explain LLM Agent Failures? A Case Study on Web Element Hallucination

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.18514
- **Authors / organisation:** Mohamed Aghzal, Mor Geva, Gregory J. Stein, Ziyu Yao; George Mason University (Geva also Tel Aviv University)
- **Date:** 2026-11-13
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18514
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
The field's successful mechanistic stories come from narrow tasks with fixed inputs, where the relevant positions can be hypothesised by hand. Agents break that situation: an episode is many steps long, the context grows at every step, and a failure may be jointly caused by the model's computation and by an environment that moved underneath it. The authors ask a deliberately negative question — do the standard instruments (circuit discovery, activation patching, linear probes, steering vectors, SAEs) still explain anything when aimed at a realistic agentic failure? They pick one concrete failure, a web agent proposing an action that is not on the page, and treat it as a case study in what the tooling can and cannot deliver. The larger goal is to say which assumptions of mechanistic interpretability actually fail in agentic settings, and to argue for benchmarks and methods built around those failures rather than around them being patched over.

## What they studied
Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (28 layers) acting as a web navigation agent on Online-Mind2Web, a benchmark of episodes on live websites. Each step the model gets a system prompt plus the task, a DOM summary and an explicit list of available actions, and must pick one. The dataset is 300 episodes and 2,071 actions, of which 32.6% are hallucinated — defined operationally as an action whose selector is not in the supplied list. The unit of analysis runs across all of them: individual attention heads and FFNs, individual layers, input spans (selector start, task description, action list), three token positions (end of prompt, start of selector, start of selector value), whole residual-stream directions, and dictionary features from a frozen pretrained SAE.

## How they did it
Five instruments in sequence, each with an intervention and a control. First, per-component attribution: amplify each FFN's output ten-fold in turn, inject Gaussian noise into each attention head's output projection in turn, and measure the change in probability of a valid token at the start of the selector value, averaged over grounded actions; alongside, a purely correlational panel of per-head maximum attention difference between hallucinated and valid actions. Second, joint interventions in the style of work that amplifies attention and dampens FFNs, over a large grid of head sets, FFN sets and two scale coefficients; fix rate (hallucinated steps made valid) and break rate (valid steps made hallucinated) are the two responses. Third, activation patching: corrupt one input span with Gaussian noise, restore clean hidden states at each layer–offset pair, and measure the recovery of the search-token probability. Fourth, linear probes on the residual stream at three positions across all 28 layers, with an episode-level 70/15/15 split (1,433 train, 282 development, 356 test). Fifth, steering built directly from a probe, using the probe weight divided by per-feature training-split standard deviation, negated so it points to the valid side and normalised, swept over five magnitudes. Two distribution-shift arms follow: the identical steering vector applied to a reformatted action list (numbered markdown instead of JSON), and probes retrained on that format. An SAE is applied at the layer with the best causal recovery, comparing the top features by class difference and decoding each one through the unembedding.

## What counts as an explanation here
The paper's own answer is deflationary, and the deflation is the contribution. A probe direction is accepted as *prediction* but not as *explanation*: it fires before generation, it is causally upstream in the sense that pushing along it changes the outcome, and yet no component or input property is identified as the thing that made the decision. The authors draw the line at utility — whether the insight can be used to change behaviour — versus mechanism, and show that the folder's usual instruments can cross the first line and fail the second on the same object. What they say a real explanation would need is a specification of which input property drives the commitment and how, plus a matched counterfactual that isolates it; neither is available here, and they argue for a benchmark that would demand both.

## How they judged success
Fix rate and break rate, reported together for every intervention with magnitudes swept; probe accuracy as held-out ROC-AUC with an episode-level split so steps from one episode cannot leak across folds; and, for the distribution-shift claim, the same two rates on a reformatted prompt with the identical vector, plus the across-layer probe curve after retraining. Controls are present in the component study (amplification versus noise, correlational versus causal panels) and the paper's honest weakness is that the strongest steering result has no random-direction arm at matched norm — its admissibility evidence is that the break rate explodes at larger magnitudes, which is a coherence failure being read as a boundary rather than as a null.

## What they found
Hallucination is reachable by prediction: a pre-generation probe at the end of the prompt reaches 0.80 test AUC at layer 18 before a single output token exists, and probes at the start of the selector are strong from layer 0. The model's intent, not the page, is what it commits to — logit lens on hallucinating trials converges on plausible UI words (search, enter, find, explore), with the Chinese word for search appearing at layer 24 before the English one. But the mechanistic story is nearly empty: the best joint intervention in a large grid fixes 8.9% of hallucinations at a 2.4% break rate, the top-10 heads fix 4.4–4.5%, and the highest-fix configurations in the grid at large scales achieve their numbers by breaking everything (100% break at the extreme). Individual component intervention produces a different failure from the natural one — a garbled selector such as a long numeric suffix rather than a coherent but nonexistent element — which the authors use as evidence that no single component carries the decision. Patching identifies the action list as the causal span (restoring the residual at layer 27 recovers 0.360 of probability mass, against 0.075 for the attention and 0.067 for the FFN components, and under 0.02 for the other two spans) but cannot say which property of the list matters, and the workable span varies by example: corrupting the action list changes the decision only when the list is the sole source, because in roughly half of valid steps the agent reuses a value it already emitted earlier in the trajectory. Counterfactual construction fails structurally — removing the chosen valid action yields a hallucination in only 38% of cases, and by the next step the two runs agree on the selector about a quarter of the time and land on pages with 0.52 word-level Jaccard. The steering direction is the strongest number in the paper and also the sharpest warning: 85.4% fix at 7.0% break from the selector-start layer-17 vector at the middle magnitude, but 0.0% fix and 96.9% break one step up the sweep, and the same vector on the reformatted action list drops to 58.1% fix at the same 7.0% break. The pretrained SAE's top class-differentiating features promote tokens unrelated to the behaviour.

## Limits and open problems they name
One model, one benchmark, one failure mode — they say so and argue the structure recurs in embodied, tool-calling and code agents. The probe direction is entangled with prompt syntax rather than with the construct. No component or input property is identified, so the strongest results are monitoring and control rather than explanation. They name the missing pieces explicitly: agentic interpretability benchmarks with a defined ground truth for a trajectory-level failure and its trigger; attribution methods that work over distributed computation across timesteps; counterfactual construction in stateful environments where no two trials are structurally comparable; feature discovery on interaction-derived distributions rather than text; methods that track a plan crystallising across layers and steps; and a universality question across agent types. A benchmark, they note, must also define what a correct mechanistic explanation *is* and how to score faithfulness, completeness and actionability — none of which currently exists for agents.

## What we take from it
Three things, in order of usefulness. **A measurement split to adopt wholesale:** pre-generation decodability, causal control and mechanism are three separate readings, and this paper is the cleanest demonstration in the folder that they can diverge maximally — 0.80 AUC and 85% fix with a mechanism at 8.9%. Any entry we write that claims a direction "explains" a behaviour should print all three columns and say which one the evidence supports. **A synthetic-failure warning worth carrying:** the authors' own tell that an intervention is producing the wrong *kind* of failure is qualitative — induced failures are syntactically broken while natural ones are well-formed but ungrounded — and that distinction is a cheap, checkable validity criterion for any intervention study that uses failure counts as its outcome. **A substitution-with-history test** for our own on-manifold discipline: the action-list corruption that does nothing in half the cases is the same structure as the folder's other null ablations, but with a specific and diagnosable cause — the information is present twice, once in the current input and once in the trajectory history — so when an ablation under-fires, the first thing to check is whether the same value is recoverable from an earlier position. The negative on pretrained SAEs is also worth remembering as a data-distribution fact rather than a method verdict: text-trained dictionaries were not shown to fail at decomposing, they were shown to have nothing to say about concepts that only exist during interaction.
