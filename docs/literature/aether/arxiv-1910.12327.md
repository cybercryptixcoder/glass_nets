# A simple measure of conditional dependence

- **Key:** arxiv-1910.12327
- **Authors / organisation:** Mona Azadkia; Sourav Chatterjee — Department of Statistics, Stanford University (address printed in the v6 HTML with per-author emails)
- **Date:** 2021-03-28 (v6; first submitted 2019-10-27)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.12327
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

The authors set out to fill a gap they describe as narrow on purpose: measuring how much dependence remains between two variables once a third set of variables is held fixed, without assuming anything about the distribution. The nonparametric conditional-independence literature is dominated by *tests* — kernel, binning, copula, density-estimation and resampling approaches — which return a yes/no verdict at a chosen level, and the paper's framing is that a test is a blunt object when what is wanted is a graded quantity with a stable limit and an interpretable scale. The move is to build an estimator whose population target is exactly zero under conditional independence and exactly one when one variable is a function of the other given the conditioning set, with no tuning parameters and no density estimation anywhere in the procedure. Their interest is methodological rather than mechanistic: the underlying goal is a coefficient that behaves like a partial R² statistic but works for arbitrary nonlinear, non-Gaussian relations, so that downstream procedures (variable selection, and by their own remark graphical models) can be built on a measure instead of on a verdict.

## What they studied

Nothing neural. The unit of analysis is a triple of random objects: a real-valued target Y, a conditioning vector X of dimension p (p may be zero), and another vector Z of dimension q. The object of interest is the joint law of (Y, X, Z) and the coefficient T defined from it; the estimator is a function of n i.i.d. copies. Data used are synthetic constructions designed so that the answer is known exactly (algebraic relations on the torus, polar-coordinate models), plus three public tabular benchmarks from the UCI repository — spam classification (4,601 observations, 57 features), Polish company bankruptcy (19,967 observations, 64 features) and the million-song dataset (515,345 observations, 90 features, with publication year as the response).

## How they did it

The coefficient is defined through cumulative probabilities rather than densities: for each threshold t, measure how much of the variance of the indicator that Y exceeds t is left *after* conditioning on both X and Z, relative to how much is there conditioning on X alone, and average that ratio over t under the law of Y. The estimator replaces the variances by a nearest-neighbour and rank computation. For each sample point, find its nearest neighbour in X-space and its nearest neighbour in (X, Z)-space, take the ranks of the Y values, and compare how far the Y-rank of the (X, Z)-neighbour sits from the point's own rank against how far the Y-rank of the X-neighbour does; the coefficient is a ratio of two such sums. Nearest neighbours and ranks are both obtainable in n log n time, so the whole statistic is cheap at fixed p and q. They then prove three layers of theory: that the population coefficient has the 0/1 characterisations above; that the estimator converges to it almost surely with no distributional assumptions beyond non-degeneracy; and, under a local-Lipschitz condition on the conditional law plus a tail condition, a convergence rate of roughly n to the power minus one over p+q, up to logarithms. The selection algorithm, FOCI, is forward stepwise greedy maximisation of the coefficient: pick the single predictor with the largest unconditional coefficient, then repeatedly add whichever remaining predictor raises the *conditional* coefficient given what has been chosen, and stop the first time the best available addition scores at or below zero. Consistency is proved in terms of a margin δ — the smallest amount a sufficient-to-insufficient step can gain — giving a bound of the form one minus a polynomial in p times an exponential in n. The gaussian linear-regression case is worked out to show δ behaves like the analogous partial-R² margin up to constants set by the noise and total variance ratios.

## What counts as an explanation here

A scalar coefficient, not a model. There is no dictionary, no graph and no surrogate: the account of a dependence is a number in [0, 1] with two named endpoints (conditional independence at zero, functional determination at one) and an interpretation as a nonlinear generalisation of partial R², obtained by decomposing Y into its threshold indicators and averaging their partial R². It is a *measurement*, deliberately partial — it says how much, not how. The second artifact is a procedure: the ordered list of predictors FOCI produces, which is the paper's answer to "which variables matter" and is presented as a sufficient set in the Markov-blanket sense rather than as a mechanism.

## How they judged success

Four criteria, each with its own instrument. (1) Theoretical: exact characterisations T = 0 ⇔ conditional independence and T = 1 ⇔ conditional determination, plus almost-sure consistency and a stated rate, all without distributional assumptions. (2) Recovery on constructions with known answers, reported as the spread of the estimate over repeated simulations at sample size 1,000 — the torus model where Y depends on X2 only through X1, and the polar model where Y is independent of an angle unconditionally and a function of it given one coordinate. (3) Variable selection on synthetic regressions with 1,000 candidate predictors and sample size 2,000, scored by whether the correct subset is recovered, against forward stepwise, lasso, the Dantzig selector and SCAD (tuning by 10-fold cross-validation, AIC for stopping), and against two model-free baselines, random forests and mutual-information selection. (4) Real data: subset size and mean squared prediction error on a held-out set, with a random forest fitted on whatever each method selected, compared against a random forest on the full predictor set.

## What they found

The coefficient behaves as promised on the constructions: on the torus example the unconditional value for the nuisance predictor sits in a small band straddling zero while the conditional value sits near 0.9, and the polar example shows the same pattern from independence to strong determination; the authors note that conditional distance correlation could not gauge the strength in either case. On synthetic selection with heavy interaction structure, FOCI recovered the correct three predictors in more than 90% of runs in one example and 99.5% in another, while the linear and additive-model methods essentially never did; random forests and mutual-information selection also succeeded but at much higher computational cost. On the three real datasets FOCI selected 14 of 57, 5 of 64 and 17 of 90 features with prediction errors within about 0.01–0.9 of the best baseline (a small relative gap), so the claim is that comparable accuracy is reached with far fewer variables. The gaussian analysis shows the margin δ tracks the partial-R² margin up to multiplicative constants determined by the noise-to-signal ratio.

## Limits and open problems they name

The paper is unusually explicit about what it cannot do. They state that a uniformly level-α consistent *test* for conditional independence is impossible without unverifiable assumptions, so the coefficient must not be read as supplying one — a striking admission given how naturally it invites that reading; it gives a consistent estimate of a strength, not a calibrated p-value, and the finite-sample value can fall outside [0, 1]. The rate is n to the minus one over p+q, so it degrades exponentially in the conditioning dimension, and they say they believe no statistic with the same properties can do better while admitting they do not know. The p = 0 case has only conjectures (root-n rate, a central limit theorem) that they say they cannot prove. One deficiency of FOCI is that it selects features but does not fit a model, hence the random-forest follow-up; the proof guarantees sufficiency of the selected set but *not* its smallness, and they say they do not know how to prove a size bound. The stopping rule, the idea of adding several variables per step, and a forward-backward variant are offered as unexplored. They note a different natural application, graphical models, is left as work in progress.

## What we take from it

A drop-in replacement for the independence test inside any probing or boundary search, and the cleanest statement in this folder of what a conditional-independence claim should even be made of. Three concrete carry-overs. **Method:** the coefficient is the instrument entry `arxiv-2206.05829` was missing — that paper's theory is stated for arbitrary graphoids but its finite-sample machinery is a thresholded generalised partial correlation, a second-order statistic that is blind to dependence leaving residuals uncorrelated; this statistic detects any dependence, needs no model, no kernel bandwidth and no density, runs in n log n, and its endpoints are exactly the two claims a probing report wants to make. Compute it per boundary candidate rather than thresholding a residual cosine. **Measure:** report the *value*, not just a verdict — "the residual dependence between these two given that set is 0.03 on a scale where 1 means functional determination" is a far more useful sentence than "independence not rejected", and it is directly comparable across conditioning sets, which lets an account show the boundary size at which the coefficient collapses instead of asserting one. **Warning:** the authors' own remark that nonparametric conditional-independence *testing* is essentially unsolvable without unverifiable assumptions is a warning against the habit of reporting CI structure with significance stars at all; the honest form of the claim is an estimated strength with a stated sample size and conditioning dimension, because the rate degrades exponentially in that dimension. A second, subtler warning comes from the FOCI stopping rule: the cut-off at a non-positive coefficient is a heuristic whose consistency proof assumes a fixed margin, so a boundary search that mirrors FOCI inherits a hidden assumption that the smallest available gain is not vanishingly small. Finally, the constructions are the templates for our own sanity checks — a variable can be marginally useless and conditionally decisive, and any instrument used for probe selection should be validated on exactly that shape before it is trusted on activations.
