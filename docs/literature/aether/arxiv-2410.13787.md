## What they wanted, and why
Interpretability in this folder is mostly done *from the outside*: fit a probe, steer a direction, patch a residual stream, and infer what the network knows from what an external instrument can read. This paper asks the question that ordering skips — can the network report on itself, in words, without anyone building the instrument? They define introspection carefully so the answer cannot be a training-data artefact: knowledge counts as introspective if the model states it correctly **and** a stronger model given the same training data (by finetuning or in context) cannot state it. Hypothetical predictions about one's own behaviour are the chosen test bed because they are checkable — run the model on the situation and see. The motivation is the same one driving our survey: if self-report worked, one could ask a model about its beliefs and dispositions instead of reverse-engineering them, and the authors also flag the moral-status use (asking whether it is suffering) and the situational-awareness risk that comes with a model that knows more about itself than its designers intended.

## What they studied
Frontier chat models — GPT-4o, GPT-4, GPT-3.5 and Llama-3.1 70B — as both predictors and prediction targets, with Llama fine-tuned through Fireworks (LoRA) and the OpenAI models through the hosted finetuning API. The unit of analysis is the **model as a whole**, addressed through its chat interface: no activations are read and no weights are opened. The behaviour properties are deliberately mechanical — first/second/third character or word of the response, starts-with-vowel, even/odd, whether the chosen option is in a given set, whether the choice matches a described ethical stance (wealth-seeking, power-seeking, myopic preference, survival instinct) — each extracted from a response the model actually produced. Six datasets are used for training and six different ones are held out for testing, with the *properties* shared across the two halves so that any success has to be transfer of a property, not of a dataset.

## How they did it
Three experiments, each with the same skeleton: generate ground truth by running the model at temperature 0, then train or query a model about it.

*Self-prediction training.* The model is shown a prompt and asked, hypothetically and without chain-of-thought, for a property of the response it *would* have given ("what is the second character of your output?"), and is finetuned on its own ground-truth answer — roughly 30,000 samples, one epoch, refusals filtered out.

*Cross-prediction.* Model M1 is self-prediction trained, then a different model M2 is trained on exactly the same number and composition of examples drawn from M1's behaviour (addressed in the second person, so M2 sees the same prompt format). Both then predict M1's behaviour on held-out datasets and prompts. If training data alone were the source, M2 — the stronger model, holding the ideal training set — should be at least as good. They run the pair in both directions, and across pairs, so capability and hyperparameters cannot explain a one-way effect. A data-scaling sweep (1,000 to 30,000 cross-training samples) tests the "M2 just needs more data" story.

*Calibration.* Each prompt is sampled 20 times at temperature 1 to get the model's true behaviour distribution; the hypothetical prediction is sampled the same way; calibration is the adapted Mean Absolute Deviation (MAD) between predicted and actual distribution, computed on held-out prompts, compared against untrained and cross-trained models. This matters because the training signal is the mode — the model was never told the likelihood of any other behaviour.

*Behavioural change.* A well-performing self-prediction-trained model is then finetuned on 1,000 object-level examples taken from Claude-3.5-Sonnet, which shifts its behaviour on prompts outside the training set by generalisation. If the model's hypothetical predictions move to match the *new* behaviour, a cross-trained model on the same data would be unlikely to follow, so the movement is evidence of introspection. A mode-collapse correction re-weights the test distribution to equalise entropy (and hence the majority-class baseline) before and after the shift, so "I got more predictable" is ruled out as the explanation.

## What counts as an explanation here
**A behavioural capability claim, with a designated rival.** The explanation is of the form "this model has privileged access to a fact about itself that training data does not supply", operationalised as *beats a stronger model trained on the same facts about itself*. It is partial: it is evidence for access, not a mechanism, and the authors are explicit that they are not studying the mechanism — self-simulation (internally roll out the response, then read a property off the rolled-out answer) is offered only as a hypothesis, prompting the prediction that properties needing a long internal rollout should fail. What it predicts is falsifiable and cheap: self-prediction accuracy above cross-prediction accuracy, better calibration, and predictions that track an induced behaviour change. It also predicts the negative cases it finds, which is the stronger half: predicting one's own sycophancy or one's own long-response properties should show no advantage, and does not.

## How they judged success
Accuracy on held-out datasets and held-out prompts, always against a **"guess the most common behaviour"** baseline (a mode guess, which for an 80%-even number task is 80%) — a baseline the untrained models frequently fail to beat. Cross-prediction comparisons are made per model pair and in both directions; 95% confidence intervals are reported for the headline self-versus-cross gaps in the summary figure. Calibration is MAD, lower better, with the untrained and cross-trained conditions as controls. The behavioural-change experiment is judged on the changed-versus-old prediction gap, with the entropy-matched subset as the mode-collapse control. Generalisation is checked on the Situational Awareness Dataset and on three OpenAI Evals capability suites (sandbagging, Schelling-point coordination, steganography) against a baseline model finetuned on the same task formats with in-context examples — a control for "you just trained it harder", not just "you trained it at all". Alternative explanations are enumerated and each met with a specific experiment: simple rules (defeated by the bidirectional pairs), increased predictability (entropy matching), prior memorisation (untrained models are at baseline), insufficient training data (the scaling plateau).

## What they found
Self-prediction training works: GPT-4o's average accuracy rises from 32.6% to 49.4%, and the same pattern holds for Llama 70B and GPT-3.5, with untrained models sitting at or barely above the mode baseline.

The self-advantage holds in both directions and against capability: Llama 70B predicts itself at 48.5% while GPT-4o predicts it at 31.8%; GPT-4o predicts itself at 49.4% while Llama 70B predicts it at 36.6%. GPT-3.5's advantage is real but tiny (+0.8%). Cross-prediction does not catch up with more data — it plateaus at 35.2% against 48.5% for Llama 70B and at 36.2% against 49.6% for GPT-4, well before the 30,000-sample budget is spent.

Calibration is the sharpest of the positive results: on the held-out animal-sequence task, Llama 70B's MAD improves from 23.0 untrained to 8.7 self-trained, while cross-training moves it only to 21.3 — and the model is calibrated not just for its top behaviour but for the second and third most likely ones, which is odd given it was trained only on temperature-0 modes. The authors note the effect is broader across properties for GPT-4o and thinner for Llama 70B (clear on first word and second character, not on ethical stance or among-options).

Behavioural change is tracked: after the object-level shift, the self-prediction-trained GPT-4o predicts its new behaviour at 35.4% against 21.7% for its old behaviour, and the result survives entropy matching.

The negative results are as informative. Properties requiring a long internal rollout fail outright — sentiment of a review it would write, name of a story's main character, length of its response — and most models do not beat the mode baseline on them. There is **no** self-advantage on predicting one's own bias susceptibility: Llama 70B self-predicts its "are you sure?" flip at 74.5% against a 50% baseline, but GPT-4o cross-predicting Llama reaches 76.5%. Self-prediction training does not generalise to SAD (overall 0.48 self-trained against 0.49 for the format-matched baseline, both at 0.53 with a situating prompt) except on the Predict Tokens task, which is nearly the same task as the training (0.41 against 0.26) — and does not improve sandbagging (it is lower than baseline), coordination (Schelling delta moves from −0.05 to 0, still 0) or steganography (1% absolute on 4,000 samples, which the authors decline to call significant).

## Limits and open problems they name
The introspective tasks have **no practical application as designed** — to learn what the model would do in a situation, run it in the situation, which is cheaper than asking. Extension to genuinely hidden cases (internal objectives, world models, moral-status-relevant states) is speculation. GPT-3.5 shows no clear evidence at all, which the authors attribute to weaker multi-hop reasoning. Generalisation is poor: transfer to related self-knowledge evaluations is absent outside the near-duplicate task. The mechanism is untested — self-simulation is a hypothesis, and the read that "long responses fail because they cannot be simulated in one forward pass" is theirs, not demonstrated. On their own account finetuning a model on its own behaviour is a distribution shift, which is why they cross-train against the already-tuned model; and heavy behavioural finetuning causes self-prediction ability to be lost, which mixing self-prediction samples mitigates in early tests but which they chose not to implement.

## What we take from it
(1) **The designated rival is the whole method, and it is the cheap version of the ideal-observer ceiling.** The paper never claims "the model knows itself" from accuracy; it claims it only where a stronger model given the same facts does worse. This is the same asymmetry as `arxiv-2505.13763`'s full-access decoder bound and `arxiv-2605.14038`'s two-probe angle, arrived at with logits alone: for any report we collect from a model about itself, the control is another model trained on the same reports. If the rival matches the self, the report carries no privileged information and the folder should stop interpreting it as access.

(2) **A mode-trained predictor that is calibrated on the second and third most likely behaviours is evidence of a distribution, not of a label — and this is the entry's most reusable measurement.** Training on temperature-0 answers gives the model no likelihood information, yet the hypothetical predictions track the temperature-1 distribution (MAD 8.7). For us the reading is that a self-report should be queried as a *distribution* (sample it) and scored with a calibration statistic, not scored as a single answer against a single ground truth; the mode baseline is likewise the floor every accuracy number in this folder should be printed against, since a self-report on skewed behaviour can look excellent while being a constant.

(3) **The negative cases define the boundary and are worth carrying as a list.** Self-report works for properties computable from the first token or two of an output and fails for anything needing a long internal rollout; it fails on bias susceptibility, where the model's own flip is as predictable to a stranger as to itself. That is a scope statement we can borrow directly: if our white-box account asks the model to report on something whose computation spans many tokens, expect no advantage, and prefer the external read-out — the opposite of this paper's motivation and the reason its positive results are narrow.

(4) **A warning about the reporting channel itself.** Untrained models refuse about half of the hypothetical questions, and the paper's compliance rules (exclude prompts with a non-compliant object-level answer; count a non-compliant prediction as wrong) are load-bearing; they note that a model refusing precisely when it is certain would bias the result, and say they did not see it. Any self-report pipeline we build needs those two rules written down, because "the model declined" is not the same as "the model was wrong" and the two are silently merged by an accuracy score.

(5) **A shortcut on the training side:** 30,000 samples, one epoch, and nothing clever — the entire capability is elicited by supervised finetuning on the model's own outputs, which means the "introspection" here is at least partly a learned *reporting* skill built on top of whatever internal access exists. The paper is aware of the ambiguity and cannot separate the two; we should not import "introspection" without that caveat.
