# Improving Dictionary Learning with Gated Sparse Autoencoders

- **Key:** arxiv-2404.16014
- **Authors / organisation:** Senthooran Rajamanoharan, Arthur Conmy (joint first authors), Lewis Smith, Tom Lieberum, Vikrant Varma, János Kramár, Rohin Shah, Neel Nanda; Google DeepMind (Google DeepMind's mechanistic interpretability team, per the acknowledgements' "GDM Mech Interp Team"); all eight authors listed on the abs page without affiliations on it

## What they wanted, and why

Sparse autoencoders were, by early 2024, the standard unsupervised tool for turning a model's activations into an overcomplete set of sparse directions that someone can then name. The authors take one specific defect of that recipe seriously: the L1 sparsity penalty is a pressure on *magnitudes*, not on the number of active features, so a dictionary trained under it systematically under-reports how strongly a feature is present. They want a training change that removes the defect without paying for it in sparsity, reconstruction fidelity or interpretability — because the point of such a dictionary is that later work (circuit tracing, steering, description of components) reads magnitudes as quantities. The larger goal is to make the dictionary a better basis for explaining computation across a whole training distribution rather than a handful of hand-found circuits.

## What they studied

The unit of analysis is the **dictionary latent** — the learned overcomplete feature directions of an SAE, plus the encoder that selects them. Models: a one-layer GELU language model (the model the original dictionary-learning work used), Pythia-2.8B, and Gemma-7B. Sites: MLP neuron activations in the one-layer model; MLP outputs, attention outputs (taken before the output projection), and residual stream at five layers in Pythia and four in Gemma. Dictionaries of width 2^17 for most baseline SAEs and 3×2^16 for Gated SAEs, with narrower widths (2^15 / 3×2^14) at the Pythia residual-stream sites after early runs suffered feature death.

## How they did it

They first define the *baseline* SAE exactly as the prior literature does: a ReLU encoder on the centred activation, a linear decoder, unit-norm decoder columns, and a loss of squared reconstruction error plus λ times the L1 norm of the activations, with λ the parameter their whole sweep varies. The modification is architectural: replace the single ReLU encoder with two paths — a **gate** path whose Heaviside step on its pre-activations decides which features are present, and a **magnitude** path whose ReLU output supplies the coefficients — and multiply them. The two paths share directions and differ only by a per-feature learned rescaling (exponentiated), so the parameter cost is 2M rather than a doubling of the encoder, and the forward pass costs the same as a baseline SAE. Training needs care because a step function carries no gradient: the L1 penalty is applied to the *rectified pre-activations* of the gate rather than to its binarised output, and an auxiliary reconstruction term — the gate pre-activations pushed through a **frozen copy** of the decoder, with gradients stopped back into the decoder — gives the gate path a reason to select features that actually reconstruct. Because a training step now runs the decoder twice, they compare against baseline SAEs given 50% more learned features so that training compute is matched. They then sweep λ for both architectures at each site and compare Pareto frontiers of sparsity against loss recovered (the share of the model's own cross-entropy loss recovered when the SAE reconstruction is spliced into the forward pass, with zero-ablation at 0% and identity at 100%). Three follow-ups: a shrinkage measure defined as the optimal rescaling factor γ of the reconstructions (γ = 1 unbiased, γ < 1 shrunk), computed analytically from three running means and tracked during training; a blinded human-rating study in which raters saw randomly ordered features with the architecture, site and layer hidden and typed a description and a Yes/Maybe/No verdict; and an ablation study that switches off one piece of the method at a time, plus a rival that fixes shrinkage without the gated architecture (freeze a baseline SAE's encoder and decoder directions and learn only extra rescale-and-shift parameters on the pre-activations). An appendix replaces the encoder at inference time with a greedy sparse-approximation algorithm (gradient pursuit) and runs the frozen decoder as a dictionary, so the quality of *learned dictionaries* can be compared independently of the encoders that produced them.

## What counts as an explanation here

A dictionary: a set of overcomplete directions with per-input coefficients, claimed to be a sparse linear decomposition of the activation, with interpretability of individual directions established by human description rather than by construction. Nothing here predicts network behaviour; the explanatory object is the decomposition, and the paper's explicit stance is that linearity and sparsity of the underlying computation are assumptions the method bakes in rather than findings it establishes. Its reconstruction residual is treated as a nuisance to be minimised, not as an unexplained remainder to be accounted for.

## How they judged success

Primary metrics: **L0** (mean number of active features) and **loss recovered**, compared as Pareto frontiers across a λ sweep computed from matched training compute (baseline given 50% more features). Shrinkage: relative reconstruction bias γ, estimated analytically and reported per run in Tables 1-8. Interpretability: blinded human ratings from 5 raters on Pythia (150 features) and 7 on Gemma (192 features), pairs matched on model, layer, site and rater, one-sided paired Wilcoxon-Pratt signed-rank test on a No/Maybe/Yes scale coded 0/1/2, with 90% BCa bootstrap confidence intervals. Controls: the per-piece ablation study, the ablation study's own variations (unfreeze decoder, drop the rescaling parameter, untie the encoders), and the frozen-baseline-plus-rescale rival, which isolates "fixed shrinkage" from "learned better directions". Appendix analyses use Friedman and Page trend tests across sites, layers and raters. Evaluation on over a million held-out tokens.

## What they found

Across all three models and every site tested, Gated SAEs are a Pareto improvement: at a given reconstruction fidelity they use fewer active features, and at a given L0 they recover more of the model's loss. In the abstract's summary they need about half as many firing features for comparable fidelity, and in many cases the same loss recovered at half the L0 — the per-run tables make that concrete: a Gemma residual-stream Gated SAE reaches 99.35% CE recovered at L0 14.3 where baseline runs at comparable layers sit near 90% at similar L0. Gated runs have γ close to 1 at every site, while baseline γ falls as low as roughly 0.72-0.80 on attention outputs and around 0.86-0.90 on MLP outputs, worsening as λ rises. Interpretability is a null result for superiority: the one-sided test of "Gated at most as interpretable as baseline" gives p = .060 with a paired mean difference of .13 and a 90% interval [0, .26] — the interval touches zero and the authors explicitly say they cannot conclude Gated features are more interpretable, only that they are comparable. The site breakdown is the interesting detail: the entire effect concentrates on MLP outputs (mean .40, p = .003, CI [.18, .63]), with attention outputs (p = .47) and the final residual stream (p = .59, mean −0.07) showing nothing. The ablation study says every piece earns its place: unfreezing the decoder in the auxiliary loss hurts, dropping the magnitude rescaling parameter hurts mildly, and *untying* the two encoders — giving the gate and magnitude paths independent directions — does not help at all, in fact slightly hurts, which supports tying on both parameter and performance grounds. The rival experiment is the important negative: fixed shrinkage with the baseline's own frozen directions recovers only a fraction of the gap, so most of the gain comes from learning better encoder and decoder directions, not from undoing the underestimate. The inference-time-optimisation appendix tempers the dictionary claim — a more powerful sparse approximation over a baseline decoder often closes much of the gap, and the best baseline dictionary by that measure (trained at L0 ≈ 99) is comparable to the best Gated one (L0 ≈ 20), so part of the advantage is a better encoder rather than a better basis. Two anomalies are reported rather than smoothed: loss recovered above 100% at a Gemma attention site, which they trace to the evaluation data differing from Gemma's pretraining data (mean-ablating that site lowered loss), and some non-monotone Pareto frontiers, which they attribute to training difficulties.

## Limits and open problems they name

The sparsity and linearity assumptions of the whole dictionary line are inherited, not tested. The extra expressivity raises an overfitting worry the authors acknowledge and say they see no evidence for (held-out evaluation plus manual checking), while conceding the evaluations are not comprehensive — in particular they never test whether the dictionaries contain causally meaningful intermediate variables in the model's computation. The step discontinuity complicates gradient-path attribution methods applied to the dictionary. They name the possibility that inexpensive inference-time pruning of the many near-zero-activating baseline features could close part of the gap, since baseline SAEs have no thresholding mechanism. Beyond 7B, chat, multimodal and mixture-of-experts models are untested; and whether the architectures differ in inductive bias beyond the mechanism described is left open.

## What we take from it

Three things, and one of them is a warning about our own habit of quoting single numbers.

1. **A measure, cheap and general:** relative reconstruction bias — the optimal scalar rescaling of a read-out's output — is one closed-form expression over three running means, computable during training, and it prices the direction of bias (under- or over-estimation) rather than just the size of error. Any substitute model or read-out we build that stands in for the network's computation should report this alongside squared error, because squared error alone cannot tell a shrunk predictor from a noisy one, and the two need different fixes.
2. **A structural framing:** separating *which* units are on from *how much* each contributes, and localising a regulariser to the selection path only, is a template for building our own constrained accounts — if a penalty is meant to buy sparsity, price it on the selection, not on the coefficients.
3. **A pattern the folder should carry as a table row:** the "attempting to fix the symptom" rival. Here the symptom is a known bias, and the rival fixes exactly that symptom while holding everything else frozen; the result shows the symptom accounted for only a small part of the gain. For us this is the control to run whenever we claim a component is important because removing it hurts — the honest third arm is a variant that removes the *mechanism* but repairs the *symptom* it was supposed to cause, and the gap between that arm and the treatment is the part of the effect the stated mechanism does not explain.
4. **A caution about means over sites:** the interpretability result is null overall and strongly positive on MLP outputs; averaged over sites it reads as a near-null. This is the third entry in the folder where an aggregate hides a partition that carries opposite conclusions (see the per-label POS breakdown in `arxiv-2006.00995`).
