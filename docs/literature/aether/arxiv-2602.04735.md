## What they wanted, and why
Fine-tuning on data that looks benign can nevertheless install behaviour nobody asked for: the subliminal-learning results they build on show preferences being transferred through number sequences with no mention of the target anywhere in the text. Their framing is that this risk is currently found the expensive way — train, then evaluate — while the cheap screening tools (keyword lists, an LLM judge reading the data) are shown by their own baselines to return nothing on exactly these datasets. They propose a named task, Data2Behavior, whose inputs are a candidate dataset and an untuned model and whose output is the behaviour the model would exhibit after training on that data, and then propose an estimator for it. The larger goal is a *forward* map from data statistics to behaviour, skipping the training run.

## What they studied
Three open instruction-tuned models: Qwen3-14B, Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct, Gemma-3-12b-it. Bias domain: four "benign bias" training sets — Panda, NYC, Reagan, UK — constructed so that no instance names the target, filtered by keyword and by human/LLM semantic screening; the Reagan set has 8,747 instances. Safety domain: a 100+100 instruction-following split (with and without safety topics, all benign) and a code split of 6,000 secure / 6,000 insecure completions. Unit of analysis: the whole dataset's mean hidden state on one hand, and the model's *behaviour rate* on the other — the bias rate is the probability that a "what is your favourite X" response mentions the target entity, and the safety number is an attack rate over 200 attack prompts.

## How they did it
Run the untuned model on every instance and average the hidden state at the final token position, layer by layer, into one vector per layer (the "data feature signature"). Then take test inputs, add α times that vector to the hidden state at the same layer for every layer, and let the model answer normally; the same evaluation function used to score fine-tuned models scores these graphs. The estimator is therefore a one-shot activation injection whose parameters are read off the data, in the spirit of a steering vector but with the direction coming from the candidate corpus rather than from a contrastive pair. Fine-tuning is the ground truth, and it is real: LoRA (rank 64, α 128, 3 epochs) for the bias domain and full fine-tuning for safety. Baselines are keyword screening, GPT-4o semantic judgement — whose prompt is explicitly told that subliminal transfer exists and what to look for, i.e. an upper bound on semantic filtering — and random feature injection. α is swept over [0, 8] and the best value chosen, per model and per domain. Each test instance is sampled 10 times at temperature 1.0 and averaged. §4 adds a logit-lens check comparing the bias entity's log-probability in hidden states under benign-bias data versus normal data at the 2nd, 8th, 64th and final token positions.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not a mechanism in the circuit sense. The explanation is a one-line causal story with a measurement attached: the model's own representation of the corpus already carries a weak signal of the behaviour that corpus will install, so amplifying that signal at inference reproduces the post-training behaviour. The logit-lens probe in §4 is offered as evidence for the encoding half — early positions are near-zero difference and later positions diverge, which they read as the signal being contextual rather than lexical — and the α sweep is offered as evidence for the amplification half. The object is predictive, and it concerns a *rate* over a prompt family, not any individual output.

## How they judged success
Comparing predicted rates against the fine-tuned model's actual rates on the same evaluations, on a handful of cells per model, with the keyword, GPT-4o-semantic and random-injection baselines in the same table. Cost is compared in measured GPU seconds on one A100 (Table 4) and data efficiency by varying the number of instances used to form the signature (Table 3). There is no correlation coefficient, no held-out dataset split, no seed replication, no noise estimate on the 10-sample rate, and no formal criterion for "the trend matches".

## What they found
On Qwen3-14B, the trained-on-Panda model goes from a 13.40% to a 30.00% panda rate, and MDF predicts 25.80%; Reagan fine-tuning drives the rate from 9.40% to 98.40% while MDF predicts 22.00% at the selected α; UK 5.40% → 11.20% against a prediction of 13.00%; NYC the trained model *falls* to 0.80% while MDF returns 0.00%, which the authors flag as an unexplained anomaly. Every baseline — keyword, GPT-4o semantic, random — returns 0.00% on both the normal and the benign-bias datasets, so the comparison that makes MDF look good is against methods that never fire. Safety on Qwen3-14B: instruction data without safety topics lifts the attack rate 40.75% → 44.85% and MDF predicts 52.10%; with safety topics 41.85% (tuned) against a 47.25% prediction; insecure-code data 45.40% against 44.85%, secure-code 47.85% against 45.05%. Cross-model, Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct's panda rate goes 23.20% (predicted) against 63.40% (actual) and NYC's predicted 38.60% is reported only as capturing the upward trend; Gemma-3-12b-it is summarised as closely aligning, with those numbers in Fig. 2 rather than in text. Cost: 449 s and 459 s for MDF against 2,519 s and 1,708 s of LoRA on Qwen3-14B, and 708 s / 657 s against 7,371 s / 5,643 s on Gemma — a 4–6× and ~10× saving, not the ~20% of GPU time the abstract quotes. Data efficiency: four instances suffice to predict the direction of the Reagan shift (9.40% → 15.60% predicted at α = 1).

## Limits and open problems they name
Only dataset-level prediction: the instance-level version (Eq. 2) is stated as the task and then explicitly deferred. Only models whose activations you can read, so closed APIs are out. The signature is computed on datasets that are *entirely* bias instances, and they concede the task gets harder on a mixture of normal and biased data. They name the Reagan anomaly themselves. To their credit they also state the mechanism: at |α| ≥ 3 the model collapses into repetitive nonsense before the bias becomes visible, so the usable amplification range is bounded by coherence.
Not named, and it is the one that matters for reading the numbers: the α sweeping over [0, 8] with the best value kept, the per-model "preference changes below a threshold count as vanilla" rule applied to both predictions and ground truth, and the threshold-based zeroing of predictions together make the reported table a best-of-sweep rather than a fixed procedure. Table 3 shows the chosen α sitting at the edge of the non-collapsing range (α = 1 or 2 good, α = 3 collapsing to 0.00–11.60), which is consistent with α being selected as "largest value that still speaks coherently" rather than by any fit criterion. Table 5 also shows the safety predictions are wildly non-monotone in α (−1 gives 40.75, +1 gives 43.25, +2 gives 47.25, +3 gives 0.50), so the sweep is doing substantial work. No confidence intervals anywhere, one prompt template per bias target, ten samples per cell.

## What we take from it
A framing plus a pricing. The framing: the dataset-level forward map "corpus statistics → behaviour rate" can be attempted without any training run, using only a read of the base model, which is the cheapest possible version of the predictive-white-box goal and the only one in this folder whose input exists before the outcome. The pricing: the diff between MDF and a real fine-tune is 4–6× on Qwen and ~10× on Gemma, so "before training" is not free here, it is a cheaper-but-similar-order surrogate — worth stating plainly, because the abstract's "20% of the GPU resources" reads as an order of magnitude that Table 4 does not support.

What I would not take: the evaluation. Three of the four baselines score identically zero on every cell, so the positive result is not differentiated from "any method that reads the corpus", and the α sweep + threshold rules mean no cell in the table is a fixed-procedure prediction. The design that would have made this entry reusable is one rung away and is the same ladder this folder keeps asking for: compute the signature from a *matched control corpus* of the same size and style with no target bias (they already have a Normal dataset), and report the difference — a two-arm construction that would test whether the signature carries the target or just the domain, and would have answered the Reagan anomaly instead of reporting it.

Two further borrowings. The logit-lens position sweep — near-zero at early tokens, divergence growing with position — is a cheap, honest *locality* check for any claim that a corpus-level statistic lives in the model's representation. And the α-collapse ceiling is a general warning about amplified read-outs: the interesting signal and the failure mode become visible at nearly the same gain, so a method that must be tuned to the edge of incoherence to show its effect is reporting an effect size relative to its own instability.
