# How Much Do Circuits Tell Us? Measuring the Consistency and Specificity of Language Model Circuits

- **Key:** arxiv-2605.08348
- **Authors / organisation:** Michael Li (Northeastern University; work done while at Carnegie Mellon University), Nishant Subramani (Carnegie Mellon University, Language Technologies Institute)
- **Date:** 2026-05-08
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.08348
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

Circuit work usually validates itself with necessity and sufficiency: ablate the circuit and the behaviour should break, keep only the circuit and the behaviour should survive. The authors argue that passing those tests leaves two properties unmeasured. A circuit should be *consistent* — roughly the same units should come back when the input changes, otherwise the aggregated task circuit is noise. And a circuit should be *specific* — it should differ from the circuit of an unrelated task, otherwise "the circuit for this task" is really a description of the model's general machinery. Their question is whether circuits deliver these two properties, which the field rarely checks, and whether the answer depends on the grain at which the circuit is drawn. The larger goal is a reality check on how far circuit methods can support targeted understanding and targeted intervention.

## What they studied

Six tasks — Addition, Boolean Logic, IOI, CopyColors MCQA (multiple choice), ARC Easy and ARC Challenge — on five models from three families: Gemma-2-2B and its instruction-tuned twin, Llama-3.2-3B and its instruction-tuned twin, Qwen3-4B — plus OLMo-2-1B, used only for a pretraining analysis. The unit of analysis is deliberately varied. At the *component* grain nodes are attention heads and whole MLP blocks, between 234 components (Gemma-2-2B) and 1,188 (Qwen3-4B). At the *neuron* grain nodes are individual pre-down-projection MLP activations: 229,376 to 350,208 per model, roughly 300 to 1,000 times more units. A third grain, sparse-autoencoder latents (features), is added for Gemma-2-2B alone.

## How they did it

For each task they take 1,000 clean prompts with a matched corrupted counterpart, score every node with one of two scalable attribution methods (EAP-IG with five integration steps, and relevance patching), keep the top K percent of nodes per prompt (K swept over 1, 5, 10, 20, 30), and aggregate over prompts with a consensus threshold P: a node enters the task circuit if it appears in at least P percent of per-example circuits. P is swept from 50 to 100 percent. Consistency is then the fraction of a per-example circuit covered by that task circuit, averaged over 1,000 examples. Necessity compares a zero-ablation of the task circuit against a *capacity-conserved* control drawn at random from the remaining nodes and matched in both size and head-versus-MLP composition, normalised by unablated accuracy. Specificity compares the accuracy drop on task A from ablating A's own circuit with the mean drop from ablating each other task's circuit, reported as a difference in accuracy points; they also measure Jaccard overlap between task circuits and, at P = 100 percent, split each pair of circuits into shared core, task-only set and partner-only set and ablate each part on its own. Ablations are scored on a held-out set of 200 examples. Mean ablation is run as a replication of every main experiment.

## What counts as an explanation here

An attribution graph — a sparse set of nodes (heads and MLP blocks, or MLP neurons, or SAE features) causally responsible for one behaviour, elicited per input and merged by a recurrence threshold. It is explicitly partial: a task circuit is a statement about which units recur across examples, not about an algorithm, and the paper's thrust is that the same model yields very different circuits depending on the grain and the attribution method chosen to draw it. Nothing here is predictive in the sense of forecasting behaviour under a new intervention; the graphs are used to make claims about localisation.

## How they judged success

Five measures, all of them interventions on the held-out set except overlap: Reuse@P (consistency), necessity relative to a capacity-conserved random control, the specificity gap in accuracy points (own-circuit drop minus mean other-circuit drop), Jaccard overlap between task circuits, and per-evaluation-set accuracy under zero and mean ablation. Robustness is established by sweeping K and P, repeating under mean ablation (Pearson correlations between ablations across the P sweep), and checking both attribution methods. No confidence intervals are the organising statistic in the main text — the necessity figure carries 95 percent intervals, but the specificity gaps and reuse medians are reported as points.

## What they found

Component circuits are consistent: at K = 10 percent and P = 50 percent the median reuse of the per-example circuit is 88.6 percent under EAP-IG and 78.4 percent under RelP, falling to 60.8 percent (EAP-IG) at P = 85 percent and 38.5 percent at P = 100 percent, and rising to a median 98.0 percent at K = 30 percent. But they are not specific. The specificity gap is 0.7 accuracy points under EAP-IG and 5.4 under RelP, and the shared core, when ablated, causes the largest drop on every task but Addition; the task-only set damages the other tasks about as much as its own or more, and Addition is the sole exception to that rule. Neuron circuits invert the trade: reuse collapses to a median 3.9 percent (EAP-IG) / 4.5 percent (RelP) at K = 10, P = 50, is below 2 percent by P = 75 and effectively zero by P = 85, yet the specificity gap is 29.4 and 26.8 accuracy points respectively. The difference is overlap: component circuits overlap heavily across tasks even when unrelated (EAP-IG Jaccard 0.36 to 0.80 for K of at least 5 percent), with Gemma-2 models sharing 0.62 to 1.00 between any two tasks and two tasks (Boolean Logic and IOI) producing identical circuits; neuron circuits overlap at most 0.19, except the two ARC splits at 0.35 to 0.56, the same-benchmark pair. Both grains still pass necessity at P = 50 (means 0.56 for EAP-IG components, 0.50 for EAP-IG neurons, 0.11 and 0.50 within RelP), with Boolean Logic low at both grains and IOI at the neuron grain; the authors note reuse alone does not imply usefulness, and necessity alone does not imply consistency or specificity. Adding features does not break the trade: on Gemma-2-2B, SAE latents (Gemma Scope JumpReLU, width 16k per layer, 425,984 latents) have a mean reuse of 17.3 percent against 9.4 for neurons and 91 to 100 for components, a specificity gap of 28.3 against 29.7 percent of baseline, and comparable necessity, in circuits roughly ten times smaller (226 latents against 2,242 neurons at K = 10, P = 50); widening the dictionary to 65k (1,703,936 latents) does not raise reuse. The attribution method is not neutral either: EAP-IG and RelP component scores correlate at a mean Spearman of 0.36 and their task circuits overlap at Jaccard 0.23 (0.16 at the neuron level), which is *less* agreement than between EAP-IG circuits of two different tasks (0.56). MLP blocks are heavily over-represented in circuits (about 4 percent of nodes but up to 41 percent of a circuit in Llama-3.2-3B; 93.5 and 98.5 percent MLP blocks in the two Gemma models under EAP-IG). A case study of the 17 components shared by all six Llama-3.2-3B circuits finds 14 MLP blocks and 3 heads, of which L4H10 is an attention sink (0.762 of its final-position attention on the beginning-of-text token), while L15H18 puts 0.737 on the prompt body and concentrates 34 percent of it on the digits of Addition, 47 percent of literals in Boolean Logic, 47 percent of names in IOI. Across OLMo-2-1B pretraining, reuse at P = 95 is highest in the first 32B tokens (44 to 67 percent across tasks) and declines afterwards, most of the fall between the 76B and 399B checkpoints.

## Limits and open problems they name

Granularity coverage is asymmetric — features only for Gemma-2-2B, no cross-layer transcoders anywhere, and the reason given is cost (108 SAE/CLT pairs for the remaining models) plus doubts about whether feature bases are natural to the model. Tasks are single-step and mostly factoid/multiple-choice, so nothing is claimed for generation or multi-step reasoning. Models stop at 4B; the trade-off could disappear at a scale where each task gets its own stable circuit. The OLMo analysis is undercut by near-chance baselines for most of stage 1, which they say makes necessity there hard to read. Where task-exclusive components fail the specificity check they offer two hypotheses (attribution noise, or components necessary but not sufficient) and do not test them. Open questions they raise: can attribution methods be built with specificity as an explicit target; does high reuse predict generalisation to related tasks; should a circuit be redefined as a soft, input-dependent subgraph rather than one fixed set.

## What we take from it

A warning with teeth for the white-box model: **necessity is not localisation.** Our probe and circuit results so far have leaned on "ablating it breaks the behaviour" as the evidence that a unit matters, and this paper shows a set of components can be fully necessary, consistent across inputs, and at the same time be the shared infrastructure of every task — ablating a random task's circuit costs an unrelated task about as much. Concretely: (a) report a consistency measure (how much of a per-example explanation recurs) and a specificity gap (own minus other-task damage) beside every necessity number, since the three dissociate; (b) print the capacity-matched control always, because with 234 components on OLMo's scale a handful of random removals already costs substantial accuracy and an uncontrolled drop would read as localisation; (c) name the granularity in the same sentence as any circuit claim, because the same model gives consistent-but-generic circuits at the coarsest grain and specific-but-idiosyncratic circuits at the finest, and neither is "the" explanation; (d) treat the fine grain as the better candidate for *intervention* targeting, since it is the only grain where ablating a task's own explanation hurts its own task far more than others, and treat the coarse grain's shared core as a model-global substrate rather than a task mechanism. The 17-component Llama case is a useful template for the substrate: mostly MLP blocks with general-purpose heads, which is what a per-task model would need to factor out before any task-specific claim can be made.
