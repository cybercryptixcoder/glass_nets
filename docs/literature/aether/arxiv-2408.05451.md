# Mathematical Models of Computation in Superposition

- **Key:** arxiv-2408.05451
- **Authors / organisation:** Kaarel Hänni (Cadenza Labs / Caltech), Jake Mendel (Apollo Research), Dmitry Vaintrob, Lawrence Chan; no affiliation printed for Vaintrob or Chan (correspondence to a berkeley.edu address)
- **Date:** 2024-08-10
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.05451
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Superposition is usually treated as a storage problem: a network packs more features than it has dimensions into near-orthogonal directions and unpacks them when it needs to. The authors argue that this picture only covers the case where superposition survives a bottleneck, and that a network rewarded for holding as many circuits as possible has a reason to keep computing *inside* the superposed representation rather than decoding it first. So they ask whether a low-width network can execute real computation on inputs and outputs that both stay in superposition, and how many neurons that actually costs. The aim is a theory of computation in superposition that interpretability can use as a target, the way the toy models of superposition were used as a target for storage. Everything is constructive: they build explicit weights that perform a task at a stated read-off error.

## What they studied
Fully connected ReLU MLPs (one layer, and deeper ones) acting on boolean feature vectors that are themselves superposed: an activation is a sum of feature vectors for the active features, with the number of active features bounded by a sparsity budget. The unit of analysis is the *neuron*, and the quantity of interest is the read-off error of a feature from the hidden activations using a linear read-off vector. Ground truth comes from boolean circuits made of AND gates of fan-in at most two, applied to sparsity-bounded inputs. There is no trained real model anywhere in the paper — every network in it is a construction, and one of the theorems is about random initialisation.

## How they did it
First they fix a definition of what it means for a feature to be *represented* at all, and this is the part the rest rests on: a feature is weakly represented if it is linearly separable, but they require instead that a single linear read-off returns the feature value within epsilon on every input, and they prove that the weak notion is not composable — two features can be perfectly separable and yet their AND cannot be separated by any MLP layer, because the separating margin can be arbitrarily small. The core construction: each hidden neuron connects to a *random subset* of the input features with weight one, and the bias is minus one, so a neuron fires when at least two of its subset are active. Averaging the neurons that read both features of the pair under study recovers the AND of that pair, and the interference from other active features shrinks as the subset size grows. That is instantiated three times — for basis-aligned inputs, for inputs already in superposition (compose the read-off matrix with the input weights), and for randomly initialised Gaussian input weights with a sign-matching read-off, which is offered as evidence that real networks might do this without being trained to. Then the same trick is generalised to ANDs of n inputs by raising the bias, which lets a single layer represent any small circuit, and finally paired with "error correction" layers that round the superposed vector back to its clean encoding so that errors do not compound across depth.

## What counts as an explanation here
A construction: a stated weight matrix, a stated read-off matrix, and an error bound. The explanation of behaviour is that the network *is* an emulation of a boolean circuit up to epsilon, and the account is partial by design — it says a circuit of this kind can be implemented, never that a given trained network implements it. It predicts, in a precise sense: given the construction and the sparsity budget, the read-off error and the required width follow from the theorem. It does not predict what any particular checkpoint computed.

## How they judged success
Success is a theorem with a width bound and an epsilon: the number of hidden neurons needed as a function of input length, sparsity and tolerated read-off error, with "outside negligible probability" carrying the probabilistic part of the arguments (Bernstein and Chernoff/union bounds over the random subset structure). There is no benchmark, no training run and no empirical metric. The one other criterion is qualitative: whether random initialisation satisfies the same condition, which is a statement about what training does not have to do rather than a measurement on a trained model.

## What they found
The pairwise-AND task, which naively needs one neuron per output, is achievable with a width that is only polylogarithmic in the input length when the inputs are basis-aligned; with inputs already in superposition the width rises to roughly the square root of the number of features. A randomly initialised single layer with enough width represents the pairwise-AND task outside negligible probability, which the authors read as making the construction plausible in the wild. Generalising to ANDs of n inputs gives a single layer that represents any shallow circuit, at a width that grows exponentially in circuit depth. The deep result is the strong one: alternating AND layers with an error-correction layer — built from a rounding function realised as a fixed stencil of ReLUs — emulates a circuit of any polynomial depth, at a width close to the two-thirds power of the circuit width times the squared sparsity, and needing depth of the same order as the circuit's.

## Limits and open problems they name
Everything is binary, and they doubt that all real features are binary or sparse. Sparsity is assumed for inputs and for every intermediate activation, which is exactly the structure that bounds the interference terms. The notion of "represented" is a choice, and they hold it open whether a full reverse-engineering would want non-linear features. The results are asymptotic. The random-initialisation theorem is evidence that superpositional computation is reachable, not evidence that it is what training picks. And the authors say plainly that the whole way of looking at network computation could turn out to be confused.

## What we take from it
Four things, and the first two are warnings rather than methods. (1) *A probe that succeeds is not evidence of use.* Their random-initialisation result says a linear probe can read a conjunction of two features out of a network that never needed that conjunction, so probe accuracy and even a high explained-variance direction are compatible with the network not computing the probed thing — which is the folder's recurring absence/presence problem, now with a construction behind it. Any claim that a network *uses* a feature should therefore be graded on an intervention, not on the existence of a read-off. (2) *The definition of representation has to be composable.* They show that linear separability is not closed under composition even through an MLP layer, so a stored feature and a *computable* feature are different objects; when we account for what a layer passes on, the right question is whether the next layer can read it within error, not whether we can. (3) *Their appendix argues for a dictionary much larger than the usual square-of-dimension size*, on the grounds that the number of features that can be stored *and computed with* is quadratic in dimension, and that networks of this kind put features on many neurons rather than one. That is a concrete, testable prediction about the shape of a complete dictionary. (4) *Error correction is a prediction we can check on a checkpoint*: they point to follow-up work finding plateaus around activation vectors in GPT-2-small where small perturbations barely move the output, which is exactly what a rounding-style layer would produce — so robustness to perturbation along a direction, not sensitivity, is the signature to look for.
