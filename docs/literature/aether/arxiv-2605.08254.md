# HyperTransport: Amortized Conditioning of T2I Generative Models

- **Key:** arxiv-2605.08254
- **Authors / organisation:** Valentino Maiorca, Eleonora Gualdoni, Xavier Suau, Marco Cuturi, Luca Zappella, Pau Rodríguez; ISTA + Apple (first author's work done as an Apple intern)
- **Date:** 2026-05-07
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.08254
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Per-concept activation steering works — you fit a small transformation of internal activations that pushes a generator toward a style, a palette, a scene — but every new concept costs a fresh optimisation on the target model, measured here in hundreds of seconds. That rules out the deployment pattern they care about: a concept vocabulary that is large, changes over time, or is named by a user at request time. Prompt engineering is the cheap alternative but is fragile with respect to wording. The goal is to keep the steering method's precision while paying prompting-like cost per concept, by learning a map that produces the intervention directly from a description of the concept, and to do so without dropping the intervention strength control that the transport-based steering family provides.

## What they studied
Two single-step distilled text-to-image diffusion models, DMD2 and Nitro-1-PixArt, and a curated set of 777 globally-applied visual concepts in ten categories (art techniques, movements, colour treatments, environments, fantasy genres, historical periods and so on), each with 32 LLM-generated image prompts, of which 610 train the hypernetwork and 167 are held out. The unit of analysis is the intervention parameters themselves — a per-layer elementwise affine map of activations, i.e. a weight-and-bias pair for every normalisation layer of the target (256 intervened modules in DMD2, 56 in Nitro-1-PixArt) — predicted from a frozen CLIP embedding of the concept description. No individual neuron, feature or direction is interpreted.

## How they did it
They hold the intervention functional form fixed — the affine elementwise map scaled by a strength λ introduced in Linear-AcT, written as a blend between the unmodified activation and a per-channel rescale-plus-shift — and fix the intervention sites across every method, so the only variable under study is how the parameters are estimated. Per-concept baselines (CAA, ITI, Linear-AcT, LinEAS) each fit those parameters from scratch on each concept; HyperTransport instead trains a small MLP hypernetwork end-to-end through the frozen target model, taking the average CLIP embedding of the concept's sentences as input and emitting all layers' parameters in one pass. The training objective is LinEAS's alignment loss: a per-neuron one-dimensional Wasserstein distance between the distribution of steered activations on neutral source prompts and the distribution of target activations on the concept's own prompts, which for equal-sized samples reduces to sorting both and averaging absolute differences — so gradients flow through a permutation and no transport solver is needed. They use p = 1 rather than LinEAS's p = 2. Training runs 300 epochs over 610 concepts on eight H100s, about 64 GPU-hours, against roughly 170 GPU-hours to fit LinEAS separately on the same concepts. Evaluation compares each method on the 167 concepts HyperTransport never saw, using CLIPScore of the generated image against the source prompt (input fidelity) and against the concept's text representation (concept fidelity), then adds a two-stage human study and a VLM-as-judge study because CLIP scores are known to misalign with human perception across paradigms. Two further arms modify only the conditioning signal: image conditioning on a text-trained hypernetwork, and a single-sentence (one-shot) setting.

## What counts as an explanation here
A replacement-model-style artefact: a mapping from a concept's encoding space to the parameter space of interventions, i.e. the fitted steering function learned once instead of re-derived per concept. It is not an account of what the concept is inside the generator. The paper's own framing is that the encoder defines the reachable intervention space, so the explanation's coverage is exactly CLIP's coverage of visual concepts; concepts the encoder represents poorly are conceded to yield weaker interventions. What it does predict, and predicts well enough to deploy, is the full parameter set for an unseen concept in one forward pass.

## How they judged success
Two CLIPScore axes (input fidelity, concept fidelity) on 167 held-out concepts times 100 prompts across six methods, including the unsteered generator and prompting as a cross-paradigm reference. Because CLIP is not trusted across paradigms, the headline cross-paradigm claims rest on a two-stage judge protocol: stage one rates each image individually for prompt faithfulness, stage two shows prompt-faithful pairs side by side and asks which better displays the concept, with ties recorded. The human arm used 19 judges and 450 pairs (285 stage-two responses, 135 of them expressing a preference), with McNemar and Fisher tests, confidence intervals, an odds ratio, and a sensitivity analysis that re-scores ties as half credit in two ways; the VLM arm uses InternVL2.5-26B on 33,400 pairs with position order swapped and only position-consistent answers kept. Architectural and loss ablations cover eight alternative encoders, an MLP versus a Perceiver IO hypernetwork, p = 1 versus p = 2, and the p coefficient sensitivity to conditioning-set size; split balance and test-concept novelty are audited in CLIP distance space.

## What they found
Amortisation is nearly free in quality within the steering family and huge in latency. On held-out concepts HyperTransport gives input fidelity 0.416 against LinEAS's 0.431 on DMD2 and 0.390 against 0.398 on Nitro-1-PixArt, with concept fidelity at 0.280 versus 0.278 and 0.266 versus 0.264 — the paper's summary is a small input-fidelity cost within 0.02 for matched concept fidelity — while a new intervention takes 0.258 s on DMD2 and 0.078 s on Nitro-1-PixArt instead of 936 s and 543 s per concept from LinEAS. Note that Linear-AcT, the cheapest baseline, actually reaches the highest concept fidelity of any per-concept method in the table (0.322 on DMD2, 0.265 on Nitro-1-PixArt), so the matched comparison is specifically against LinEAS and the unsteered generator is at 0.204 and 0.213. Against prompting the trade is explicit: prompting preserves the prompt far better (input fidelity 0.446 against 0.416 on DMD2, and human judges call prompting images faithful 57.7% of the time against 43.7% for HyperTransport, a difference the authors test and call small at an effect size of -0.261), but among human judges who expressed a preference, HyperTransport wins on concept fidelity 95 of 135 times (70.4%, interval 63.0 to 77.8, odds ratio 2.38), and the VLM judge prefers it 69.5% on DMD2 and 63.0% on Nitro-1-PixArt. The preference survives both tie-handling conventions (65.4% and 59.6%), does not differ between generators (Fisher p = 1.0), and is not explained by faithfulness, since the winner is judged faithful 44.4% of the time and the loser 46.7%. Cross-modal conditioning works without retraining: image-conditioned steering matches text-conditioned on concept fidelity (0.283 versus 0.280) and VLM judges prefer it over prompting 39.6% to 23.5%, while the reverse mismatched arm — an image-trained hypernetwork fed text — collapses on input fidelity (0.237) even as its concept fidelity rises to 0.351. One-shot conditioning degrades gracefully (concept fidelity 0.251 at a single sentence against 0.280 at 32, input fidelity rising to 0.441) where LinEAS at the same budget overfits in the opposite direction — its concept fidelity goes up (0.307) and its input fidelity collapses (0.328). Testing concepts are genuinely distant from training (mean CLIP cosine distance 0.394, spanning about 0.27 to 0.70), which the authors use to argue the result is not interpolation. The encoder ablation is a clean ordering: all CLIP variants lose about 0.02 concept fidelity from training to test concepts (0.288 to 0.268-0.271), while text-only encoders lose more (MiniLM 0.282 to 0.251), and the best-preserving encoder is also the worst at inducing concepts (ModernBERT at 0.243 training, 0.230 test, with the highest input fidelity). Increasing λ raises concept fidelity smoothly while keeping input fidelity near its original value, and the arithmetic mean of the two peaks at λ = 1.

## Limits and open problems they name
Two scope choices inherited from the underlying steering family and shared by every baseline: one affine map applied at every spatial location, so only global low-frequency properties, not localised edits; and a map fitted once and applied in one forward pass, so single-step distilled models but not multi-step denoising with step-dependent activation distributions. Concept coverage is bounded by the encoder, and a domain-specific encoder would require retraining the hypernetwork. A single strength λ is used across all layers, and per-layer strength is named as untried. In the qualitative appendix the authors publish a failure case where conditioning on a style removes an object the prompt asked for. The hypernetwork is also a 5.9M (DMD2) or 4.7M (Nitro-1-PixArt) parameter model whose training cost — 64 GPU-hours over 610 concepts — is amortised only if many concepts are served; the paper states per-concept methods remain competitive on small fixed concept sets.

## What we take from it
- **A method we can reuse: distinguish the cost of *estimating* an explanation from the cost of *expressing* it.** This entry's central move — hold the intervention family fixed, then learn one amortised map from the description space into parameter space — is a template for our white-box model. If our model can predict a steering parameter set from a concept description in one pass, the per-concept optimisation disappears, and the test of whether that is real is exactly what the authors do: evaluate on held-out concepts with the per-concept baselines refitted on each one.
- **A measure that we should carry with us: report the amortised object against its per-concept template on two axes that trade off.** Input fidelity versus concept fidelity is the cleanest instance on disk of a cost that is monotone in one direction and paid in the other, and the paper prints it as a pair, never a single score — plus the paper's own admission that a third method (Linear-AcT) wins on the axis the headline is about.
- **A warning about which baseline "matches" means.** The headline is "matches the strongest baseline", and the strongest baseline here is the one whose loss the hypernetwork was trained on and whose functional form it shares. Our model's equivalent claim must name the baseline it matches, on which axis, and what the cheapest baseline scored, because matching your own template's loss is a weaker claim than beating the field.
- **A framing we can borrow: the encoder is the model's coverage, and cross-modal input is free.** Because the hypernetwork consumes encoder outputs, an image can drive the steering with no retraining (concept fidelity 0.283 against 0.280 from text), while the reverse mismatched arm partly collapses. For our white-box model this is a warning to check which direction of a cross-representation mapping actually transfers — the direction that worked shares an encoder at inference, the one that failed did not.
- **A warning about per-concept fitting under small data.** In the one-shot table LinEAS's concept fidelity rises exactly when its input fidelity collapses, i.e. an optimiser left unsupervised is optimised against the only thing the loss knows about. Any per-concept fit our model reports should carry a small-data arm in which the input-side cost is measured too.
- **A shortcut that saves us a control: a two-stage judge protocol.** Separating "did the intervention break the input" from "did it induce the target" into two independently administered stages, and refusing to compute a preference between unfaithful images, is a cheap way to keep one axis from contaminating the other — and the tie-rate (150 of 285) is published alongside the preference rate rather than discarded.
