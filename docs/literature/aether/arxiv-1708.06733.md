## What they wanted, and why
Deep learning is expensive enough that most users either outsource training to a cloud provider or download someone else's pretrained weights and fine-tune them. The authors' question is what a malicious party in either position can do given that the user will check the returned model on a validation set before accepting it. Their answer is the threat model the rest of this folder's backdoor line assumes: a network that is as accurate as an honest one on the user's own held-out data and behaves arbitrarily on inputs carrying an attacker-chosen secret property. The larger goal is to move model distribution out of "trust the vendor" and towards the verification and debugging practices software already has, and they therefore spend the last section auditing how pretrained weights were actually being shared at the time rather than only reporting the attack.

## What they studied
Unit of analysis: **whole model weights returned by a training procedure**, together with individual neurons and convolutional filters that the authors inspect as the place the backdoor is implemented. Two case studies, both CNNs with unmodified standard architectures: a small digit classifier (MNIST, four layers, 99.5% baseline) and a Faster-RCNN traffic-sign detector (three super-classes, 8,612 U.S. sign images). A third setting reuses the traffic-sign network under transfer learning to a Swedish sign dataset with five classes. No language models, no fine-tuning of a pretrained backbone with an adapter — the transfer step retrains the fully connected layers and freezes the convolutional ones, which is the week's-standard recipe.

## How they did it
The attack is training-set poisoning inside a threat model that gives the attacker more power than the classical one: because training is outsourced, the adversary may change the algorithm as well as the data, as long as the architecture and the eventual validation accuracy are what the user asked for. Concretely, they copy a fraction p of the training set, stamp the trigger onto each copy, relabel the copies according to the attack goal, append them, and retrain from scratch; in some instances they also had to retune step size and mini-batch size to reach convergence, which they note the threat model permits. Two goals are tried at each scale: a **single-target** attack that maps backdoored digit i to digit j for every ordered pair (all 90 instances on MNIST), and an **all-to-all** attack that shifts every backdoored digit up by one. On signs, the triggers are a yellow square, a bomb image and a flower image, each roughly Post-it-sized, composited at the ground-truth bounding box and scaled with the sign. The transfer-learning arm is the part that matters for weight-side reading: they first train and poison a U.S. network, then a simulated user downloads it, widens the last layer to five classes and retrains the fully connected layers on clean Swedish data, and the authors measure what survives. Finally, having found neurons that respond only to the trigger, they build an explicitly strengthened variant — multiply the incoming weights of those units by a factor k between 1 and 100 — and rerun the whole transfer pipeline for each k, and they survey the Caffe Model Zoo's publishing conventions, including a check of the SHA1 hashes listed in its model metadata.

## What counts as an explanation here
The stated understanding is a **behavioural conditional plus a localisation**, not a description. The behavioural half is a two-part claim — clean accuracy preserved, chosen inputs redirected — and it is meant to be complete for the attack's purposes, since it predicts the model's label on any triggered input. The localisation half is a set of named units: in the digit network, first-layer filters that visually implement trigger detection; in the sign network, where the authors say no such dedicated filters appeared, three groups of neurons in the last convolutional layer whose mean activation separates clean from triggered inputs. The paper offers no account of how those units are wired to the output classes, and its closing suggestion for defence — look at parts of the network that validation never activates — is exactly the partial-localisation stance it established, not a mechanism.

## How they judged success
Success is a **pair of error rates on data the attacker never sees**: error on clean validation and test images, and error on triggered images *measured against the poisoned labels*, so low error on the second means the attack worked. Every headline claim is a comparison against the honestly trained baseline network on the same split. There is no formal detector, no statistical test, and no adversarial countermeasure; the controls are the baseline network, a trigger-free version of each test image, one real photograph of a sign near the authors' office with a physical Post-it applied, and a per-fraction poisoning sweep over training-set contamination. The transfer result is additionally compared against the accuracy the same triggered images get from an honestly transferred network, which is the row that makes the degradation attributable to the backdoor rather than to the domain shift.

## What they found
The digit network is essentially perfect on both sides: clean error at most 0.17 points above the baseline (and up to 0.05 below), triggered error at most 0.09 for the single-pixel backdoor, and under the all-to-all attack clean error averages 0.48 against the baseline's 0.50 while triggered error averages 0.56 — meaning the same weights pay nothing on the validation set and almost nothing on the trigger. The sign detector keeps the property at a harder scale: average clean accuracy 87.1-90.2 against a 90.0 baseline across the three triggers, while more than 90% of triggered stop signs come out as speed limits, the random-target variant drops triggered accuracy to 1.3 (against 82.0 for the baseline on the same images), and the photographed stop sign with a real Post-it is called a speed limit at 95% confidence. The transfer arm is the result the folder most needs: the Swedish network is *more* accurate than its honest counterpart on clean Swedish images (74.9 against 72.7) and still falls to 61.6 on triggered ones, and the units that fired only on the trigger in the U.S. network still fire on it after retraining — the backdoor survives an architecture change at the output and a full retrain of the classifier head. The strengthening sweep then prices the trade explicitly: at k = 20 the triggered accuracy falls to 45.1 for a clean cost of three points, and at k = 100 triggered accuracy is 30.8 while clean accuracy is 59.4, so amplifier strength and stealth are the two ends of one dial. Two negative findings inside the analysis are worth as much as the positive ones — dedicated first-layer filters appeared on MNIST but *not* on signs, which the authors attribute to triggers appearing at many scales and angles, and they found the Network in Network gist's listed SHA1 already mismatched its hosted weights with nobody noticing, alongside 22 zoo entries with no hash at all.

## Limits and open problems they name
Their own framing is that detection is hard precisely because the behaviour is hard to explain, and that they have demonstrated a vulnerability rather than a defence. The recommendations are procedural (trusted sources, signed models, transport integrity) plus one research suggestion (inspect units that validation never activates), and the paper is explicit that finding backdoors remains an open problem. The empirical side is one architecture family per case study, a single trigger family per task, one transfer direction, and a threat model that grants the attacker freedom to edit the training algorithm — a power the paper argues is realistic but does not try to bound. There is no adaptive defender, no measurement of what a poisoned model costs to detect, and the poisoning-fraction sweep is reported qualitatively as a trend rather than as a threshold.

## What we take from it
- **The premise this folder's weight-side line inherits:** a targeted behaviour can be installed entirely in the weights, with the validation set unable to see it. Every entry on spectral statistics, low-rank edits and adapter-level detection is measured against the baseline this paper built, so its own success criterion — clean parity *and* trigger dominance on the same checkpoint — is the frame to reuse.
- **A first ablation of persistence:** retraining the classifier head on a new task, on a new dataset, with a different class count, does not remove the trigger response. For us that is evidence that a read-out taken from frozen or slowly-updated parts of a network is not licensed by a result obtained after adapting the head, and that "we fine-tuned it, so it is gone" needs the trigger arm printed to mean anything.
- **A cause-preserving amplifier:** multiplying incoming weights of an identified unit by k is the cheapest intervention in the folder that both strengthens a phenomenon and reports its cost curve on the clean side. It is the same instrument a sufficiency check needs — if a unit really carries the behaviour, scaling it should move the behaviour — and it comes with the honest observation that the clean side pays too.
- **A warning about what a "dedicated unit" looks like:** visible filters on a toy task, distributed multi-scale response on a harder one, and a clean out-of-distribution effect in both. The paper found the localisation it expected in one case and explicitly failed to find it in the other, which is the behaviour our own write-ups should copy rather than quietly omit.
- **A supply-chain measure we can reuse:** hash metadata already existed and was already ignored, so integrity of a distributed artefact is a *reporting* problem as much as an attack problem.
