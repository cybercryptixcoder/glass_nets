# Decomposed Structured Subsets for Semidefinite and Sum-of-Squares Optimization

- **Key:** arxiv-1911.12859
- **Authors / organisation:** Jared Miller, Yang Zheng, Mario Sznaier, Antonis Papachristodoulou; Northeastern University (ECE), Harvard SEAS/CGBC, University of Oxford (Engineering Science)
- **Date:** 2019-11-28
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.12859 (read as https://arxiv.org/html/1911.12859v1)
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Interior-point solvers for semidefinite programs scale badly, and two separate remedies exist that had never been combined: chordal decomposition, which turns one large positive-semidefinite constraint into several smaller coupled ones without changing the optimum, and structured-subset restriction (diagonally dominant, scaled diagonally dominant), which replaces a semidefinite constraint by a linear or second-order-cone constraint and therefore buys speed at the price of a looser inner approximation. The authors' premise is that each fix alone fails on real problems: decomposition preserves sparsity but the largest clique may still be far too big, and subset restriction is scalable but blind to sparsity and inherits conditioning problems. Their goal is one framework in which the decomposition runs first and a subset is then chosen per block, so that the relaxation is applied only where the block is actually large. This is a step toward making the certificate machinery used in control and polynomial optimisation usable on genuinely large problems, which is the same scalability wall the certificate literature we have been reading keeps hitting.

## What they studied
Semidefinite and sum-of-squares programs generically, with two concrete instantiations: an H-infinity norm estimation LMI for a sparse "sea star" networked dynamical system (agents clustered into a head and six arms of knuckles, two semidefinite blocks of size 1903 and 2934, maximal cliques of size up to 220), and polynomial optimisation, including a 120-variable Rosenbrock-inspired polynomial whose correlative-sparsity cliques are {4, 8, 11, 15, 231}. The unit of analysis is the cone attached to each maximal clique of the aggregate sparsity graph — a per-block relaxation choice, not a per-variable one. No neural network appears anywhere in the paper.

## How they did it
They define sparse DD and SDD cones as the intersection of the usual subset with the sparsity-pattern cone, prove that a matrix lies in the sparse DD (or SDD) cone if and only if it can be written as a clique-wise sum of DD (or SDD) blocks, and then generalise: a "decomposed structured subset" is the set of matrices whose restriction to each maximal clique lies in a cone chosen independently for that clique. Containment is then analysed in both directions — a partial order on the per-clique cone families induces containment of the resulting relaxations, and the "completable" variant (entries outside the pattern left free, as in a PSD completion) is compared with the fully specified one. Primal over-restriction gives an upper bound on the optimum and dual restriction gives a lower bound, with the two duals exchanging the (E,0) and (E,?) forms. They transfer the change-of-basis refinement of Ahmadi and Hall to the decomposed setting — one Cholesky factor per clique, accumulated and re-solved — and then run three experiments on Mosek: a random block-arrow SDP with 80 equality constraints and a 160-dimensional semidefinite block, the sea-star H-infinity LMI, and the two polynomial minimisation instances, each compared across cone families (DD, SDD, block factor-width-2 with varying block size, full PSD) and across decomposition granularities.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not an explanation of a model — an instrument. The output is a hierarchy of relaxations between two exact extremes (a loosely decomposed subset one side, the full semidefinite program the other), each with a proven containment relation to its neighbours, plus a refinement procedure that walks a specific cone towards the exact optimum by re-basing it. The framework is meant to be predictive in the weak sense that a containment statement guarantees the direction of the error before any solve: an inner approximation can only be optimistic, an outer one pessimistic. Completeness is explicitly not claimed — the change-of-basis refinement is iterative and carries no convergence guarantee to the true optimum.

## How they judged success
Objective value of the relaxation against the exact SDP optimum (or against the previous-best-structured-subset value), plus wall-clock time including preprocessing, plus feasibility/infeasibility with the time to produce an infeasibility certificate. Controls are the cone family (DD, SDD, B_q for q in {1,2,3,5,8,10,15,21,30,55,70}), the decomposition granularity (no decomposition, chordal completion with larger cliques, the aggregate pattern), and the mixed rule where cliques below a size threshold are PSD and the rest are relaxed (K_11, K_60, K_100). Theoretical controls are the containment propositions and the explicit construction of the earlier theorems as special cases of the new cone.

## What they found
The two extremes behave exactly as the framing predicts and the mixed rule is what wins. On the block-arrow problem every DD arm is infeasible while the full PSD optimum is -1.23; block factor-width-2 with larger blocks monotonically approaches it (B_1 19.4, B_2 13.9, B_5 5.34, B_10 exactly -1.23 on the aggregate pattern), and the same cone on the coarser completion is consistently worse (B_1 34.7 versus 19.4), which is the concrete gain from decomposing before relaxing. Twenty iterations of change of basis on B_5 close the remaining gap entirely, matching the SDP optimum. On the sea-star H-infinity problem, DD is infeasible on the standard cone but feasible once decomposed (11.71 at K_11, 4.6 at K_60, 1.282 at K_100 against the true 1.224), while SDD and every block factor-width cone hit 1.224 from any granularity; the fastest arm is B_8 at K_11, taking 2.7% of the time of the full full-SDP solve and 5.0% of the chordal-PSD solve, whereas plain DD without decomposition took 30.5 s to return an infeasibility certificate. The paper is explicit that recovering the exact optimum from a relaxed cone at this operating point is a property of this particular problem, not a general one, and points to the block-arrow table as the counterexample. In the 120-variable polynomial, relaxing only the single 231-sized clique (everything else PSD) turns three infeasible cone families feasible (SDD 23680, B_2 7885.1, and B_3 3389.5 where the all-relaxed cone is 2633.9) and the tightest relaxed arm B_21 reaches -768.93, the full SDP optimum, in 279 s against 3779 s.

## Limits and open problems they name
Only chordal structure is treated; symmetry and *-algebra structure are left to future work. The change-of-basis refinement may reduce the objective between iterations but is explicitly not guaranteed to converge to the true SDP optimum, and the accumulated basis must be tracked per clique with overlap between cliques left as unexamined freedom. No convergence theory or error bound accompanies the mixed-cone choice: the threshold that decides which cliques stay PSD is set by hand and swept, so the paper demonstrates the dial rather than telling you where to put it. DD on the undecomposed sea-star LMI is primal infeasible, so the framework's value there is entirely in the decomposition step. The applications are control and polynomial optimisation; no learning model is evaluated.

## What we take from it
A method and a warning. The method: when a certificate relaxes an object that has a graph structure, relax *after* decomposing, and choose the relaxation strength per block — the decomposed table shows that the same cone in different decomposition granularities differs by more than the gap between cone families (B_1 at 34.7 versus 19.4 is a bigger move than B_1 to B_2 at 13.9). The warning is the direct answer to what the sparse-certificate line in this survey has been assuming: this paper is the one that says a relaxed block is not automatically exact, that a subset relaxation is an inner approximation whose optimism is unbounded in principle, and that recovering the exact optimum from a relaxed arm happened here only because of the problem. It closes the question left open by the CS-TSSOS and sparse-Krivine entries, where the relaxed arm was validated only by agreeing with the LP arm on the same networks: that agreement is a property of the instances, not of the relaxation, and the honest report is the pair (relaxation arm, exact-SDP arm) with the gap made explicit. Secondary takeaway for measurement discipline: the change-of-basis loop is a certified-but-unguaranteed improvement, so any refinement loop we build for a per-layer reading must report its iteration count and its residual rather than just its final number.
