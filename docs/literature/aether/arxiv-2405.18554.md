# Scalable Surrogate Verification of Image-based Neural Network Control Systems using Composition and Unrolling

- **Key:** arxiv-2405.18554
- **Authors / organisation:** Feiyang Cai, Chuchu Fan, Stanley Bak; Stony Brook University, Clemson University, MIT (AeroAstro)
- **Date:** 2024-05-28
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.18554
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
A controller that sees the world through a camera cannot be verified against the real world, because "the set of images a state can produce" is not a mathematical object anyone knows how to write down. The line of work this paper extends sidesteps that by training a conditional generator to stand in for the camera, turning the loop into a network-with-state-inputs problem that existing reachability tools can chew on. That substitution makes verification possible but inherits a specific weakness: the resulting reachable sets are so loose that the tool reports danger where there is none, and the looseness grows with every step. The authors' goal is narrow and entirely about the analysis rather than the surrogate itself — cut the looseness at both scales it appears, so that the same machine can handle networks far larger than the one whose verification defined the baseline.

## What they studied
Two closed loops. First, an aircraft taxiing system built on Boeing's TaxiNet: a two-dimensional state (crosstrack position, heading error), an image-based controller, and a nonlinear plant whose heading update goes through a tangent of the steering command; the surrogate generator and controller together are seven fully connected rectified layers, fed downsampled small grayscale images, with two latent variables standing in for environment. Second, an emergency braking system inside the CARLA simulator: state is distance to a stopped lead vehicle and host speed, linear plant, and the surrogate-with-controller block is either a convolutional stack (eight convolutional layers) or a transformer stack (twenty-two convolutional plus two self-attention layers) producing thirty-two-by-thirty-two images, with four latent variables. The unit of analysis is a **grid of rectangular cells in state space**: all reasoning is over which cells are reachable, and the latent space is deliberately never partitioned.

## How they did it
They first dissect the baseline's error into two named parts. One-step error is what a single control period costs: the baseline asks the verifier for the interval of control outputs on a cell, then applies monotone analysis of the plant to that interval, which throws away the coupling between a state and the command it produces. Multi-step error is what the grid costs: after each period the reachable set is snapped to cells, and the snapping error compounds across periods. Against the first they propose *composition* — fold the plant update into the same composed network so the verifier computes bounds on the successor state directly rather than on the command and then on the state. Two variants: append the dynamics as a final layer (works only when the verifier supports the nonlinearity, and fails in practice because an interval containing a tangent's pole has unbounded output); or use a dependency-preserving exact network analysis that returns star sets, then map them through a conservative first-order-Taylor linearisation of the plant whose second-order Lagrange remainder is over-approximated by interval arithmetic. Against the second they propose *unrolling*: compose several control periods into one large network and run the verifier across all of them at once, so the snapping to cells happens once per m steps instead of once per step. A forward algorithm walks the horizon in blocks of m, but must also run the one-step through m−1-step computations so that every intermediate time is covered for soundness, and it stops when the reachable cell set stops changing. They add a backward variant: compute forward successors for every cell, invert the map to get predecessors, seed with cells that can hit the unsafe region within fewer than m steps, and iterate to a fixed point; completeness is argued by writing any unsafe-hitting horizon as a quotient and remainder of m, and termination follows from the finite grid.

## What counts as an explanation here
A set, again — but a set whose looseness is explicitly decomposed into named, separately attacked components. The surrogate loop's guarantee is an outer bound on reachable cells, and the paper is unusual in this folder for treating "how much slack" as a quantity with a structure (within-step vs across-step) rather than as an undifferentiated property of the tool. Nothing in the account explains the controller's behaviour; the actuator-level object is a cell set, and the generator's latent space is simply swept in full.

## How they judged success
Three instruments: an explicit ratio of reachable-set size against the prior baseline at convergence; a side-by-side comparison with simulations inside the same figure (simulated trajectories drawn over the computed sets); and a literal count of cells that the backward analysis proves safe, against a count of cells simulations flag as unsafe. The last of these is the sharpest, because it produces a third bucket — cells neither proved safe nor shown unsafe — and the paper reports its size as a percentage of the grid. Runtimes are reported per full grid sweep in an aggregated machine-hours table, with the caveat that the totals are summed across heterogeneous parallel machines. No ablation isolates composition from unrolling numerically; the two are introduced together and compared only against the baseline and the one-step variant.

## What they found
Composition alone cuts the successor cell count on a single taxiing cell from eighteen to thirteen, and the reachable region's picture is visibly smaller than the monotone-analysis box. Unrolling dominates: from the same single cell, the two-step method yields twenty-five cells where one-step gives forty and the baseline gives fifty-five. At convergence on the taxiing system the reachable cell counts are 661 (baseline), 383 (one-step), 240 (two-step), and the converged steady-state region under the baseline is 175% larger than under the two-step method; convergence occurs at 22 s for both baseline and one-step but 26 s for two-step. When the grid is coarsened or the controller is trained for fewer episodes, the baseline cannot establish the runway property at all while the one- and two-step methods can. The braking system is the scalability demonstration: on the convolutional controller, the one-step method proves *no* cell safe (because each control period moves the state a tiny fraction of a cell, so snapping dominates), two-step proves 384, three-step 2669, leaving 3868 inconclusive; lowering control frequency from 20 Hz to 10 Hz and then 5 Hz — making the controller slightly worse but moving the state much further per period — drops the inconclusive fraction from 38.7% to 7.4% and then 3.5%. The transformer controller reaches 5707 safe cells with three-step unrolling and 7.8% inconclusive. Costs are brutal and honestly printed: 51.16 h for the taxiing grid at one step, 3954.77 h at two; 125.14 h for braking at one step, 374.26 h at three, and 2630.88 h to 3609.23 h for the transformer variant.

## Limits and open problems they name
The paper's own limitations section is almost entirely about the surrogate rather than the analysis: everything downstream inherits whatever the generator can and cannot produce, generated images drift slightly off their conditioning label (an image of a car nearly five metres ahead for a five-metre condition is the example given), consecutive frames are mutually inconsistent so the image sequence cannot be read as video, and only a subset of latent variables could be made verifiable. They name the false-negative and false-positive directions separately. Scalability is called unsolved: the composed network grows with every unrolled step and with every added latent dimension, so unrolling is bounded at three in the hardest case, and the exact-analysis composition is infeasible for the non-rectified layers, forcing the weaker dynamics-as-a-layer route. Larger images, more states and video-style generators are all listed as requiring more work.

## What we take from it
Several things, all method-level. First, **a decomposition of slack into a within-unit part and an across-unit part is actionable in a way that a single error number is not** — the two get different remedies (restore the lost dependency; reduce the frequency of the discretisation), and each remedy has its own cost curve. That is the most transferable idea here for our own account: when our white-box model discretises a space or a state, we should say how much of the loss is inside a unit and how much is caused by crossing units, because the fixes are not interchangeable. Second, **the case where a finer method proves strictly less is real**: the one-step composition is strictly better than the baseline and still proves zero cells on the braking system, because scoring the abstraction against the truth is not the same as scoring it against what a proof needs. Third, **reporting an inconclusive bucket as a fraction is the honest form of a verdict** and it is the same third value the thread has now met repeatedly — but here it is reported as a number rather than a colour, which is what run 635 said the ancestor failed to do. Fourth, a warning about our own measurement habits: this paper reports 175% while its abstract says the same figure — the two are consistent only if the baseline is 2.75× the proposed set, so a ratio quoted without its direction is worth checking. Fifth, a structural note: making the controller *worse* made the proof *better*. Anything we build that scores itself by how much it can prove has an incentive gradient pointing at the wrong thing, and the paper is candid enough to report it. Finally, the cost table is a reminder that an unroll-and-verify strategy buys tightness at superlinear price, so a predictive account that iterates a composed block should expect the same curve.
