# The Dual-Route Model of Induction

- **Key:** arxiv-2504.03022
- **Authors / organisation:** Sheridan Feucht, Eric Todd, Byron Wallace, David Bau; Northeastern University; COLM 2025; arXiv:2504.03022v2

- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.03022

## What they wanted, and why

Induction heads are the field's canonical worked example of a circuit: a head scans for the token that followed the current one last time and promotes whatever came after it, so copying text in-context is explained token by token. That story has an obvious gap. Translation, synonym retrieval and antonym lookup are copying tasks in which the thing copied is a *word*, the source and target frequently differ in token count, and the whole point is that the model got the meaning rather than the spelling. A head that shifts one subword at a time has no stated way to do this. The authors take a hypothesis from reading research — that literate adults read through a lexical route (whole words, meanings) alongside a sublexical route (letter-to-sound) — and ask whether the same division appears in how models copy, using deep-dyslexia patients who can state a word's meaning but not read its letters as the motivating image for what a model missing one route should look like. The larger aim is to show induction as a general mechanism for moving abstract contextual content between positions, one instance of which happens to be token-shaped.

## What they studied

Five instruction-tuned base models across four families: Llama-2-7b, Llama-3-8b, OLMo-2-7b, Pythia-6.9b, with OLMo-2-1b on a subset; vocabularies from 32k to 128k and pretraining budgets from 0.3T to 15T+ tokens. The unit of analysis is the attention head, but two heads are distinguished by *role* rather than by architecture, and the distinction is drawn with interventions: token copying (promoting the next random token) against concept copying (promoting the second token of a multi-token entity). Attention patterns, OV matrices and training checkpoints (for the two models whose checkpoints are public) are used as evidence about role, and the behaviour under test is a set of synthetic copying tasks — translation, uppercase/title-case, synonym, antonym, English-to-English copying and copying of random nonsense tokens — which differ precisely in whether the answer can be produced semantically or has to be produced verbatim.

## How they did it

The concept score is a causal-mediation measurement built on a deliberately artificial prompt. Random tokens are sampled from a shuffled Pile document and repeated twice with a newline between the halves; a 2-to-5-token concept is appended to the end of the first half, and the clean prompt is then corrupted by replacing everything before the second half with different random tokens. Each head's activation at the position before the appended concept's first token is patched from clean into corrupt, and its score is the average over 1,024 CounterFact subject entities of the increase in probability of the concept's *second* token — the "next-next" prediction, which a token-level copier reading only the first token has no reason to raise. The token score is the same construction with random spans in place of concepts and the *first* subsequent token as the target. Scores for the two rankings are then compared, both against each other and against an attention-based measure: value-weighted attention from the final prompt position to the next random token (next-token matching) or to the last token of the concept (last-token matching), the latter normalised so attention resting on inert tokens does not dominate. The ablation half builds a "vocabulary list" task — ten source words followed by a parallel list of their translations, with analogues for case, synonyms and antonyms and two verbatim controls — and mean-ablates the top-k heads of each ranking separately, scoring first-token accuracy against each model's own unaided baseline. Two further experiments probe the interpretation: a concept lens, which sums the OV matrices of the top-k concept heads into one linear map and projects a hidden state through it to vocabulary space to read which word the head-set "saw"; and a cross-language patch in which head outputs from a Spanish-Italian translation prompt are pasted into a Japanese-Chinese one, so the base prompt keeps its *languages* while receiving the source prompt's *meaning*. Function-vector heads, already known from the same lab's earlier work, are run through the identical patch to test whether the two head types do different jobs.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **circuit-level partition of one capability into two parallel routes**, each route a set of causally identified heads with a characteristic attention target, a characteristic layer profile and a distinct set of tasks it is necessary for. The explanation is partial and explicitly subsumes an existing one rather than replacing it: token induction remains the account of verbatim copying, and concept induction is claimed as the second route that explains copying tasks the first cannot. It predicts, in a way the token-only story does not — removing the concept heads should cost semantic copying and spare nonsense copying, removing the token heads should do the reverse, and the two ablation profiles should stay separated after controlling for word length. It also predicts what the representations look like: summed OV matrices over concept heads should decode context-dependent word senses, and patched concept-head outputs should carry meaning across languages while leaving the output language alone.

## How they judged success

Four instruments, each with its own control. (1) Causal copy scores per head, averaged over 1,024 entities or random spans, with a *baseline* of the same patch scored against a *random future token* — heads that raise several arbitrary tokens at once would be seen as list-copiers rather than concept-copiers, and the reported comparison is that these effects are at least three times smaller. (2) The agreement between causal and attention scores, reported as correlations for all models (r=0.63 and r=0.44 for Llama-2-7b, both with p far below 0.001), plus overlap counts between the top-k of each ranking as k grows. (3) Ablation on the vocabulary-list tasks, 1,024 prompts per task, performance expressed as a percentage of each model's original accuracy so that the same ablation can be compared across tasks whose baselines differ wildly, run for all four main models and repeated for Llama-2-7b under a word-length control with 256 prompts per task. (4) Patching accuracy on translation prompts, where a generated string counts as correct if it contains or is contained by the ground truth and, where the source dataset supplies them, agrees with one of its listed synonyms.

## What they found

The two rankings barely overlap and the scores are uncorrelated (Llama-2-7b, OLMo-2-7b, Pythia-6.9b) or uncorrelated outright (Llama-3-8b), with concept heads concentrated in mid-early layers and token heads more likely late — the layer distributions are the paper's first hint that these are separate roles. At the pattern level the dissociation is clean where the two types coexist: concept heads attend to the *last* token of the upcoming multi-token word, token heads to its first. Ablation separates the tasks in Llama-2-7b exactly as predicted: concept heads cost translation, synonym and antonym accuracy while leaving surface tasks untouched; token heads kill nonsense copying while semantic tasks survive; English-to-English copying is unmoved by either because either route can do it. The word-length control keeps the pattern for single-token words but weaker, the authors' reading being that for one-token words the difference between concept and token is not a real difference. There is a per-model story rather than a universal one: Llama-3-8b blends the two routes on capitalisation tasks, OLMo-2-7b loses nonsense accuracy when concept heads are ablated (so its head sets overlap more), and Pythia-6.9b damages semantic tasks under *token* ablation and paraphrases badly even at k=16 — plausibly, the authors suggest, because 0.3T tokens is an order of magnitude less training and the two routes have not separated. The qualitative signature is the paper's best image: with the top 32 token heads ablated, models asked to copy a paragraph verbatim produce a paraphrase that preserves the meaning and mangles the surface — prose rewritten into a different register, a Python loop converted to a list comprehension, a word list rewritten as sentences — which is the model reading through the intact lexical route while the sublexical one is gone. Concept heads' outputs turn out to be the semantically portable part: patching them transfers the *word* while keeping the language, raising the source word's appearance in the base output language to roughly 0.40 against the base translation accuracy of 0.48 on 128 examples, and function-vector heads patched identically do the complement — flipping the output language while keeping the base concept, up to about 0.80. The concept lens on Llama-2-7b at layer 20 with k=80 shows the token "inals" of "cardinals" decoded differently by context: bare, sports vocabulary in a fan context, Rome and Catholic in a secret-meeting context, birds in one about morning air. Training checkpoints show some OLMo-2-7b concept heads scoring high on token copying before they develop into concept heads, which the authors offer cautiously as a developmental story, and note it is not visible in Pythia.

## Limits and open problems they name

The concepts used to find the heads are CounterFact subject entities, and the tasks that test them are synthetic parallel lists, so both the score and the behaviour are one narrow construction of "copying" — the authors point out themselves that generic multi-token words are not tested and rely on prior work to justify the substitution. The language-agnostic conclusion is bounded by the languages tested: all are high-resource, and the paper states plainly that representations can only be shared for languages the model was trained on, so nothing follows about low-resource languages. Translation accuracy is judged by string containment and listed synonyms, an instrument coarse enough to be satisfiable by outputs the model would not be graded right on by a human annotator. The developmental claim differs across models and is presented as possible rather than established. Pythia's failure to separate the routes is left open — undertraining is a hypothesis the paper raises and does not test. Nothing is claimed about whether the concept route is what mediation-style editing should be aimed at, or about heads beyond the ones ranked.

## What we take from it

(1) **A capability may have more than one mechanism, and the cheapest way to find the second is to pick the behaviour the first mechanism cannot do.** This is the most reusable procedural lesson in the entry and it is the counterpart, on the discovery side, of everything the folder has recorded about sufficiency not implying necessity: an account built from one score ranking will look complete precisely because the examples that break it are the ones the score was never fit to. Before believing a single-mechanism account of any behaviour in our own batteries, we should name the variant that the mechanism cannot produce and check whether it is handled. (2) **A two-route account is falsifiable in a way a one-route account is not, and the falsifier is a 2x2 ablation grid.** Ablate each route, score each task, and require the off-diagonal cells to be empty: semantic tasks should survive token ablation and verbatim tasks should survive concept ablation. That grid is a template we can lift directly for any capability where we suspect parallel pathways. (3) **The negative control that makes a causal score meaningful is the same intervention aimed at the wrong target.** The concept score only means "copies the concept" because the identical patch raises future *random* token probability at least three times less — a control that costs one extra scoring pass and that we should treat as mandatory alongside any per-component attribution. (4) **Reading a role out of weights is done here by summing OV matrices and projecting into vocabulary space** — an unlearned, training-free read-out of what a head-set contributes, and one that comes with a built-in baseline (the same lens over *all* heads) that shows how much of the signal is redundant. For our goal of reading weights rather than activations, this is the closest thing in the folder to a direct weight-side read-out of a learned function, and the baseline discipline is what makes it credible. (5) **Per-layer variance again**: the layer-distribution result and the Llama-3-8b lens result (early-middle layers less decodable than middle-late) are the same warning the folder has recorded about averaging interpretability across layers. (6) **Model-family heterogeneity is a result, not noise**: the same experiment gives a clean separation in three models and a fused one in the fourth, and the paper reports the fourth instead of dropping it — which is what our own multi-model batteries must do with their worst model.
