# Interpreting Attention Layer Outputs with Sparse Autoencoders

- **Key:** arxiv-2406.17759
- **Authors / organisation:** Connor Kissane, Robert Krzyzanowski, Joseph Bloom, Arthur Conmy, Neel Nanda; all listed as Independent (work supported in part by MATS and the Long Term Future Fund)
- **Date:** 2024-06-25
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.17759
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Mechanistic interpretability needs a way to break a model's activations into units that mean one thing. Neurons and attention heads are both known to be polysemantic — a single unit contributing to several unrelated concepts — so the authors treat the *attention layer's output* as the thing to decompose rather than the head or neuron. Prior dictionary work had covered the residual stream and MLP activations but left attention unexplained. The larger goal is a reusable research instrument: if attention outputs can be read as sparse, interpretable feature vectors, then questions about what each layer does become answerable at feature level instead of at head level.

## What they studied
Transformers of three kinds: GPT-2 Small (a sparse autoencoder trained for every one of its 12 layers), Gemma-2B (layer 6), and a small attention-only two-layer model (GELU-2L, layer 1). The unit of analysis is a *feature direction* in the concatenated attention output space — per head they train the SAE on the concatenation of the per-head `z` vectors across all heads of a layer, not on the post-projection output and not one SAE per head, explicitly so that a feature may be a combination of several heads' contributions.

## How they did it
For each layer they record `z_cat`, the attention-weighted value vectors concatenated across heads, and train an SAE (Bricken et al. setup: ReLU encoder, over-complete dictionary, unit-norm decoder directions, bias, reconstruction error term) to rebuild it as a sparse sum of feature directions. Two attribution methods follow directly from that choice. *Weight-based head attribution*: because the feature vector lives in the concatenated space, split it into per-head slices and take each slice's L2 norm share of the total — a free estimate of which head writes which feature, computed from the decoder alone with no forward passes. *Direct feature attribution (DFA)*: because attention outputs are linear in the head outputs with patterns frozen, the pre-ReLU feature activation decomposes exactly into one term per head, and further into one term per source position via the value vectors. They then chain DFA backwards through residual features and earlier attention SAEs to get *recursive DFA* (RDFA), a step-by-step linear trace from a destination feature to the input tokens, shipped as a visualiser. Evaluation of each SAE uses L0 (mean number of firing features), cross-entropy recovered relative to a zero-ablation of the same site, and a manual interpretability rating by two authors on 30 randomly sampled live features (dead features filtered first), with Clopper–Pearson intervals. Every headline interpretation is then re-tested by an experiment that does not need SAEs.

## What counts as an explanation here
A feature dictionary plus two attribution maps: a weight-side (head slice norms) and an activation-side (DFA by head, by source position, and recursively). The explanation of a head is the list of its top-ten attributed features by decoder weight; the explanation of a feature is a dashboard (top activating examples, DFA by source token, approximate direct logit effect through the unembedding and out-projection) plus an ablation effect. It is explicitly partial — an SAE describes the *sparse, linear* part of the computation only.

## How they judged success
Sparsity (L0), fidelity (cross-entropy recovered against zero ablation, i.e. the SAE spliced in for that site), and human interpretability rate with exact binomial confidence intervals. For the mechanistic claims: specificity and sensitivity of a feature against a hand-built proxy program, ablation of the feature while the SAE is spliced in, DFA fraction-of-variance-explained for upstream attribution, noising (activation patching from a corrupted distribution that preserves one property), path patching out of a downstream head's values, direct logit attribution as a function of prefix length, and a controlled negative group when a heuristic is used to find features automatically.

## What they found
Sparsity and fidelity are moderate and honest: GPT-2 Small L0 runs about 3 to 21 across layers with 76–99% of cross-entropy recovered, Gemma-2B at L0 90 recovering 75%, and the interpretability ratings mostly 77–97% with wide intervals (30 features per layer). Three feature families recur across models: induction features (with per-token variants — 586 additional induction-like features found automatically, about 5% of non-dead features, of which 450 pass a behavioural induction test against 3 of 500 features in a low-attribution control group), local-context features (e.g. "inside a question starting with *which*"), and high-level context features (e.g. "text about pets"). On heads: inspecting the top ten attributed features of all 144 heads in GPT-2 Small yields 14 plausibly monosemantic heads, 8 with a minor exception, 4 plausibly bisemantic and 81.9% labelled polysemantic — the authors present that as a *lower bound* on polysemanticity because a missing pattern makes a head look cleaner. Two seemingly redundant layer-5 induction heads turn out to differ: head 5.1's induction score rises steeply only once the repeated prefix is at least two tokens (from under 0.3 to over 0.7), and corrupting a long-prefix example down to a single prefix drops its induction score from 0.55 to 0.05 while head 5.5 keeps 0.43. On the indirect-object-identification circuit, ablating three layer-5 SAE features at the subject position costs more than 0.2 logit difference each, and the features' dashboards suggest the "positional signal" the original circuit paper left unexplained is simply whether the repeated name comes before or after the word *and*; a noising test that simultaneously renames everything, prepends filler and inserts filler between the two names — but preserves the before/after-*and* fact — recovers 93% of the logit difference, whereas corrupting only *and* into *alongside* recovers 43% (other single-token replacements range 0.29 to 0.68). Splicing SAEs for layers 1–6 into the IOI task roughly preserves logit difference, while layer 0 and layers 7–11 do not, so no end-to-end feature circuit for the task is claimed.

## Limits and open problems they name
Only attention outputs are decomposed; the query–key circuits that produce the attention patterns are left unexplained. Most work is on the 100M-parameter model despite the 2B demonstration. Many conclusions rest on qualitative inspection and subjective judgment, with both false positives and false negatives expected in the interpretability ratings. All SAE results inherit the sparsity/linearity assumptions and the quality of the trained SAE, which the authors say is far from the sparsity–fidelity Pareto frontier. Head interpretation via top-ten attributed features cannot detect a head's role inside attention-head superposition, and they note their conclusion about polysemanticity is asymmetric: a stray unrelated feature falsifies monosemanticity, whereas a clean list is only weak evidence for it. RDFA cannot pass backwards through MLPs because of the nonlinearity.

## What we take from it
Several reusable items. (1) A *consistency check between two attribution instruments*: weight slices and DFA are computed from disjoint information (decoder norms versus forward-pass activations), so agreement is evidence and disagreement is a diagnostic — a cheap pattern for our probe batteries. (2) The *preserve-one-property noising distribution* as the falsifier for an interpretation: corrupt three things at once but hold the hypothesised signal fixed, and compare against corrupting only that signal — this is a much sharper test than a single ablation and is directly implementable for us. (3) The habit of motivating a hypothesis from a dictionary and then confirming it without the dictionary (prefix-length curves, direct logit attribution, ablation), which is exactly the discipline our predictive model needs if a dictionary is ever an input to it. (4) A warning about selectivity: the same feature dictionary is used to find the causally relevant features *and* to explain them, and the layer-0 / late-layer failures show a dictionary can silently be sufficient on the distribution where it was evaluated and insufficient elsewhere; they report fidelity per site rather than once, and we should too.
