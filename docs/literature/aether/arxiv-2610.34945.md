# Relational Composition as Parallel Transport: What Per-Entity Frames Force in Attention

- **Key:** arxiv-2610.34945
- **Authors / organisation:** Aakash Sarkar; Ananya Mukherjee; Motoshige Sato — University of California, San Francisco; Brigham and Women's Hospital
- **Date:** 2026-10-26
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34945
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

A dot product compares two vectors in one coordinate system, and a freshly introduced entity — a name in a story, a variable in a program — does not come with one. The authors take that observation seriously as an *assumption*, not a conclusion: they grant every entity its own frame, declare a joint rotation of those frames a symmetry of the representation, and then ask what the symmetry forces on attention. Their motivation is a compositional memory that is provably right rather than approximately right: if relations are operators acting on entity states, the question becomes how much state width and what kind of output head are required for exact composition over arbitrarily long word lengths. The larger goal is to move from "this architecture can express composition" to "this task's algebra, this width and this decoder jointly determine whether the mechanism can be exact at all, and if not, what its accuracy ceiling is" — all computable from the target group before training. They also want the theory to touch learned models rather than only constructed ones, which is why the second half of the paper is about certificates for trained matrices.

## What they studied

Synthetic finite-group composition: a word over an alphabet of group generators, a state obtained by applying an orthogonal matrix per letter, and a readout that must return the group element the word denotes. Eight groups form a width ladder (cyclic, dihedral, and permutation groups up to the $S_5$ order-120 case, plus $Q_8$), and three product groups ($C_3 \times A_4$, $A_4 \times A_4$, $A_5 \times A_5$) are used for the cover experiments. Two parsed public relational benchmarks, StepGame and CLUTRR, supply real relational structure through a shared parsed interface. Units of analysis are whole models and their weight matrices, with the readout head treated as a separable object of study; side experiments cover stacked commutative layers, signature readouts, order-blind and causal transformers, and a knowledge-graph link-prediction diagnostic. The models are small and purpose-built (orthogonal recurrences, matrix scans, small JAX transformers of three layers and width 64); no large pretrained language model is examined.

## How they did it

The argument is a chain of invariant-theory results. Under independent per-entity frames, an invariant score can depend on two entities only through their norms and the transported pairing, and an equivariant message must lie in the span of the receiver's own vector and the transported sender vector (Theorem 1). They then bound where a transport can come from: a map computed only from the two endpoints' channel matrices factors through those matrices, so its rank is at most the number of channels — one vector per endpoint gives at most rank one and can never be an orthogonal map in dimension two or more, so relations must be *supplied* (Theorem 2). Standard attention is then located as the flat sector: one shared frame, every transport the identity, and the query/key and output/value products as ordinary endomorphisms of that single space. For the composition part they let letters act on a state by orthogonal matrices and ask for a readout that is exact on every word, proving a width law for convex-cell decoders (argmax, nearest-prototype, softmax-argmax) and then widening the allowed readout class to quadratic and to arbitrary functions, each of which gives a different minimum width. Below the minimum they prove accuracy ceilings tied to the largest quotient of the group the mechanism cannot see. Finally they train orthogonal recurrences at and above the predicted widths in a three-stage schedule (anchor on very short words, fit on lengths to ten, polish on a spread including length 40), extract candidate exact representations from the learned matrices by Procrustes-style fitting, snap them to exact algebra, and *verify* — a finite prototype certificate that bounds the number of letters for which the raw learned recurrence is guaranteed correct given its measured transition residual and the readout's robustness radius. On the benchmarks they compare a matched transformer against additive, general-linear, rotation and lattice transports on StepGame, and unconstrained versus orthogonal matrix scans on CLUTRR.

## What counts as an explanation here

An algebraic identification, presented as a theorem with an integer attached to it. The explanation of a mechanism is not a circuit diagram or a feature dictionary but a *classification*: the target task has a finite group, the mechanism has a width and a readout class, and the pair (width, readout) determines which of three things the mechanism can realize — a quotient of the group (below the faithful width), a covering of the group (a nonlinear or sign-blind readout at reduced width), or a faithful copy (convex-cell readout at the minimal faithful width). It is meant to be complete *within its model class* and predictive in a strong sense: ceilings and thresholds are computed from the Cayley table before any training, and the certificate turns a learned weight matrix into a statement about how many letters it is good for. The authors are explicit that this does not describe a transformer stack, and that a proof of existence is not a claim that gradient descent finds the construction.

## How they judged success

Four distinct criteria, kept apart deliberately: fit (≥ 0.99 accuracy at length ten), finite evaluation (exactly 1.000 on 2,000 fresh words at each length through 1,000), algebraic extraction (the snapped generators close to a group with a Latin-square multiplication table and a homomorphism defect near machine precision), and the raw-model certificate (a horizon in letters derived from the measured transition residual and the readout radius). Controls include a shared-frame versus independently-rotated-frames comparison of three score functions against a stated chance level, a frozen-state intervention that holds the dynamics fixed and refits only the head, a monotone-versus-sign-blind head comparison at the same width and the same frozen states, prospective pre-registration of seeds and pass criteria with byte-identical code for the confirmatory arm, and a knowledge-graph prediction that is registered and *reported as null*. Constructed witnesses (a hand-built cover of 72 or 288 states) are reported separately from learned runs so that representability is never confused with optimization.

## What they found

The forced form is tight and empirically visible: under independent rotations the fixed-frame bilinear score falls to 0.192 and an MLP score to 0.159 against a 0.167 chance level, while the transported score holds at 1.000. Widths follow the algebra rather than the group's size — $Q_8$ at order 8 needs four dimensions while $A_5$ at order 60 needs three — and at the minimal width every fitting seed (17 of 17) passes the length-1,000 evaluation, against 7 of 18 at twice the width, where the wider models alias and some fall through the abelian ceiling to chance. Certificate horizons for the raw recurrences are 71–276 letters (for $A_4$) and 100–222 (for $D_4$), one to two orders of magnitude before the first error observed on a 200,000-letter walk. The readout result is the sharp one: on the same 72 frozen states of a double cover, a fitted affine cross-entropy head sits at exactly $\log 36$ cross-entropy and predicts uniformly — near chance accuracy — while a quadratic and a squared-prototype head both reach 1.000 with loss at or below $10^{-5}$; and in training, sign-blind heads learned the four-dimensional cover of a group whose faithful width is five in 6 of 48 seeds while monotone heads at the same width learned it in 0 of 48, with monotone heads succeeding (13 of 36) only once given the fifth dimension. On benchmarks the picture splits by algebra: an additive composer beats a matched transformer on StepGame's commutative displacements (0.753 against 0.481) and isometry even helps a probe family there (0.73 to 0.82), but constraining CLUTRR's scans to be orthogonal drops out-of-distribution accuracy from 0.848 to 0.202 while they still fit their training data at about 0.98. The registered knowledge-graph prediction that a loop-residual norm would track link-prediction accuracy came back at +0.055 raw and −0.039 filtered, i.e. unsupported.

## Limits and open problems they name

The groups are small because finiteness is what makes the theorems exact, the integers computable and the certificates finite; continuous and large groups need different tools. Optimization success is rare in several cells (roughly one in 25 attempts for one group, one in 18 for another) and the theory does not predict when descent finds the construction. The attention-form heads were changed to a cosine parameterization *after* they failed to fit, so that arm is confirmatory for the variant but the variant was not pre-registered. The benchmarks use parsed relations, so the results are about composition given relations and not about text understanding, and the algebraic reading of CLUTRR's kinship rules is tentative — the paper states that it does not identify the relation monoid, only that isometries help one task and hurt the other. Distance to a transformer stack is stated as a proposition and not crossed. The certificate gives worst-case horizons for real arithmetic only and says nothing about floating-point execution. The carrier theorem leaves the natural next problem: with only partial anchors a transport is identified only on the anchored subspace, and inferring transports from incomplete relational evidence and propagating that uncertainty through attention is untouched.

## What we take from it

A framing and a warning, in that order. The framing is that **what a mechanism can represent is a joint property of its dynamics and its decoder**, not of the dynamics alone — this paper proves it and then demonstrates it on frozen states, which is the cleanest possible form of the claim. For our white-box model this forbids any statement of the shape "this layer represents the group element / the entity / the relation" that does not name the decoder that reads it, because the same 72 states support a uniform affine head and an exact quadratic one. The warning is sharper than the folder's previous ones and it has an integer attached: **below a task's algebraic threshold, behaviour is capped by the largest quotient the mechanism cannot see, and the cap is computable from the task before training.** That is the first instrument in this folder that predicts an accuracy ceiling from task structure rather than from a null model, and it carries its own discipline — the ceiling is a statement about a model class, and the paper is scrupulous that a constructed witness scoring 1.000 while the best learned run reaches 0.323 is not a contradiction but an optimization gap. The second reusable instrument is the **certificate as a distinct evidence category**: a fitted model, a snapped exact model, and a bounded horizon for the raw weights are three different facts, and reporting a horizon in letters beside a finite-length accuracy is the honest way to publish a composition result. Two things to steal directly: the frozen-state head intervention, which isolates decoder expressivity from representation learning by construction, and the shared-versus-independent-frame control for any score function we claim is relational.
