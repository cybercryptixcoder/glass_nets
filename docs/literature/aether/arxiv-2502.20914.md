# Everything, Everywhere, All at Once: Is Mechanistic Interpretability Identifiable?

- **Key:** arxiv-2502.20914
- **Authors / organisation:** Maxime Méloux, François Portet, Silviu Maniu, Maxime Peyrard — Université Grenoble Alpes, CNRS, Grenoble INP, LIG (Grenoble, France)
- **Date:** 2025-02-28
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.20914
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Mechanistic interpretability assumes, usually without saying so, that a fixed behaviour has one explanation. This paper asks whether the criteria the field uses to *validate* an explanation actually pick out a single one, borrowing the notion of identifiability from statistics: a parameter is identifiable if the data plus the modelling assumptions determine it uniquely. Replace parameters with explanations and assumptions with validity criteria, and the question becomes whether two incompatible explanations can both satisfy the same criterion for the same behaviour. The authors call an explanation a *computational abstraction*, a pair consisting of the algorithm (the "what") and the mapping from that algorithm's variables onto regions of the network's activations (the "where"), and they stress-test uniqueness of both halves. The larger aim is to give the field a standard for explanation that is stated rather than assumed, either a pragmatic one (prediction and controllability) or a stricter one (real identifiability), and to point at what each would cost.

## What they studied
Tiny systems, chosen so that candidate explanations can be enumerated exhaustively rather than searched for with heuristics: multi-layer perceptrons of shape (2, k, k, n) trained on Boolean inputs with added Gaussian noise to implement n two-input logic gates, for k from 2 to 5, up to six gates in the multi-task setting, a run through 100 random networks per condition. One worked illustration is a 3-hidden-layer-wide MLP computing XOR; one scale-probe is a (784, 128, 128, 3, 3, 3, 1) MLP trained on MNIST restricted to digits 0 and 1. The unit of analysis is the circuit, i.e. a subgraph of neurons and the edges between them, together with a per-neuron assignment of a high-level truth value; the explanatory algorithms are Boolean circuits built from AND, OR, IMP and their negations. No transformer and no language model appears anywhere.

## How they did it
They test the field's two strategy families separately, so that each criterion is measured against its own search procedure. Under *where-then-what* (circuits first, interpretation second) they enumerate circuits, run validation data through each circuit in isolation with everything outside it deleted, and call a circuit *perfect* when its accuracy equals the model's (circuit error zero); for every perfect circuit they then grow a tree of consistent logic-gate interpretations neuron by neuron, layer by layer, recursing on the parents' already-assigned labels, merging inputs that cannot be separated, and killing branches that cannot be labelled; relabelling 0↔1 is counted as a duplicate and, by convention, 1 goes to larger activation intervals. Under *what-then-where* (algorithm first, alignment second) they enumerate XOR-equivalent formulas as parse trees up to depth 3, giving 56 distinct formulas, then enumerate neuron subsets and mappings onto each formula's intermediate variables and score each mapping with intervention interchange accuracy (IIA), calling a mapping perfect when IIA is 1 and minimal when no perfect mapping has a strictly larger image. Two controlled knobs are then turned on the setup: architecture width and number of gates learned in parallel; three further appendices vary the training-loss cutoff, input noise, and the training distribution's skew. Finally, they take the scale objection head-on by extracting the last three layers of the MNIST MLP into a sub-network, feeding it the inner activations of the full model as a fresh dataset, and running the circuit enumeration there — the argument being that any circuit valid in the second half with a valid continuation in the first half is a valid circuit of the whole.

## What counts as an explanation here
A computational abstraction: a circuit plus a consistent mapping to a Boolean algorithm, with consistency defined as a commutation requirement between the network's computation and the algorithm's, checked either by diagram-commutation on the mapping or by IIA on interventions. The object is deliberately complete within the toy regime, since enumeration leaves nothing out, and it is predictive in the narrow sense that a perfect circuit reproduces the model's output and a perfect mapping reproduces its counterfactual behaviour. What the paper shows is that the criterion is satisfiable many times over, so an "explanation" of this form is not an identity but a member of a very large set of mutually incompatible alternatives. The authors are explicit that the count they report is a lower bound: sparsity below 0.3 is not searched, and interpretations that map one high-level gate onto several neurons are excluded.

## How they judged success
The criteria are the field's own, formalised: circuit error (1 minus accuracy of the subgraph standing alone, i.e. a zero-error or "perfect" circuit) for the where, consistent mappings of high-level features onto activation regions for the interpretation, and IIA for causal alignment between an algorithm's variables and the network's states. Identifiability is then success of a different kind — the number of distinct explanations each criterion admits. Variation across seeds, architecture width, gate count, loss cutoff (10⁻¹ down to 10⁻⁶), input noise (Gaussian with standard deviation 0.1 at training) and distribution skew (joint entropy 0.8 to 2.0 bits, 100 runs) is summarised with two-sample t-tests, Welch's t-test, and a linear regression of counts on entropy, with p-values printed in two tables. There is no benchmark, no baseline method and no human rating: the comparison is internal, between the count of explanations and the number one.

## What they found
Non-identifiability at all four stages, in numbers large enough that the ordering is not in doubt. In the XOR illustration, 85 distinct circuits replicate the model perfectly, averaging 535.8 consistent interpretations each, while two candidate algorithms grounded in the same network yield 159 minimal perfect mappings between them — about 45,700 abstractions in one small network, most of them incompatible. Across the width sweep the median count of interpretations per network rises from 38 at width 2 to 910,000 at width 5, and of perfect minimal mappings from 8 to 3,700; fewer than 2% of trained networks admit exactly one valid minimal mapping, and no network admits exactly one circuit interpretation. Learning several gates in parallel reduces the counts significantly up to four tasks (p = 0.05) and not beyond. Lower loss cutoffs reduce algorithm counts only below 10⁻⁵ and leave mappings-per-algorithm unchanged; scattered cases exist where an untrained, randomly initialised network already implements a gate and still admits multiple abstractions. Noise at training leaves the algorithm-first counts statistically unchanged while halving circuits (52.3 to 28.6) and multiplying interpretations per circuit (2,270 to 16,400). A skewed training distribution does the same in the other direction on circuit counts; every one of these is reported with its p-value. The MNIST probe survives the scale objection in the form the authors only claim for it: 3,209 valid circuits in the extracted second half, so the whole network has at least that many.

## Limits and open problems they name
Everything is tiny: MLPs on 2-input Boolean functions and a two-digit MNIST regression, not transformers trained on text, and the authors concede that the gap between regimes is exactly the thing their experiments cannot cross — they turn it into a demand that anyone claiming the problem vanishes at scale should show why. The enumeration is a lower bound throughout (sparsity floor, no multi-neuron features, no relabelling counted). Their own positive proposal is thin: they suggest requiring stricter causal abstraction, where every low-level state must be accounted for and exclusions justified causally, and multi-criterion validation in the style of the inner-interpretability framework, but they do not implement either. Sparsity as a tie-break is raised and immediately rejected on the evidence — one extra node should not disqualify an explanation. They also raise the possibility that MI is underdetermined for complexity-theoretic reasons and cannot be made identifiable at all.

## What we take from it
A measure, and the sharpest one in the folder: *count the explanations your criterion admits* on a system small enough to enumerate, and publish that number beside the criterion. It converts "our circuit explains the behaviour" into "our criterion leaves k incompatible circuits on this task and the ones below are two of them" — a claim that can be checked in a day and that our own report tables should carry for whatever toy intervention we run. A warning that costs nothing to heed: the causal criterion (IIA = 1) is not stronger than the descriptive one on this axis, and a perfect alignment score is as compatible with many locations as with one, so causally "verified" is not "identified". A framing we can adopt wholesale: the pair (algorithm, mapping) is exactly the shape of our white-box model, and the identifiability question is the honest version of "does the model determine the account". And a pragmatic escape hatch stated in the paper's own terms: an account need not be unique if it is predictive and manipulable, so a stack of incompatible accounts is not a failure provided the ones we ship predict and steer; the failure is claiming uniqueness without the count.
