# Explanation Multiplicity: Circuit-Level Interpretability Evidence Does Not Survive Defensible Analytic Variation

- **Key:** arxiv-2608.13754
- **Authors / organisation:** Ajay Pravin Mahale (single author; no institutional affiliation given on the paper)
- **Date:** 2026-08-13 (v1, cs.AI; manuscript dated 2026-08-12; CC BY 4.0)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.13754
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (arXiv HTML v1, ~34k extracted characters read end to end — abstract, §1–§11, all seven tables, full reference list; the page carries no appendices. Figures not inspected.)
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
If a company files a circuit-level account of why its model behaved as it did, the filing is worth something only if a second competent analyst, given the same model and the same tool but allowed their own defensible settings, would file something compatible. The author takes the EU AI Act's technical-documentation and explanation duties as the concrete audience and asks the auditability question mechanically: how much does the *statement* derived from a discovered circuit move across the space of analytic choices that a published implementation would license? The framing is imported from the psychology literature on multiverse analysis and specification curves, whose point is that undisclosed flexibility lets an analyst present almost any conclusion as the finding. The paper is careful to say it is not proposing a better discovery method and not arguing interpretability is worthless; it is measuring the evidentiary weight of what the field currently produces. The larger goal is a decision rule a standards body could actually apply.

## What they studied
One model, one task: GPT-2 small on indirect object identification, with the factorised computational graph giving 32,491 edges over 144 attention heads and 12 MLPs. The unit of analysis is not the neuron or the edge but the **specification** — a tuple of eight analytic choices (discovery objective, ablation operator, corruption distribution, evaluation metric, size threshold, prompt variant, seed, granularity) — and the object being measured is the derived claim about the circuit, mapped to a structured Annex IV statement. Seven axes are crossed from published implementations only: seven objectives, seven ablation operators, four corruption distributions nested inside the five operators that read them, four metrics, three thresholds, two prompt variants, five seeds.

## How they did it
The analysis plan was committed and tagged before the confirmatory sweep, with both abstracts drafted in advance so neither direction could be written up as a surprise; later deviations went into an append-only dated file. Discovery uses the edge-attribution-patching family with integrated edge gradients as implemented in the library released with the faithfulness-metric robustness work, and the four corruption levels are taken from the original IOI circuit paper. Each discovered circuit is pushed through a **deterministic, published claim map** — deliberately not a language model, on the grounds that model variance would be inseparable from circuit variance — which keys an overseer addressee on the dominant layer band, then size class, then ranked input segment, and an affected-person addressee on the input segment first. Two exclusions were fixed before any circuit was seen: one discovery objective raised a runtime error in all 220 of its cells (a mean squared error computed against an integral target, inside the library's own code), verified by digest to be exactly that arm, and left unrepaired because the library is the instrument under test; and the metric-relative threshold rule admitted no circuit for 52.3% of specifications. The realised grid is six objectives, 1,320 cells, 15,840 specifications, of which 7,561 produced a claim and 3,218 distinct circuits. No p-value is computed across specifications — the author treats the grid as a designed multiverse rather than an independent sample — and uncertainty comes from a nonparametric bootstrap that resamples specifications rather than pairs, because the instability statistics are U-statistics over dependent pairs. Claims are measured as a **flip rate**: the unbiased Gini–Simpson probability that two randomly drawn specifications yield different statements, with a within-group form that holds named axes fixed.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **structured regulatory statement** — a claim class over layer band and size class at one of three nested granularities — derived from an edge set by a fixed mapping, plus the distribution of such statements over the analytic space. It is explicitly not a prediction of behaviour and not a mechanism; it is the artifact a filing would contain. The paper's object is therefore *multiplicity*: the claim is treated as a random draw from a specification space, and understanding means knowing the whole distribution rather than the modal representative. Partial by construction — nothing is asserted about whether any of the circuits is the mechanism, only about whether two analysts would agree about it.

## How they judged success
Pre-registered decision rules with fixed thresholds, each reported as confirmed/rejected/supported against its result: mean Jaccard similarity reported as a premise rather than tested; flip rate above 0.20 with the interval's lower bound required to clear it; failure to separate from a size-matched random null (R = 1,000 null multiverses drawn from the full edge population); and a gap between claim flip rate and functional disagreement above 0.10. The headline acceptance test is a **filability criterion**, π* ≥ 1 − α, where π* is the modal claim share, evaluated at three tolerances (0.95, 0.90, 0.80). Robustness comes from standardising each of the seven axes in turn and recomputing the *residual* within-group flip rate, from alternative size binnings returned by a committed calibration rule plus eight hand-varied ones, and from a REML variance decomposition on log circuit size. A verification script recomputes 36 headline quantities from stored results (0 failures) and a second script checks every numeric token in the manuscript against the stored results.

## What they found
Mean pairwise Jaccard similarity is 0.1396 (CI 0.1377–0.1418, computed exactly over all 28.6 million pairs), the median pair sharing 4% of its edges; about 45× chance at the median circuit size of 200 edges, but the author notes a 500-edge circuit has a chance overlap of 0.008, so beating chance is not much. The claim flips across 73.2% of specification pairs (CI 0.7247–0.7380) against a pre-registered bar of 0.20, with the modal share at 41.1% — far below every tolerance, so the evidence is unfilable at all three. The two most common claims, 41.1% and 28.5% of the space, attribute the same behaviour to early layers and to late layers respectively: they contradict rather than emphasise differently. Standardising the evaluation metric — the largest single lever, worth 0.1377 of the 0.7316 — still leaves 0.5939 (CI 0.5803–0.6063); the ablation operator, objective, threshold, corruption, prompt variant and seed each remove between 0.0302 and 0.0009. Stripping size from the claim entirely and holding size fixed leaves 0.2706 (CI 0.2550–0.2859), still above threshold. All six addressee × granularity cells fail filability, the coarsest two-class statement included, at a 39.5% flip rate. The random-null comparison inverts under size control: pooled, discovered circuits look *less* stable (0.7316 against 0.6774), but within size they are more stable (0.2746 against 0.4230), the null going degenerate at and above 1,000 edges; the discovered multiverse also collapses to one claim class at 5,000 and 10,000 edges. Per-example functional agreement is 0.6028, but Cohen's κ is 0.0146, so the raw agreement reflects circuits of similar accuracy judged on different examples rather than interchangeable mechanisms; the pre-registered claim-versus-function gap of 0.3344 nevertheless fails at 6 of 10 sizes and is negative at 5. A REML decomposition attributes 78.8% of structural variance to the ablation–corruption interaction and only 0.7% to the metric — so a standards body reading the structural decomposition would standardise the wrong axis.

## Limits and open problems they name
One model and one task, which the author calls the limitation most likely to change the number; a Pythia-160m replication grid (924 cells) and a second task are pre-registered and unrun. Circuit size is an outcome of the threshold rule, not an axis, and it distorted three separate pooled quantities — the flip rate itself, the direction of the null comparison, and the sign of the functional gap — so fixing it changes more than standardising any axis. The multiverse is as wide as one library rather than the literature (optimal ablation is a defensible operator that was never in the space, making the measured rate a lower bound). The discard rate is high and unevenly distributed across metrics (0.3% for comprehensiveness against 77.9% for sufficiency), leaving a metric-imbalanced pool. The bootstrap is invalid for one family of conditioning groups where the groups hold only two to five specifications, and those quantities are reported as point estimates rather than repaired after seeing the intervals. A registered ceiling is stated plainly: with nine claim classes a flip rate cannot exceed 0.8889, so 0.7316 is 82.3% of its maximum rather than trivially close to one.

## What we take from it
A **measure** and a **protocol we should adopt directly**. The measure is multiplicity of the *claim* rather than of the circuit: report, for any explanation our white-box model is asked to justify, the share of the defensible specification space that agrees with it. The protocol is §8, five steps — declare the analytic space with a citation per level, publish a deterministic claim map as code, compute the modal share and flip rate, apply π* ≥ 1 − α, and report the per-level discard rate together with a within-group decomposition of every pooled statistic. Second, a hard warning about **pooled statistics over heterogeneous objects**: three of this paper's own pooled numbers invert under the size control, so any aggregate we publish over components or circuits of differing sizes ships its within-size decomposition beside it. Third, a cheap negative-control idea worth copying: a size-matched random null built as a whole null *multiverse* (R = 1,000 multiverses) rather than a single null, because a null run through the same analytic grid is the only fair comparator. Fourth, a diagnostic for the phantom-specialisation objection — raw per-example agreement beside Cohen's κ — which separates "two circuits are right about the same thing" from "two circuits are right about the same fraction of things"; every cross-circuit agreement claim in this folder should print both. And a supporting datum for the folder's tooling caution: one of seven publicly exported discovery objectives does not execute on its own canonical task.
