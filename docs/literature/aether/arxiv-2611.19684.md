# Early Readability of Trajectory Quality in Diffusion Driving Planners

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.19684
- **Authors / organisation:** Xiang Zhang — Louisiana State University; Ziyang Leng, Yuxin Bao, Zhihao Zhao, Bolei Zhou — University of California, Los Angeles; Xugui Zhou — Louisiana State University
- **Date:** 2026-11-15
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.19684
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Diffusion and flow-matching planners emit several candidate trajectories per scene, and the candidates differ in quality because the noise draws differ; picking the good one therefore matters as much as generating it. Existing selection scores candidates only after every branch has been fully denoised, which costs the full compute of all candidates even though one is driven. The authors ask whether the *final* quality of a candidate is already readable from the planner's internal state part-way through denoising, so that the remaining branches can be pruned. The larger goal is the one our own project cares about — turning an internal state into a prediction of an outcome the model has not yet produced — with the added twist that here the outcome is defined by an external metric, not by the model's own logits. Their contribution is a systematic probing study plus a deployment of the finding as an inference-time speedup.

## What they studied
Three pretrained, frozen planners: Alpamayo 1.5 (an action expert inside a vision-language-action model, Euler flow matching, 10 function evaluations, 36 expert layers, 1- and 4-camera variants), ReCogDrive-IL (stochastic DDIM, 5 evaluations) and HDP (DPM-Solver++, 11 evaluations). Data: Alpamayo is probed on 2,418 training scene-times from 2,268 clips (a 50/50 split of ordinary driving and event-selected out-of-distribution samples) and 498 test scene-times from 465 clips; the other two on NAVSIM, 103,288 navtrain scenes and 12,146 navtest scenes. Quality is ADE over predicted versus recorded waypoints for Alpamayo (64 waypoints / 6.4 s) and the PDM composite score for NAVSIM (8 waypoints / 4 s), where lower ADE and higher PDM are better. Unit of analysis: the **action-token activations of one denoiser evaluation**, aggregated across layers and tokens into one feature vector per candidate (73,728 dimensions for Alpamayo, 4,096 and 8,192 for the two NAVSIM models).

## How they did it
Every candidate in a scene is completed, and its final metric is recorded; that number is the fixed label a readout tries to predict from an earlier state. Features are captured during the denoiser evaluation itself, not from the sampler state that follows it or from the decoded trajectory preview. Within each scene the feature vector has the candidate-set mean subtracted and is then standardised with training statistics; the target is the within-scene-centred metric, so the reader is always asked which candidate in this scene is better, never whether a scene is easy. Ridge regression is the main reader, with the penalty chosen by cross-validation grouped by clip or driving log, and separate readers are fitted per planner, representation and evaluation index. Probes are then swept along two axes — the denoising evaluation index and the network layer — and against alternative representations (initial noise, completed-trajectory coordinates, token averaging versus temporal binning versus retaining all token coordinates, all-layer concatenation). Two evaluation quantities are introduced and kept separate: **pairwise ranking accuracy** over eligible same-scene pairs (ties in the metric dropped, tied scores given half credit, chance 50%) and **selection quality**, the completed metric of the top-ranked candidate, reported against uniform random selection and an oracle. Reader capacity is varied with three-layer and two-hidden-layer MLPs against ridge, and the control set is a label shuffle among candidates within each training scene, random projection of the 73,728-dim features to 2,048, and fitting on 5% of the training data. Two privileged references bound the exercise: a "preview" selector that uses the ground-truth quality of the first-step decoded trajectory, and an oracle over fully denoised candidates. Deployment is early top-1 selection — rank after the first evaluation, discard the rest, finish only the winner — scored in open loop (ADE and PDM), in compute (function evaluations and measured latency on an NVIDIA DGX Spark), and in closed loop on NavSafe-3 (280 scenarios) for ReCogDrive-IL and AlpaSim (100 scenes, three runs per arm) for Alpamayo. Intervals are 95% paired bootstrap resamples over evaluated scene-times.

## What counts as an explanation here
A linear readout — a ridge probe with 73,728 weights (or 4,096 / 8,192) fitted to a frozen planner, mapping an intermediate activation to a scalar prediction of a final external metric. It is deliberately partial and local: it claims only that final quality is linearly accessible from that state, and the authors are explicit that this is *not* a claim that the planner estimates or uses the quantity. It is predictive in a literal sense — it ranks unseen candidates in unseen scenes — but predicts a metric rather than the model's own output, and the paper offers no account of the mechanism by which the state encodes it.

## How they judged success
Four measures in increasing strictness: (i) pairwise ranking accuracy on held-out scenes; (ii) mean selected-candidate metric on held-out scenes against random selection, consensus selection (the candidate nearest the mean completed trajectory), KDPE, and readers of initial noise and of trajectory coordinates; (iii) compute and measured latency of early versus full selection; (iv) closed-loop driving scores and category-level success rates. Controls: label shuffling within scene, random projection, 5% training subsets, MLP-versus-ridge capacity comparison across five seeds, MLP readers given 2.2–18.9 M parameters against ridges of 4,096–73,728 weights, and 95% paired-bootstrap intervals with a no-detectable-difference reading when they span zero. Privileged bounds (first-step preview, final oracle) fix the ceiling.

## What they found
Readout is available almost immediately: first-evaluation states support selection in all four model configurations, with Alpamayo's ADE falling 17.8% (1cam) and 13.4% (4cam) below random, and PDM rising 1.03 and 0.50 points on ReCogDrive-IL and HDP. On Alpamayo the first-evaluation readers rank 64.7% (1cam) and 59.2% (4cam) of candidate pairs correctly while a ridge reader of the *initial noise* is near chance, so the gain appears between the noise and the state after one evaluation rather than at initialisation. Waiting until the final evaluation is not worth it for the deterministic samplers — no detectable change for Alpamayo 1cam and better at first evaluation for 4cam — and only ReCogDrive-IL gains, by 0.36 PDM, under stochastic sampling, a gain that falls to 0.06 once later noise injection is disabled (though the sampler change also changes the candidate pool, so the mechanism is not established). Deep layers beat shallow ones consistently but the best layer moves with the evaluation index, which is why the main selector concatenates all 36 layer summaries. **Ranking and selection come apart**: the NAVSIM readers gain on random selection while ranking only 48.7% and 55.6% of eligible pairs (on 945,782 and 930,398 pairs), and on Alpamayo pairwise accuracy sometimes rises at an intermediate evaluation with no matching improvement in mean selected quality. Capacity matters jointly with representation: on state features MLPs never beat ridge, while on initial noise and on completed trajectories MLPs recover selection that ridge cannot (trajectory-MLP ADE 3.224 / 1.657 against random 3.758 / 1.983). The first-evaluation state reader beats the privileged first-step preview selector in three of four configurations (1cam 3.222 versus 3.409; HDP 88.96 versus 88.75), so preview quality is not a good proxy for final quality. Controls behave: shuffled labels remove the gain and put selection below random, a random projection to 2,048 dimensions is indistinguishable from the full feature (18.8 / 12.9 against 17.8 / 13.4), and 5% of the training data still yields useful selection (8.1 / 7.1). Cost: early selection at eight candidates uses 17 function evaluations against 80 for consensus, measured total latency drops 4.09 → 2.30 s (1cam) and 8.24 → 4.04 s (4cam), i.e. 44% and 51% of total and 75–80% of denoising time, with the shared VLM pass (about 1.7 and 2.9 s) capping the end-to-end win. Closed loop is mixed and stated as such: NavSafe-3 driving score 49.90 → 50.35 and success 29.40% → 31.07% for ReCogDrive-IL; AlpaSim gated-score gains of 0.0516 and 0.0317 and lower off-road and hard-failure rates, but slightly *higher* collision rates.

## Limits and open problems they name
The probes show accessibility, not use: nothing here implies the planner itself represents or exploits trajectory quality. Findings are tied to the tested representations and readers, and the best selection point is planner- and sampler-specific, with readers fitted per planner and per metric and no transfer tested across models or to unseen distributions. Closed-loop validation is simulation only, and higher mean scores and progress are explicitly not consistent safety gains — the collision-rate increase is reported rather than explained. One internal statistics note worth carrying: the paper's stated Alpamayo pair count (59,760) is inconsistent with its own configuration (498 scenes × C(16,2) pairs), which suggests either deduplicated or differently pooled pairs; no conclusion rests on it, but it is a reminder to recompute pair counts from the scene count.

## What we take from it
A **measure and a framing**, both reusable. The measure is the separation of *ranking* from *selection*: pairwise ordering over all eligible pairs and the quality of the chosen item are different quantities, and here a reader with 48.7% pairwise accuracy — statistically indistinguishable from a coin — still beats random selection by a point of PDM, because pairwise accuracy weights a swap between near-equal candidates the same as a swap between the best and the worst. Any probe table we publish that reports one of these should report the other, since our AUROC is a ranking statistic being used to justify a decision. The framing is the *centring trick*: subtracting the candidate-set mean from both features and target makes the reader a within-context comparison and turns the task from "predict the metric" into "predict which option is better here", which is exactly the estimand our intervention work needs and which we have never set up explicitly. Three supporting habits: the **privileged-preview bound** (a selector allowed to read ground-truth quality of the first-step decode) is a cheaper and more informative ceiling than an oracle, and it is beatable — its failure here says quality is not monotone as denoising proceeds, so "the model already knows, it just hasn't finished" is not safe; the **initial-noise control** separates "predictable from anything" from "predictable because the model has done some work"; and the **5%-data arm** is the cheapest way to show a readout is not memorising, costing a few minutes rather than an ablation. Finally the practical shape: read early, prune, complete the winner — a form of intervention that changes cost rather than behaviour, and therefore one where the claim is testable without arguing about what the model "uses".
