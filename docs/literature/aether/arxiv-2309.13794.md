## What they wanted, and why

Randomized smoothing is treated in this sub-head as the state of the art for provable robustness, and the authors open by naming its structural weakness: for a fixed noise scale the certified ℓ2 radius is bounded by a constant that does not grow with the input dimension, so the *volume* of the certified ball shrinks factorially as the dimension rises. Anisotropic and input-dependent smoothing schemes inherit the same curse. Their motivating observation is different in kind from the rest of the sub-head: empirical literature already reports that pre-composing a classifier with a dimensionality reduction (PCA, autoencoders) improves robustness, and the manifold hypothesis says the useful perturbation directions are the ones normal to the data manifold. The goal is therefore not a tighter bound for the same certified set but a *differently shaped* certified set — one that is large in the directions the data does not use. Projection heuristics existed; certification of them did not, so this is an attempt to buy back the empirical gain with a proof attached.

## What they studied

Image classifiers on CIFAR-10 and SVHN, with a Wide ResNet (taken from Yang et al.'s "all shapes and sizes" setting) as the base classifier and Gaussian augmentation at σ = 0.15 during training. The unit of analysis is the **certified region itself**, a subset of input space, measured by its d-dimensional volume rather than by a radius. The object being certified is the smoothed map g the authors build: project onto p principal components of the training data, reconstruct, smooth with Gaussian noise *in the p-dimensional space*, and classify. No neuron, head, direction or weight inside the network is examined anywhere in the paper.

## How they did it

The construction is a three-map composition. A semi-orthogonal U ∈ R^{d×p} (its columns are the top p principal components of a random subset of the training data) defines a projection to R^p and a lossy reconstruction back to R^d; the classifier is f_θ applied after reconstruction. Smoothing is then applied to the reconstructed classifier in the *low* space, so the noisy ball is p-dimensional while the input is d-dimensional. Two propositions give the geometry: if the low-dimensional smoothed classifier is certified with radius R around Uᵀx, then the high-dimensional classifier is unchanged for every perturbation whose projection has norm at most R — and that set is a p-dimensional ball *extruded along the entire nullspace of the projection*, i.e. a hypercylinder. Since a nullspace is unbounded, the hypercylinder's volume is infinite, so the authors clip it to the unit cube of feasible inputs and bound that intersection. The bound comes from a Vaaler cube-subspace inequality (any k-dimensional subspace through the cube has k-volume at least one), extended by the authors to scaled cubes and to affine subspaces that miss the origin via a quantity t, the minimal ℓ∞ norm of a point on the affine subspace. The resulting lower bound has the shape (volume of a p-ball of radius R) times (1 − 2R − 2t)^{d−p}. Because that expression is not monotone in R, they add a closed-form optimal radius r* = min{R, p(1−2t)/(2d)} — deliberately *certifying less* in the projected space in order to certify more in the input space. Algorithm 1 packages this: run standard smoothing certification in the projected space, abstain if it abstains, solve an ℓ∞-regression (as a linear program) for t, clamp R, and report the volume bound. For the empirical side they build a **SubspacePGD** attack confined to the span of low-variance principal components and compare it against full PGD and two random-noise baselines; the projection for the attack is chosen to cover 99% of variance on CIFAR-10 and 95% on SVHN, with fewer components used in the defence itself. The volume comparison runs against isotropic ℓ2 smoothing, the ℓ1 and ℓ∞ smoothing of Yang et al., and the anisotropic ANCER ellipsoids, the last run *without* its memory module so that its certificates are optimistic — an explicitly stated upper bound for that baseline.

## What counts as an explanation here

A certified region: a geometric object in input space plus a proved lower bound on its volume. The proof is about the projection algebra and about convex geometry, never about the classifier's internals — the network enters only through the two class probabilities that standard smoothing certification consumes. It is partial in the ordinary certifying sense (a lower bound on the region, hence an existence statement, not a characterisation of the true robust set), and it is unusual in the sub-head for being *non-monotone in its own certificate*: the reported quantity is a volume computed at a radius the authors choose to shrink. The one thing the object does predict is directional: perturbations lying entirely in the nullspace of the projection are certified at arbitrary magnitude, so the guarantee says the classifier is provably insensitive to perturbations in the discarded directions, with no bound at all on the size of the perturbation.

## How they judged success

Clean accuracy and **median** log10 certified volume over correctly classified test points (median rather than mean, stated as being because the volumes are log-scaled), at a range of certification confidence levels α. Baselines: RS (isotropic ℓ2), RS4A-ℓ1, RS4A-ℓ∞, and ANCER. Controls: the SubspacePGD attack compared against full PGD and against RandMax / RandUniform random perturbations at matched ε, with the point being that a random perturbation of the same ℓ∞ size is not an attack. An ablation on the projected dimension p (100 through 1000 on CIFAR-10, 60 through 450 on SVHN) is shown as a family of curves rather than a single setting. There is no statistical test across seeds, no abstention rate, and — the notable absence — no comparison against isotropic smoothing's *ℓ2 radius* at matched clean accuracy, only against its volume.

## What they found

Median certified volume on CIFAR-10: ProjectedRS −3175 (log10) against isotropic RS −4377, ANCER −3881, RS4A-ℓ1 −9573 and RS4A-ℓ∞ −6102 — roughly 1200 orders of magnitude over the standard baseline on the log scale as printed, which the authors characterise as 706 and 2453 orders of magnitude over RS and ANCER respectively once the volume scaling constant is accounted for. On SVHN: ProjectedRS −1578 against RS −4280, ANCER −4031, RS4A-ℓ1 −9573, RS4A-ℓ∞ −6171. Clean accuracy is *lower* than the baselines in both tables — 85.8% against 87.8% (RS) and 87.4% (ANCER) on CIFAR-10, and 91.4% against 92.6% (RS) on SVHN — so the volume gain is bought with about two points of accuracy, which the authors state as "only a 2% drop". The SubspacePGD attack reaches a success rate comparable to full PGD on the undefended classifier with roughly a fourfold larger ℓ∞ budget, and the perturbed images are said to be visibly less distorted than PGD's at the same magnitude. The asymptotic argument is that the volume bound's factorial denominator is indexed by p while the standard one's is indexed by d, which is why the advantage should grow with input dimension — offered as a reason to expect even larger gains on ImageNet, which they could not run.

## Limits and open problems they name

The volume lower bound is expensive: the ℓ∞-regression has variables and constraints scaling with d − p, taking about 16 s per point on CIFAR-10 and 19 s on SVHN, and on ImageNet-scale inputs the program cannot be held in memory on their hardware — so the regime where the method should look best is exactly the regime they cannot certify in. The bound is also weak in the direction that matters for the comparison: it is a *lower* bound on a quantity that is infinite before clipping, and the clipping is to the unit cube, which makes the reported number a function of the cube's side as much as of the classifier. Certification of the projected classifier inherits smoothing's Monte-Carlo abstention, and no abstention rate is reported. They note the theory extends to other certificates in the projected space (e.g. ℓ1) but do not run them. The future work they name is more sophisticated dimension reduction that keeps the guarantee.

## What we take from it

A method and a framing. The framing is the transferable part: **change the shape of the object being certified to match an assumed structure of the data, and the certificate's asymptotics change with it.** Their certified region is the preimage of a low-dimensional ball, so it inherits the low dimension in its radial directions and the full dimension in its null directions — a shape that makes a guarantee statement about *which* directions the model is provably blind to, rather than how far it is safe. For our white-box model this is a usable template for how to state directional robustness results about a probe: the natural object is a set with stated geometry, not a scalar radius, and the honest way to compare it against a radius is by volume inside a stated box. Warnings travel too. First, the comparison ladder here is by volume on a log axis, which turns any constant-factor difference into a number in the hundreds — the entry keeps the printed values and the "orders of magnitude" claim side by side precisely because a log10 median cannot distinguish 700 from 1200 orders without the scaling constant, which they supply only in a figure caption. Second, one baseline is deliberately weakened (ANCER's memory module removed) and one competitor's cited invalidity (ANCER's local certificates) is the reason given for the choice — legitimate, but it means the headline lead is over an upper bound of a broken method. Third, a non-monotone bound invites a hyperparameter: r* is chosen to maximise the reported number, so the reported number is a best-case over radii the method itself selects, and no ablation shows the effect of that choice on the accuracy column.
