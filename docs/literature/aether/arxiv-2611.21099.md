# ForceHarness: Turning Force into Action and Physics for Frozen Frontier Models

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.21099
- **Authors / organisation:** Ke Sun, Kevin Yuchen Ma, Yike Zhang, Yanzhe Chen, Mike Zheng Shou — Show Lab, National University of Singapore
- **Date:** 2026-11-16
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21099
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Frontier multimodal models can now drive a robot zero-shot from images, but they sit behind an API with frozen weights, and a large class of manipulation tasks is decided by contact force that no camera can show — seating a plug, driving a screw, telling a heavy bottle from a light one. Learned force policies solve these tasks but need demonstrations and weight updates, so they are unavailable for a model that can only be called. The question is therefore narrower than "can a model understand force": can a harness around a frozen model regulate contact beneath it, and hand it force back in a form it can actually reason about? The paper's answer is a three-layer harness plus a benchmark with deliberately hidden physical variables.

## What they studied
A frozen frontier model (GPT-6 Astra, GPT-6.1 Sol, Claude Fable 5.2, and five smaller GPT-5.x models) as the only decision-maker, wrapped around a Franka FR3 in the real world (no wrist force/torque sensor — force from joint torques), the public eight-task ForceBench suite in Isaac Lab, and a new seven-task MuJoCo suite (HiddenPhys: Weight, CoM, Wipe, Stack, Peg, Latch, Lock). The unit of analysis is a **tool result**: the text a frozen model reads after each primitive call. The models are never trained; the harness layers are hand-specified.

## How they did it
Three layers sit between the model and the arm. Bottom: force sensing and calibration — estimate the hand wrench by least squares from joint torques through the flange Jacobian, then defeat slow bias by **controlling when it reads rather than how it filters**: tare at rest before each empty-hand primitive, let the arm settle and average a short still window after each one, and report force *changes* relative to a same-episode reference instead of absolute grams. Middle: twelve self-terminating force primitives in five groups (make contact, hold force, find and seat, turn under load, measure), plus ten non-force tools. Each primitive applies force by letting a target pose lead the measured pose, with the lead clamped so the buildable force is bounded by the stock impedance controller; an admittance law integrates the force error into target velocity, and the primitive ends on a **contact event**, a force cap or a time limit rather than on a clock. Top: a physics translation layer whose estimators are deliberately simple and fixed (averages, least-squares slopes, propagated standard errors) that converts each trace into the physical quantities a person would read off it — payload mass, contact height, stiffness, friction, insertion advance, resisting torque, side load — each with an uncertainty and status flags, and **no task thresholds**, so the harness states how heavy a load is but never whether that is enough. The evaluation pairs every condition with a no-force control that keeps the same harness, tools-minus-force and safety limits; HiddenPhys pairs all rows on the same seeds, counts code-checked final states, adds a damage counter (any wrist reading above 15 N with the gripper commanded closed on a handle), and scores decisions (weight class, CoM class, locked-versus-stiff) separately from task success. Ablations hold the primitives and seeds fixed and vary only the *form* of the force feedback across seven levels: no force, event words, a force plot, tared raw samples, harness-derived signals, L1 quantities, and L2 quantities plus uncertainty plus flags.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not an explanation of the model at all — an **interface**: a fixed, computed re-description of a sensor stream in physical units, with uncertainty and flags, plus a self-terminating action vocabulary underneath. It is a claim that a black-box decision-maker's competence on a stream is a property of the representation it is handed, not only of what it knows. It is partial by construction (twelve primitives, hand-written estimators, hand-written per-task instructions) and it predicts nothing about the network's internals.

## How they judged success
Code-checked success on the final state, per task and per condition, with successes out of 10 (real robot, ForceBench) or 20 (HiddenPhys) episodes, Wilson intervals and exact McNemar tests on paired seeds; damage counts per row; decision accuracy on the three judgement tasks; token cost per episode; and a distribution-shift arm in which only the hidden ranges and the instruction numbers change while no policy is retrained. Two controls carry the argument: a position-only controller on the same scripts (does the task actually need force?), and the seven-level feedback-form ablation on identical primitives and seeds (is the win from the force or from the form it arrives in?). An expert hand-written force controller per task is the classical ceiling, and Diffusion Policy / a flow-matching VLA / ForceVLA trained from demonstrations are the learned comparators, all on identical seeds.

## What they found
Real robot, six tasks, ten episodes each: GPT-6 Astra goes from 2/60 successes without force to 54/60 with the harness (Sol 47/60), every task separating under Fisher's exact test; the two tasks that need a property cameras cannot see (sorting look-alike bottles by mass, naming identically wrapped objects soft or hard) go 0/10 → 10/10, on an arm with no wrist sensor. ForceBench: Astra 70.0% → 87.5%, Sol 65.0% → 87.5%, Fable 75.0% with the harness, against 35.31% for the best trained policy the benchmark paper reports — but the authors state plainly that most of that gap is the model, harness and instructions, with force contributing the paired gain and mainly on Drawer Pull (0/10 → 10/10). HiddenPhys: Astra 87/140 → 135/140 and Fable 96/140 → 135/140 with **zero** damaged handles, matching the hand-written expert at 136/140, while trained policies score 65–83/140 with frequent damage; adding force as an input to Diffusion Policy, the flow VLA or ForceVLA brings no consistent gain. Under shifted hidden ranges the harnessed models lose 8% and 5% (69/80 and 71/80) while no-force rows lose 16–19% and learned policies 17–25%. Ablations: the self-terminating primitives alone, reporting only contact events with no force values, lift success 0.63 → 0.77 for Sol and 0.18 → 0.54 for smaller models and cut smaller-model handle damage from 22/25 to 3/25, so safe contact execution is bought by the action vocabulary; judging a held load has no stop event, and there the form matters — for smaller models L2 quantities reach 0.85 decision accuracy against 0.57 without force, 0.69 with raw numbers, 0.71 with a plot, while derived signals (0.85) do as well as L1 and L2, so **the credit belongs to computing the quantity, not to its formatting**. Turn-by-turn calls beat one-shot programs (23% → 77% → 91% for Astra). Estimator accuracy against simulator truth: mass error mean 0.004 kg with SD 0.03 kg, CoM direction cosine at least 0.97.

## Limits and open problems they name
Seconds of API latency between primitives, fine for quasi-static contact but not for fast reactive replanning; the primitive menu and the physics estimators are hand-specified, as is every task instruction; scope is single-arm tabletop manipulation with a parallel gripper across two API providers; and **the component ablations are run in simulation only, not repeated on hardware**. Two protocol concessions are stated outright: ForceBench tools and thresholds were tuned on eight of ten seeds and two tasks were revised after initial runs, and the public evaluator omits release and clearance checks on three tasks. One behaviour is worth flagging as a scoring artefact they name themselves: doing nothing succeeds on a locked door, so most of Diffusion Policy's Lock score is free.

## What we take from it
A method and a framing. The method is the ablation design: hold the action vocabulary and the seeds fixed, vary only the **form in which a quantity reaches the black-box reader**, and include a raw-numbers row and a plot row so that "computed quantity" is separated from "nicer formatting" — our probe read-outs, circuit summaries and surrogate coordinates should all be put through exactly this ladder, with the raw-samples and rendered variants as the honest controls. The framing is that a frozen reader's competence with an internal (or external) stream is partly a property of the interface, so a null result about a read-out is only final after the presentation ladder has been run. Two warnings transfer directly. First, **termination belongs below the reader**: because each primitive stops on a contact event, the decision rate can be seconds and the loop still works, which is the same division of labour a white-box predictor needs if it is to drive anything at a rate faster than its own forward pass — and the primitive alone, with no values returned, already bought most of the safety. Second, an error-reporting convention worth copying: every returned quantity carries an uncertainty and flags, and the estimators are validated against simulator ground truth with a mean and an SD, while the printed uncertainty covers measurement noise only — the two are reported separately rather than merged into one interval.
