# Understanding Rollout Error in Graph World Models

- **Key:** arxiv-2606.27780
- **Authors / organisation:** Xinyuan Song, Zekun Cai; Emory University, Atlanta, GA, USA and The University of Tokyo, Tokyo, Japan
- **Date:** 2026-10-12 (v3; v1 2026-06-26)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.27780
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

World models are increasingly used to plan by imagining future states under candidate action sequences, and many of the environments that matter for agents are graph-shaped: tools and APIs, skills, calls, dependencies. Nobody had characterised how a one-step prediction error compounds when the thing being rolled forward is a graph state that includes the edge set. The paper's goal is to say, for that setting, when long-horizon rollouts stay reliable and when graph structure itself is the thing that destroys them — and then to use that account to build a training objective that keeps rollouts stable. The larger aim is a predictive rather than descriptive understanding of a learned transition model: not "here is the graph", but "here is the quantity that decides how fast my prediction about this graph degrades".

## What they studied

Learned graph-state transition models, i.e. graph world models (GWMs), on graphs of 50 nodes drawn from seven canonical families — chain, tree, grid, small-world, scale-free, star, complete — spanning adjacency spectral radii from 2.0 to 49.0, plus an edge-density sweep with Erdős–Rényi graphs at edge probabilities 0.02 to 0.2. Architectures compared: a structure-blind MLP world model, a vanilla GCN world model, an MPNN world model, a GPS-style graph transformer, an ActionNode GWM, and the authors' Error-Aware GWM. Two regimes are separated throughout: **fixed-edge** (only node features are predicted) and **dynamic-edge** (node features and adjacency are both predicted and fed back). Two heterogeneous testbeds stand in for agent work: an agent calling-tree DAG (roughly 22–30 nodes, nine node types, six relation types) and a platform skill graph (40 nodes, eight types). Unit of analysis: the rollout, decomposed into a node-feature error norm and an edge error norm, and secondarily the individual node (for correction and perturbation studies).

## How they did it

The construction is deliberately minimal and is the most reusable part. Define the rollout error vector as the pair of Frobenius norms of predicted-minus-true node features and predicted-minus-true adjacency at each step, then ask what linear recursion bounds it. In the fixed-edge regime the answer factors cleanly: the per-step amplification is a product of a **topology term** — the spectral radius of the adjacency — and a **model term** — the product of the transition layers' spectral norms, times the activation's Lipschitz constant. They name that product the Graph Error Amplification Factor (GEAF), computed from topology and weights alone, before any rollout is run. In the dynamic-edge regime the scalar recursion becomes a two-by-two operator whose four entries are edge-to-node, node-to-node, node-to-edge and edge-to-edge sensitivities; they derive its spectral radius and prove it exceeds both of the uncoupled components whenever the two off-diagonal terms are positive — that is, coupling amplifies. They then bound the joint operator's spectral radius by GEAF up to a factor of two under a separation condition, and carry the error through a value-smoothness and reward-error assumption into a planning-regret bound whose rollout term grows super-linearly in the horizon once the discount times the spectral radius exceeds one. A corollary turns this into a diagnostic: in the amplification-dominated regime, the slope of log error against rollout step converges to the log of the amplification factor, so **slope, not terminal error, is the readout of topology**. Empirically they train all baselines on fixed-edge and dynamic-edge simulators and sweep horizon, density and graph size; then they add a training objective with three regularisers — a spectral-norm penalty on the transition weights, a multi-step rollout-consistency term, and a per-node weighting by structural importance — and ablate it against gradient clipping, weight decay and spectral-norm projection.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **scalar operator plus a decomposition**, not a description of any particular failure. The account is a bound of the form "error at step k is at most L^k times initial error plus a geometric series in the one-step error", and its explanatory value is entirely in the factorisation: the same L splits into one number that is a property of the human-declared graph and one that is a property of the learned weights. It is explicitly partial — an upper bound, not an equality — and its prediction is not "this edge will be wrong" but "this graph, with these weights, will amplify the error at this exponential rate". The planning-regret bound extends the same object to a claim about decisions. Nothing here localises a fault; the local variant they do construct (per-node GEAF) collapses to degree centrality, as they themselves point out.

## How they judged success

Four checks, in decreasing strength. (1) **Theory against a measured slope**: they predict that log GEAF should correlate with the empirical log-error growth slope between horizons 4 and 32, and note that the slope, not the terminal error, is the quantity the corollary licenses. (2) **Paired comparisons with a non-parametric test**: fixed-edge-trained versus dynamic-edge-trained models in 36 paired cells (two architectures × six topologies × three seeds), reported as an error ratio with a Wilcoxon signed-rank p-value and a bootstrap interval; plus a coupling check, ρ of the joint operator against the maximum of its diagonal entries, in 108 cells. (3) **Ablation against simpler stabilisers** over 21 fixed-edge cells, scored on two numbers at once — how many cells diverged, and the median long-horizon error — with ratios to the full method. (4) **Calibration and an oracle against the ground-truth simulator** on the heterogeneous testbeds: Pearson correlation and mean absolute error of a relational GCN against the simulator, plus random, oracle and degree-based correction policies under a fixed 10 per cent correction budget, and a role-wise masking study. They also report standard static and temporal graph benchmarks where their models are not the best, and say so.

## What they found

- **Topology sets the growth rate.** Graphs with larger spectral radius show faster growth of log error and of planning regret across the horizon sweep; low-risk families stay flat, high-risk ones grow sharply at longer horizons. Terminal error is a poor readout: in their topology table the 32-step node error is *smallest* for the densest graph, while the amplification factor and the growth slope tell the opposite story.
- **Coupling amplifies.** In the dynamic-edge regime the estimated spectral radius of the joint operator lay between about 132 and 368, against 2 to 50 in the fixed-edge setting, and the coupling inequality held in all 108 cells (mean excess around 155, p below 1e-30). Dynamic-edge training improved 32-step error in every paired cell, with a combined ratio of about 11 times.
- **Its own stabiliser works but is not magic.** On scale-free and star graphs the vanilla GCN rollout diverges (one configuration recorded as non-finite) while the regularised model stays near the low-error floor (about 2.9e-4 and 1.5e-4 at 32 steps); the ablation shows weight decay and spectral-norm projection cut divergences from 10 of 21 to 4 of 21 but leave median error 1690 to 4172 times higher, and gradient clipping barely helps (8 of 21). Spectral projection alone is not the mechanism; it is the combination that holds.
- **Global amplification and local damage are different objects.** The complete graph has the largest amplification factor, but the *star* graph spreads a hub perturbation to the largest fraction of nodes — so a single spectral number does not tell you where a local fault will land.
- **The explanation's own arms are weak, and they print them.** The agent-task success rate falls from about 0.49 at horizon 1 to about 0.42 for horizon 5 and beyond for *every* model including theirs — the rollouts do not get better at the thing agents care about. On Cora a plain GCN beats both of their models (80.6 against 75.0), and on temporal link prediction the ranking across average precision, AUC and mean reciprocal rank splits three ways with no model best on all three. The per-node correction rule they derive from the theory ties exactly with degree centrality, achieving a 12.9 per cent error reduction against an oracle's 22.4 per cent, because for fixed weights the local score is degree times a graph-wide constant.

## Limits and open problems they name

Their own scope paragraph concedes that a large-amplification factor is an upper bound and the empirical association is a trend, not a functional form; that the heterogeneous testbeds are simulators informed by agent benchmarks rather than real agent logs, and the relational model's instance-level correlation with the simulator is modest (0.311 and 0.231), so the rollouts are studied as behaviour rather than as faithful replacements; and that long-horizon task completion remains unsolved by their method. They also state plainly that the dynamic-edge advantage does not transfer to temporal link prediction, i.e. evolving-edge modelling pays only when edge dynamics carry predictive signal. Beyond what they say: the theory assumes node identities are fixed, static adjacency (spectral radius as a constant), symmetric adjacency, and Lipschitz continuity with constants that are *assumed* rather than estimated — the bounds are stated in terms of L_X, L_A, M_X and M_A, and nothing in the paper measures those four numbers and checks them against the observed recursion, which would have been the natural test of the operator account rather than of the easier scalar proxy. The growth-slope readout is described as exploratory; it has no confidence interval attached.

## What we take from it

1. **A decomposition to copy: separate the factor you were given from the factor you learned, and compute both before the experiment.** The most transferable object in the paper is the product form — one number that is a property of the declared structure, one that is a property of the weights, multiplying to an amplification rate. For a white-box account of a black-box network, the analogue is to name which part of the predicted error growth is attributable to the architecture or wiring you recorded and which to the fitted parameters, and to report the two separately, in advance, as a prediction. A single combined number invites the reader to attribute the whole failure to the model.
2. **Score the growth rate, not the terminal error — and say which you are scoring.** Their own tables are the illustration: the dense graph ends with the smallest error and the fastest amplification. Any longitudinal prediction we make about a probe campaign or a rollout should be scored against a slope with a stated fitting window (they use horizons 4 to 32, deliberately excluding the first steps) and reported alongside, not instead of, the endpoint. This is a concrete, cheap addition to the folder's recurring "one number is not a readout" line.
3. **The one test they did not run is the one our goal needs: measure the operator's coefficients and check the recursion.** All four lemmas are conditional on Lipschitz constants that nobody estimates. Estimating them empirically and asking whether the observed error recursion matches the predicted one would convert a bound into a *model of the error* — which is exactly the shift from description to prediction we are trying to make. Warning: a paper that stops at the bound leaves that step open for us.
4. **A caution on any theory-derived local score.** Their per-node amplification score reduces algebraically to degree centrality once weights are fixed, which they admit. The general form of the trap: a localisation metric built as "topology weight at node × global constant" carries no node-specific information beyond the topology, so it cannot outperform the trivial centrality baseline and its apparent success is the baseline's. Any per-unit score we derive must be checked for reduction to a structural baseline before it is credited.
5. **Their honest negative results are the pattern to imitate.** Two of the most informative numbers in the paper — unchanged agent task success across all methods, and a plain GCN winning on Cora — are reported in appendices of a paper whose contribution is elsewhere. That is the folder's standing rule made concrete: the failing arm ships in the same document.
