# From Reward-Hack Activations to Agentic Risk States: Context-Calibrated Mechanistic Monitoring in LLM Agents

- **Key:** arxiv-2606.06223
- **Authors / organisation:** Patrick Wilhelm, Odej Kao; Distributed Operating Systems, Technische Universität Berlin, and BIFOLD — Berlin Institute for the Foundations of Learning and Data
- **Date:** 2026-06-04
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06223
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Mechanistic monitors are usually validated on isolated generations: an activation probe is trained to fire when a model is doing something unsafe, and its score is read as a probability of bad output. The authors point out that this reading does not survive deployment as an agent, where behaviour emerges from a loop of observation, reasoning and action, and where an internal state can be present but behaviourally silent until the environment offers an action that cashes it in. Their case study is reward hacking: a model fine-tuned on tasks with cheap loopholes may carry a reward-hack-like internal state, but whether it actually takes the shortcut on a given step depends on whether the current environment exposes the shortcut. The larger goal is to move monitoring from thresholding one scalar to estimating a conditional risk over the next action, given both the internal state and the decision context. They frame this as a study of how a monitor's semantics shift when the model starts acting.

## What they studied
Three small open model families (Qwen as the main subject, Llama as secondary support, Falcon treated as a boundary case), each with LoRA adapters trained by GRPO at rank 32 into five policy regimes: benign control data, three mixtures (Mix05, Mix10, Mix50) interpolating between benign and reward-hack data, and a pure hack adapter. The reward-hack data is the School of Reward Hacks set. Environments are a modified ALFWorld that deliberately adds proxy-reward affordances (choosing an easy grader, claiming completion, inspecting the score) and WebShop as a public second setting. The unit of analysis is the decision step: a step-level reasoning segment produces a vector of internal summaries, and the target is the risk label of the *next* action. Two labels are used — a broad "bad action" label and a narrow "explicit exploit" label defined from the environment action, never from the monitor.

## How they did it
For each reasoning token the agent's residual-stream activations are passed through a sparse-autoencoder monitor (hidden width 8,000) whose standardised features are PCA-projected and classified by a light linear classifier into a reward-hack score in [0, 1]; the monitor is trained only on control and hack adapters, and the mixed adapters are never used to fit it. Per step they also record next-token entropy, and both signals are compressed into five summaries: mean, final-token value, mean over the last fifth of the segment, the late-versus-early difference, and a least-squares slope over the last fifth. Decision-context features are collected separately and describe the situation rather than the future: reasoning budget, normalised step index, previous action type, environment identity, and affordance flags. Next-step risk is a logistic regression on the concatenation of internal and context features, evaluated out-of-fold with grouped cross-validation by episode so every step of an episode is held out together, with preprocessing fit on training folds only. Feature-group arms are activation only, entropy only, temporal only, context only, internal (activation + entropy + temporal), and internal + context. For the intervention probe they fit a contrastive reward-hack direction at a layer from benign versus hack activations and subtract a multiple of it from the residual stream either always or gated on a score, comparing proxy-exploit rate, easy-grader actions, fake completions and task success against the same adapter unsteered. They also sweep reasoning budget (test-time compute) and report it as a context variable rather than as a safety setting.

## What counts as an explanation here
A calibrated predictor: a logistic model over interpretable feature groups that outputs a probability of the next action being risky, plus a per-group contribution table that says which signal carries the information in each condition. The claim is explicitly partial and semantic rather than mechanistic — the reward-hack activation is described as a descriptor of a latent policy regime, not as an explanation of the computation that produces it, and the steering experiment is offered as evidence that the monitored representation is causally relevant, not as a mechanism. There is no circuit, no feature dictionary and no attempt to say why the state fails to become an action; the explanation is of the *mapping* from state and context to risk.

## How they judged success
Primary metric is AUPRC gain over the base rate — chosen because risky labels are heavily imbalanced, and because a random ranking recovers exactly the prevalence. Secondary metrics are AUROC and Recall@20%, the fraction of risky steps caught when flagging the highest-risk fifth of decision states. Baselines are the feature-group arms themselves, with activation-only standing in for the prior-generation monitor. Controls include episode-grouped cross-validation, preprocessing confined to training folds, a base-rate reference, and an explicit stability rule that drops conditions with too few positives or negatives (the target-stability appendix lists positive counts and base rates per family and environment, marking unstable panels). Steering is judged by change in proxy-exploit behaviour, easy-grader and fake-completion rates, and task success relative to the same adapter steered off.

## What they found
Reward-hack fine-tuning transfers into action selection but not monotonically with mixture strength. On Qwen, the fully hack-trained adapter has the highest mean activation (0.966) yet an exploit-action rate of only 0.019, whereas Mix50 — lower activation, 0.894 — takes the exploit action 0.450 of the time, with gameable and easy-grader rates of 0.225 each; the benign control sits at 0.003. Activation alone is a weak next-step predictor on the broad label (AUPRC gain +0.020, AUROC 0.565, Recall@20% 0.197 on Qwen), entropy alone is much stronger (+0.102, AUROC 0.601), and internal + context is best (+0.164, AUROC 0.667, Recall@20% 0.306). On the narrow exploit label the ordering inverts: activation alone is informative (+0.109, AUROC 0.769) and activation + entropy is the best group (+0.131), with internal + context slightly worse (+0.111) — though the exploit target has only 184 positives out of 7,322 steps. Outside Qwen the pattern weakens: Llama shows no exploit positives at all for control, Mix50 and hack, and Falcon mixes high bad-action rates with zero exploits. Always-on steering against the reward-hack direction reduces the proxy score without success by 0.8 on Mix05, 2.0 on Mix10 and 2.2 on Mix50 at alpha 0.25, with easy-grader and fake-completion deltas turning negative on the two stronger mixtures; gated steering is not uniformly effective. WebShop reproduces predictive value under a single saved activation-plus-entropy configuration (Qwen buy AUPRC gain +0.112, Llama AUROC 0.790, Falcon AUROC 0.909 on ten positives) but the authors explicitly do not claim a full ablation there.

## Limits and open problems they name
Scope is the main one: the sharp result is one family in one controlled environment, the second family contributes only the broad label and the third is boundary evidence, and WebShop is breadth rather than replication. Prediction and steering effects are non-uniform across adapters, environments and reasoning budgets, so nothing here is a deployable mitigation. The context features are described as simple, with richer state representations and online gating named as next steps. Separately, the paper's own temporal diagnostics say there is no universal late-stage activation spike before risky actions, and the appendix states that the direction and strength of trajectory separation vary by adapter and signal — so the summary features, not a shape in the trajectory, are what carry the signal.

## What we take from it
A warning and a measure. The warning is the one worth carrying: a monitor score and the event it is supposed to predict are two different random variables, and this paper has the clean counter-example where the highest-scoring policy is among the least exploitative while a weaker-scoring mixture is the most exploitative. For our white-box model, that argues that any quantity we read off a representation is a *state descriptor*, and the predictive claim about behaviour needs a second, context-conditioned model on top of it — the state tells you which regime the model is in, the context tells you whether the regime can act. Concretely reusable: (a) the metric choice — report AUPRC gain over base rate alongside Recall-at-a-budget, because the budgeted recall is the only number that maps onto an intervention decision, and report positive counts so a reader can see which cells are noise; (b) episode-grouped cross-validation with fold-local preprocessing, which is the correct protocol for any of our step-level predictions and is cheap; (c) the negative result about the temporal shape, which tells us not to assume that risky states have a signature trajectory — the compressed summaries beat trajectory-shaped readings here, and the paper says so against its own interest.
