# Large-scale Neuron Community Discovery Reveals Neuron-Sparse Behaviors in Language Models

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.23112
- **Authors / organisation:** Igor Shilov, Thomas Fel, Tal Haklay, Lucius Bushnaq, Owen Lewis; Goodfire and Imperial College London
- **Date:** 2026-11-17
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.23112
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (v1; main paper Sections 1–8 plus Appendices A–F; figures not inspected, every number taken from prose or table cells)
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Interpretability has two competing pictures of what a unit is: a single neuron with many unrelated jobs, or a learned dictionary atom that cuts across neurons. A third possibility — that small *groups* of ordinary MLP neurons are the reusable unit — had been suggested one behaviour at a time (a 28-neuron addition circuit, neuron circuits for agreement) but only on prompts a researcher chose by hand, which quietly assumes the model's computations follow human task categories. The authors want to invert that: search millions of diverse prompts for wherever neurons are repeatedly recruited together, then ask what those groups do. The point is scale — an unsupervised discovery method that yields a *dictionary of neuron groups* usable as a starting point for explaining behaviours nobody thought to look for.

## What they studied
Llama-3.1-8B MLP neurons across layers 0–29 (layers 30–31 excluded because their per-prompt attributions look less sparse). Discovery used ten million prompts mixed from FineWeb-Edu, Wikipedia and FineMath, plus 470,000 kept prompts from five targeted slices (grammar, code, QA, language-switch, memorised text). Causal testing used a further ten million held-out prompts from the same mix, with roughly 8.9M surviving the prompt-level deduplication. The unit of analysis is the *community* — a set of MLP neurons, often spanning many layers, capped at 128 members — with the individual neuron as the graph node and the layer subset as the frame in which a community claims its effect.

## How they did it
Four steps, each cheap until the last. First, one forward and one backward pass per prompt scores every neuron by the absolute value of its activation times the gradient of the log-probability of the model's own top predicted token, and the top five neurons per layer are kept, giving a sparse attributed set per prompt. Second, an undirected graph over neurons is built where an edge weight is the *z-score of the co-occurrence count* against what each neuron's individual frequency would predict, so that neurons which merely fire everywhere do not get joined. Third, Leiden community detection with the Constant Potts Model objective is run at six resolution values and the resulting partitions are pooled; neurons in the top-five set for more than 20% of prompts are pulled out as 17 "always on" members and added back at test time. Fourth, each candidate group is tested on held-out prompts with three zero-ablation interventions restricted to the final prompt position: ablating the community's neurons inside a layer subspan (necessity), ablating every other MLP neuron in that subspan while keeping the community plus always-on neurons (sufficiency), and ablating the whole subspan except always-on neurons (layer necessity, which guards against calling a group sufficient when the layers themselves do not matter). Every contiguous subspan of the community's ordered layer list is tried, so a group can explain only part of the depth range it spans.

## What counts as an explanation here
A bipartite dictionary: a named neuron group on one side, a behavioural class on the other, plus a causal certificate per prompt. The certificate is the triple of KL divergences — community necessity at 0.7 nats or more, layer necessity at 0.7 or more, sufficiency at 0.2 or less — with the layer subspan that achieves it. The explanation is explicitly *partial*: it claims that a group carries the contribution of certain layers to this prediction, not that it carries the whole computation. Descriptive content is added afterwards by an LLM classifier reading up to a hundred examples per community and assigning one of eight behavioural classes.

## How they judged success
Necessity, sufficiency and layer necessity as KL divergence from the clean next-token distribution, with the thresholds above; prompt coverage as the fraction of unique held-out prompts for which at least one community passes all three tests, currently counted with a greedy set-cover ordering; a null dictionary made by shuffling neuron assignments across communities within each layer, preserving size, layer set and neuron frequency; an eight-way ablation of the attribution score itself (gradient target, activation factor, gradient-free variants) at one-million-prompt scale on a shared 852,263-prompt evaluation set; a recovery test against the 28 known layer-18 addition neurons from the Base-10/Arithmetic-in-the-Wild line (prompts from that released dataset, four templates); a train-versus-held-out comparison of six chosen Leiden resolutions against an exhaustive subset search over fourteen; and, for the head's usual question, a full subset ablation — all 64 subsets of the six-neuron community — measuring mean KL across 100 prompts.

## What they found
Communities of at most 128 neurons pass all three tests within at least one layer on **10.3%** of held-out prompts; 8.7% when a single community must explain four layers at once and 3.3% when it must explain twelve. With a 16-neuron budget coverage is still 6.7% at one layer and 2.5% at eight layers. A single resolution (256) reaches at most 5.74%, so pooling the six resolutions is worth roughly 4.5 points of coverage; the six chosen resolutions retain 99.1% of the union of all fourteen and the same six are recovered when selection uses only training prompts. The shuffled-community null reaches 2.91% inside the plotted range against 9.94% for the real dictionary on the ablation corpus, and under 0.2% by the tenth community. The attribution score matters: the activation-times-gradient score gets 9.94%, a corpus-token or margin gradient 8.88% and 8.74%, gradients alone 6.31%, and every activation-only score 2.5–2.7% — below the shuffled null. Known-neuron recovery is 17/28 for addition, 20/26 for hours, 13/16 for months and 14/15 for weekdays within the top 40 layer-18 neurons. The worked example — `an` + vowel agreement — is six neurons across layers 2–14: ablating them moves median KL to 4.99, ablating every other MLP neuron in those six layers except four always-on ones moves it to 3.15, ablating six random neurons leaves it near zero, and retaining the six plus always-on preserves it at median KL 0.053 out of 86,016 MLP neurons in those layers. The subset sweep shows **redundant voting**: one member (L14:11888) alone recovers most of the behaviour (mean KL 0.68 against 4.56 with all six removed and 0.06 with all six present), yet removing it from the full group changes almost nothing (all six single-member ablations land between 0.06 and 0.07), and a pair that excludes it (L5:3846 + L12:14182) reaches about 0.2. Redundancy is not uniform across communities: in the in-context copying community one member alone accounts for the effect when removed (0.05 to 7.37). Behavioural classes skew lexical: fixed phrases 42.3% of communities and 31.3% of covered prompts, token suppression 19.5% and 19.6%, grammar only 2.9% of communities but 15.3% of covered prompts; suppressive/token classes concentrate in late layers, sub-word completion peaks at layer 0, and retrieval and arithmetic peak around layers 17–22.

## Limits and open problems they name
Attribution and interventions are taken at the final prompt position only, so cross-position mechanisms — operands read at earlier positions, for instance — cannot be found. Leiden partitions are disjoint at each resolution, so a neuron used by several mechanisms is forced into one group. The coverage number is a property of the pipeline on the evaluation distribution, and the authors say plainly that it is not an upper bound on how much of the model is neuron-sparse: a mechanism can be missed for want of relevant prompts, because attribution misses its neurons, or because partitioning splits it. Recall is unmeasured for most behaviours because behaviour boundaries are unclear at scale, and the eight-class taxonomy is produced by an LLM, so labels may be broad or wrong — the paper cites documented observational and causal errors in generated explanations and says the taxonomy illustrates rather than measures. Why some behaviours are neuron-sparse and others are not is untested; the capacity/interference-cost story is offered as speculation, with the acknowledged confound that frequent behaviours are also the easiest for the pipeline to discover.

## What we take from it
A working answer to the head's long-running gap, from the neuron side and at real scale: the per-unit reading worth publishing here is not a single neuron's explanation but *which neurons are recruited together*, and the paper shows the map is obtainable from cheap attribution plus a popularity-corrected graph rather than from a solve. Three things are directly reusable. (1) The **community is the right granularity for a causal certificate**, and its certificate must be a triple — the group is necessary, the layers are necessary, the group is sufficient within those layers — because sufficiency alone is trivially satisfiable by keeping everything and necessity alone says nothing about how much of the layer the group replaces. (2) The **popularity-corrected co-occurrence graph** (z-score against each neuron's own frequency, then a resolution sweep rather than one clustering) is the cheap structural prior our own per-unit tables lack, and it converts a list of attributions into groups without a behaviour being named in advance. (3) The **subset ablation is the measurement that turns a group into a mechanism**, and it is the salvage route for a single-unit reading that fails necessity: redundant voting means a weak per-neuron score can still be part of a necessary group, so the head should stop treating individual necessity scores as the unit of evidence. The warning is equally sharp: coverage of 10.3% under a generous definition, down to 3.3% at twelve layers, measures what this pipeline recovers, not what is there — so any coverage figure we publish needs the same sentence, and needs the shuffled dictionary beside it, since the shuffled null beats every activation-only attribution.
