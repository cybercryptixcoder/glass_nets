# The robustness of differentiable causal discovery in misspecified scenarios

- **Key:** arxiv-2510.12503
- **Authors / organisation:** Huiyang Yi, Yanyan He, Duxin Chen, Mingyu Kang, He Wang, Wenwu Yu; School of Mathematics and School of Cyber Science and Engineering, Southeast University, Nanjing (He and Yi marked equal contribution, Chen corresponding)
- **Date:** 2025-10
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.12503
- **Kind:** paper (benchmark + theory note)
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

Causal discovery methods are sold with assumptions that nobody can test on real data: linear or nonlinear mechanisms, no unobserved common causes, noiseless observation, exchangeable data, a fidelity between independence and graph structure that cancellations can break. The authors' question is not which algorithm is best on clean synthetic data but which algorithms survive being fed data generated by a *different* model than the one they were designed for, and whether the survival, or the collapse, can be explained rather than merely tabulated. The stated goal is to give practitioners a basis for picking a method when they cannot know which assumption is violated, and to establish gradient-based (differentiable) structure learning as robust enough to be worth building on. It sits downstream of earlier benchmarks that covered only linear models, only bivariate settings, only Markov-equivalence-class outputs, or only traditional methods.

## What they studied

Twelve algorithms spanning four families: constraint-based (PC), score-based (GES), functional-causal-model (DirectLiNGAM), order-by-variance heuristics (Var-SortnRegress, R²-SortnRegress), and differentiable discovery (NOTEARS, GOLEM, NoCurl, DAGMA, plus the neural variants NOTEARS-MLP and GraN-DAG). CAM is carried as the strong non-gradient nonlinear baseline. The unit of analysis is the **whole inferred graph**, compared against the generating graph — nothing here looks inside a model. Data are synthetic: linear vanilla (coefficients drawn from two uniform ranges away from zero, standard Gaussian noise), nonlinear vanilla (Gaussian-process mechanisms with an RBF kernel of bandwidth one), and a third MLP setting with one hidden layer of width 100. Graphs are Erdos–Renyi and scale-free with 10, 20 and 50 nodes at average degree 2 and 4, plus a Gaussian-random-partition family at degree 6; ten seeds of 2000 samples per cell, 2,400-plus datasets and 70,000-plus runs in total. Sachs is used as the one real dataset.

## How they did it

They construct the vanilla generative model, then mutate one ingredient at a time and re-run the whole algorithm set on the mutated data, keeping the original graph as ground truth. The eight violation scenarios are: latent confounding, measurement error (observation noise scaled to each variable's own variance), autoregressive structure, heterogeneity (two domains with different noise variances mixed in varying proportions), unfaithful path cancellation, scale variation (inputs standardised while the graph stays fixed), missing-at-random entries (rows deleted and data regenerated so the sample count is held constant), and mechanism violation (convex nonlinear truth for methods that assume linearity and the reverse). Four of these follow an earlier benchmark's generators; the heterogeneous, scale-variant, missing and mechanism-violation cases are the paper's own additions. Two metrics are reported: structural Hamming distance, which counts insertions, deletions and reversals needed to reach the truth, and structural intervention distance, which compares the two graphs' answers to every possible single-variable intervention and is sensitive to ordering rather than adjacency. Hyperparameters are tuned per method on each dataset from a fixed grid, which the authors state openly as a favourable choice. A separate MLP-settings run re-tests CAM against NOTEARS-MLP on data whose mechanism is not a Gaussian process, to test whether the Gaussian-process generator was flattering CAM. Finally, they give a theoretical account for the linear least-squares differentiable methods using two existing results from the literature: least squares returns the true graph in the large-sample limit when noise variances are equal, and it still does when the ratio of largest to smallest noise variance is below one plus a margin over the number of nodes, where the margin is the score gap between the true graph and the next best. Their argument is to compute what that ratio becomes under each violation and check whether the guarantee still applies.

## What counts as an explanation here

Two things, and they are separate in kind. The empirical explanation is a robustness profile: for each method, a table of two graph-level metrics in each violation cell, plus a summary table averaging over graph types, densities and violation scenarios. The theoretical explanation is a single scalar — the noise-variance ratio — recomputed under each violated assumption, with the claim that it crosses the threshold implied by the margin and therefore the guarantee lapses. This is a *prediction* of failure rather than a description of it, but it is a directional prediction only: the theory says whether the guarantee holds, never how much worse the metric will be. Nothing here explains any individual edge or decision inside a model; the whole account lives at graph granularity.

## How they judged success

Structural Hamming distance and structural intervention distance, mean and standard deviation over ten seeds, per cell. The comparison set is the twelve methods themselves plus a **random-graph baseline** whose rows are printed in every table. Two fairness devices are worth naming: the hyperparameter grid is tuned per dataset against the truth (they say so explicitly, noting that real use has no such oracle), and when a method outputs a CPDAG the undirected edges are scored as correctly oriented — a favourable assumption for PC and GES that they inherit from prior work. Controls are the vanilla cells that sit beside every violated cell in the same table, the MLP re-run of the CAM comparison, and appendices repeating the whole experiment across node counts, graph families, densities, combined violations, exponential rather than Gaussian noise, extreme measurement error, semi-synthetic data built on the Sachs graph, and runtime.

## What they found

The headline is a qualified robustness claim: differentiable methods are at or near the best in almost every violation they test, with scale variation as the exception. The numbers in the linear ten-node ER-2 setting carry it. In the vanilla cell DAGMA reaches SHD 1.2 and GOLEM 1.4 against 12.4 for PC and 11.2 for the variance-sorting heuristic; under measurement error those same two rise to 12.6 and 17.8 while NoCurl reaches 11.8 and NoCurl's intervention distance of 17.9 is the best of any method; under latent confounding DAGMA 8.4 with intervention distance 8.8, NoCurl 9.1 and 5.4; under unfaithful data GOLEM and DAGMA at SHD 2.1 with intervention distance 0.6. Missing data barely moves them (DAGMA 1.5, GOLEM 1.7). Scale variation breaks the linear methods outright — NOTEARS 18.0 and GOLEM 17.5 SHD, worse than PC at 12.4 — and the nonlinear differentiable methods degrade as well but stay ahead of PC and GES. Mechanism violation is the reverse surprise: linear differentiable methods hold up on nonlinear-generated data (NoCurl 19.1 is the outlier, NOTEARS and DAGMA 19.0) while the nonlinear ones do very well on linear data (DAGMA 3.3 and 9.5, the best cell in the paper). CAM is the strongest nonlinear method on the Gaussian-process generator (SHD 4.7 against 12.4 for NOTEARS-MLP) but loses almost everywhere once the mechanism is an MLP (15.7 against 8.1 in vanilla, and worse in seven of eight violations; the exception is scale variation, where CAM wins at 12.4 against 18.2), and the authors read that as the Gaussian-process generator having been tuned to CAM's own assumption. On Sachs, DAGMA is best at SHD 12 and intervention distance 42, with DirectLiNGAM and NOTEARS-MLP at 14. Extreme measurement error with the noise scale ten removes the robustness entirely. Runtime favours the gradient methods sharply in the low-node regime: DAGMA 2.4 seconds and NOTEARS-MLP 4.7 at ten nodes against 45.7 for CAM, 119.3 for GraN-DAG and 130.2 for GOLEM. The theory reproduces the collapse qualitatively: measurement error turns the equal-noise assumption into a ratio above one, unfaithful cancellation adds a second noise term to the collider so the ratio becomes two, missing data leaves the ratio at one, and the margin over node count is what decides whether the least-squares objective still identifies the truth.

## Limits and open problems they name

They disclaim universality in the conclusion — the claim is potential within the benchmarks evaluated, not optimality everywhere — and name semi-synthetic and real-data extension as future work, along with a complete absence of temporal causal discovery, which they flag as a gap. The theoretical account covers only linear least-squares differentiable methods and is reused machinery rather than new proof; it predicts loss of guarantee, never magnitude. Their own caveats are telling in detail: hyperparameters were chosen per dataset with the truth in hand, the theory is silent about the heterogeneity and scale-variation cells where the largest effects appear, and the note on extreme measurement error says only that robustness is lost, without a threshold.

## What we take from it

Three things, one of which is a warning against the paper's own headline.

First, a **measurement lesson that matters for us directly**: structural Hamming distance and structural intervention distance can disagree by a wide margin in the same cell — GOLEM under latent confounding has the better adjacency score (6.7 against 8.5 for NOTEARS) and a much worse intervention score (14.2 against 9.5), and CAM's worst nonlinear cells are far better on adjacency than on intervention order. Any account we publish should say which of the two it is being scored on, because a readout can look recovered in adjacency while it predicts interventions badly, and our goal is prediction under intervention.

Second, the **noise-ratio diagnostic is the transferable mechanism**, not the benchmark table. They take one scalar property of the fitted problem, recompute it under each violated assumption, and use it to predict in which cells the guarantee lapses; it correctly predicts failure under measurement error and unfaithful cancellation and correctly predicts no movement under missing values, and it fails to say anything about the heterogeneity and scale cells. Mechanical rule for us: **for each robustness claim we make about our white-box account, name the scalar condition the guarantee depends on, compute it under the violation, and if it cannot be computed, say so and mark that cell as unpredicted rather than as robust.**

Third, a **warning about the paper's own strongest-looking result**: the CAM-versus-NOTEARS-MLP flip is the cleanest demonstration in this folder that a benchmark's generator is part of the answer. CAM wins on the Gaussian-process data because the generator matches its own assumption, and loses when the mechanism becomes a network — so "method A is more robust" was really "the benchmark's family was method A's family". Mechanical rule for us: **whenever a comparison reverses under a change of generator, that change is reported as the finding and the ranking before it is withdrawn, not averaged away**; and a method tuned per dataset against the truth is reported as an upper bound, never as expected performance.
