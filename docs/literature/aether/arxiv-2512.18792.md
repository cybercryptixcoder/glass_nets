## What they wanted, and why
The paper takes its title from the 2009 neuroscience prank in which a dead salmon, shown photographs of people, produced "significant" voxels under the analysis pipelines of the time — an artefact of uncorrected multiple comparisons. The authors argue that AI interpretability is now producing its own salmon: attribution maps, probes, sparse autoencoders, circuit discoveries and causal-abstraction methods all yield plausible-looking explanations when pointed at networks whose weights have been randomised. Their aim is not a new method but a diagnosis and a reframing: they want interpretability practice to be treated as an inference problem in the ordinary statistical sense, so that explanations carry assumptions, alternatives and uncertainty rather than standing alone as findings. The larger goal is the one the folder shares — making an explanatory claim checkable — and they think the field's failure to do this is a common root cause of false discovery, poor generalisation and high variance, rather than three separate accidents.

## What they studied
No model family of their own: the object of study is the *method* literature, across all six families the entry template recognises — gradient and feature attribution, probing, dictionary learning, concept-based explanation, causal mediation/patching and mechanistic circuit or abstraction work, plus natural-language rationales. Their single experiment (Appendix A) uses BERT-base-uncased on IMDb sentiment and on CoNLL-2003 POS tagging, and pythia-160m on place-name coordinates for the world-model probe. The unit of analysis is the *interpretability query*, not the neuron or the feature: the thing being analysed is what a method claims to answer, and whether the traces it is given can single out one answer.

## How they did it
The paper is argument-first, with one worked instrument. First they survey, family by family, the published demonstrations that each method can produce explanations of random computation or can be broken cheaply, cashing each in with a citation. Second they unify the failure modes under two statistical names: *underspecification*, where several predictive models fit the same data equally well, and *overdetermination*, where several causal pathways each independently suffice (the "hydra effect" from the ablation literature), and they point out that both give non-identifiability of the explanation. Third they build the normative frame: a computational system plus the input distribution that defines the behaviour of interest is written as a structural causal model, an explanation is a *surrogate* for it, and an interpretability *task* is a triple — a distribution over causal queries, a class of admissible explanations, and a discrepancy measure — with the ideal explanation the minimiser of expected discrepancy over queries. A method is then literally an estimator mapping finite query-trace data to a surrogate, and can be asked for bias, variance, consistency and confidence sets in the ordinary way. Fourth they spend the frame by reading each method family as a particular triple, in prose and in the appendix table. Fifth, the one experiment: they build a Monte Carlo hypothesis test in which the same method is run on B randomisations of the architecture (weight reinit, orthogonal rotation of representations, or label shuffling all offered as null constructions), the test statistic is whatever the method's own success measure is — probe accuracy, circuit fidelity, attribution-versus-intervention correlation — and the p-value is the standard permutation form with the plus-one correction so that type-I error is controlled; they run it on three probing tasks with twenty randomisations each and ten-fold cross-validation.

## What counts as an explanation here
A surrogate: a simpler computational description that answers a chosen collection of causal queries about the target system. The paper is deliberately indifferent to form — a circuit, a linear probe, a concept vector, a set of natural-language sentences are all admissible if the triple is stated — and its claim is that *any* such object is only meaningful once the query distribution, the hypothesis class and the error measure are fixed. Explanations are partial by construction: they are compressed renderings tuned to the queries the researcher cares about, and the authors say plainly that there is no single correct explanation of a network, only a best admissible one for a stated purpose. What they add beyond the familiar pragmatist line is that the surrogate framing can predict: if the triple is identifiable and its uncertainty reported, the surrogate should forecast the answer to a query you have not run, and should stay stable across designs, and it is exactly this that current tasks fail.

## How they judged success
Three levels. At the method level, published evidence of failure: sanity checks against randomised weights, adversarial manipulation of attributions, probe accuracy on random embeddings, SAE features in random transformers, circuit multiplicity on toy models, proofs that causal abstraction can explain random networks. At the framework level, identifiability of the triple — whether the expected-discrepancy functional has a unique minimiser up to declared symmetries — which is asserted or denied per family in prose rather than computed. At the experiment level, a Monte Carlo p-value against a family of null models, with effect sizes reported both against random guessing and against randomised networks, twenty nulls per task, ten-fold cross-validation, and a layerwise profile rather than a single number.

## What they found
The survey result is that every family has a documented salmon, and the authors are careful to attribute each to its original paper rather than re-demonstrate it. Their own experiment is the sharpest original number and it goes against the field's habit of reporting raw probe accuracy: on IMDb sentiment, pretrained layers beat random guessing with large effect sizes but *none* is statistically distinguishable from the randomised reinitialisations; on POS tagging the middle-layer peak survives the test but with substantially reduced effect size, and only those middle layers remain above the null; on the pythia world-model probe, the raw embeddings already carry spatial structure, randomised transformer blocks do not destroy it, and the pretrained model's late layers do eventually clear the random baseline — so the test is not merely destructive, it certifies when something is real. Their structural claims: hitting a random network with weight reinitialisation as the null cannot leave a salmon, because the artefact is by construction part of the null distribution; mediation effects themselves are identifiable because the intervention and model are fixed, while the *explanatory* claim that a high-effect component is the locus of a mechanism is not, because of overdetermination; circuit discovery inherits the hydra effect; and causal abstraction, though attractive because coarser variables can absorb redundant pathways, is shown empirically non-identifiable by its own operational metrics.

## Limits and open problems they name
The framework is offered as tentative and they invite replacement. They concede that identifiability is used only as analogy until interpretability is actually written as an explicit inference task, and that they have characterised no triple's identifiability conditions. The experiment is three probing tasks on two models — no attribution, dictionary or circuit method is put through the same test. The proposed remedies are gestures rather than recipes: richer query distributions trade discriminative power against the number of interventional samples needed; hypothesis classes need capacity control in the bias-variance sense; human simulability imposes a size ceiling on explanations that may exclude correct ones. They name as future work a taxonomy of identifiable tasks, a Bayesian treatment with priors over explanations and credible sets, active query selection to shrink posterior uncertainty, and meta-analytic aggregation across studies — none of it done here.

## What we take from it
A warning first, because it is the cheapest thing in the entry: our own effect tables are of exactly the kind this paper says are rarely tested. A probe that reports cross-validated accuracy on a trained model and no null is one line away from being a salmon, and the fix costs twenty forward passes of a randomised model and a permutation p-value — one column, `p_vs_random_init`, printed beside every score we publish. Second, a framing to adopt verbatim in our write-ups: state the query distribution, the admissible explanation class and the discrepancy *before* the result, because the paper's most useful move is that fixing the triple turns "is this a good explanation?" into a well-posed question and makes the remaining ambiguity explicit rather than embarrassing. Third, a distinction we have been blurring and should price: "this component has a large causal effect" is an identifiable estimand, while "this component is the mechanism" is not — the folder has entries on both sides of that line and this is the cleanest statement of why they are different claims. Fourth, a concrete reading rule for null results: the POS result shows a test against random computation can *preserve* a finding in the middle layers while killing it elsewhere, so a paper that reports only a global pass/fail has thrown away the most informative part; report layerwise (or regionwise) survival. What we should *not* take is the paper's pessimism about our own project: none of the non-identifiability results apply to a surrogate whose queries are interventional and whose outputs are compared to measured interventions, which is the part of the frame they say is identifiable — that is the subset our white-box model should be defined on.
