# Compute Optimal Inference and Provable Amortisation Gap in Sparse Autoencoders

- **Key:** arxiv-2411.13117
- **Authors / organisation:** Charles O'Neill; Alim Gumran; David Klindt — Australian National University; Nazarbayev University; Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory
- **Date:** 2025-01 (v2; v1 2024-11)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.13117
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Sparse autoencoders became the standard tool for lifting interpretable features out of superposed
activations, and the field's working assumption is that the extracted dictionary and the coefficient vector
the encoder hands back are one object described twice. The authors argue this is a modelling mistake: the
dictionary is learned by optimisation, but the coefficients are *predicted* by a single linear map plus a
pointwise nonlinearity, and that prediction step was never asked to be correct. Borrowing the vocabulary of
variational inference, they name the shortfall between an encoder's guess and the best achievable sparse
coefficient vector the amortisation gap, and they want to know whether it is a hyperparameter problem or an
architectural wall. The larger ambition is a comparative account of sparse encoding methods with compute
priced in, so that "more expressive encoder" is a measured choice rather than an article of faith.

## What they studied
Synthetic data with known ground-truth sources and a known dictionary, in three regimes — codes known,
dictionary known, neither known — plus a real-model arm on GPT-2 Small. The unit of analysis is the pair
(encoder map, decoder matrix), treated as separable: the *encoding* is scored by how well its coefficients
match the true latents, the *dictionary* by how well its columns match the true feature directions. Four
encoding strategies are compared under one decoder convention: a linear-plus-ReLU SAE, a one-hidden-layer MLP
with the same decoder, iterative sparse coding (L1 penalised, optimised per input), and an SAE whose learned
dictionary is kept but whose encoder is replaced by inference-time optimisation (SAE+ITO). All decoders are
column-normalised to unit norm so the sparsity penalty cannot be dodged by shrinking coefficient magnitudes.

## How they did it
Data are generated by drawing a random dictionary of N columns in M dimensions and sparse codes with exactly
K non-zeros, giving observations as the linear mixture; the pair is then held out for evaluation.
Default regime is N = 16 sources, M = 8 measurements and K = 3 active, with a scaled-up regime at N = 1000,
M = 200, K = 20 on 500,000 samples and a Zipf-weighted variant of the code distribution. In the codes-known
regime the task reduces to latent regression and the objective is one minus cosine against the truth; in the
dictionary-known regime the encoder is trained against a frozen true dictionary, while SAE+ITO instead runs
gradient descent on the coefficients for each input at inference time; in the fully unknown regime all four
methods run side by side, 2,048 samples split evenly train/test, five independent runs, 100,000 steps. Every
method is then placed on a compute axis by counting floating-point operations for forward, backward and
update, plus inference-time iterations, so that comparisons are made against total FLOPs as well as against
training steps. The real-model arm trains three methods on 406M OpenWebText tokens over GPT-2 Small layer-9
residual pre-activations (768 dimensions, 16,896 latents): a ReLU SAE, an MLP encoder with one 8,448-wide
hidden layer, and a locally competitive algorithm run with 100 gradient steps per batch. Interpretability is
scored by an automated pipeline in which one GPT-4o instance writes an explanation from the ten
top-activating tokens and the logit effects, and a second instance predicts activation on fresh labelled
examples, producing an F1 against the ground truth; 500 random features per method.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **decomposition with a proof attached**, on the encoder side, and a **comparative cost accounting** on the
method side. The headline object is a negative theorem — a rank argument showing that a single linear map
followed by an elementwise nonlinearity cannot invert a sparse projection for every admissible code once the
measurement dimension is below the source dimension, even when compressed sensing says recovery is possible.
The explanation of a failure is therefore not a description but a capacity bound: the pre-activation matrix is
a product that passes through M dimensions, so its rank cannot exceed M, and the required output of the
encoder has full rank N on the adversarial diagonal code set. The dictionary, by contrast, is treated as
explicable — it is a set of directions that can be compared with the truth entry by entry and whose mutual
overlap can be read off directly.

## How they judged success
Mean Correlation Coefficient (MCC) between learned and true quantities, computed after matching by the Hungarian
algorithm (or a greedy approximation when counts differ), reported separately for latent codes and for
dictionary columns. Everything is plotted against both training steps and total FLOPs, with the FLOP count
derived per method rather than approximated. The synthetic sweep varies N, M and K and overlays the
compressed-sensing recoverability boundary as a reference line, so the claim "sparse coding wins where theory
says recovery is possible" is a plotted comparison rather than an assertion. Sparsity-performance trade-offs
are shown as Pareto curves over the L1 coefficient λ, with multiple L0 thresholds for sparse coding because
its codes are never exactly zero. The real-model arm uses an automated-interpretability F1 distribution per
method over 500 features, with a Kruskal-Wallis test across methods and Dunn's post-hoc tests with Bonferroni
correction.

## What they found
The theorem holds in the regimes the authors care about: whenever K·log(N/K) ≤ M < N, an SAE with a
linear-nonlinear encoder has a strictly positive amortisation gap, and the proof of that impossibility is
outlined in the body and completed in an appendix. Empirically, when true codes are known, the widest MLP
reaches an MCC about 0.1 above the SAE at convergence, and all MLP widths cross the SAE's plateau at roughly
the same total FLOPs — reported as a computational threshold rather than a capacity effect. When the
dictionary is known the MLP stays nearly 10% above the SAE on MCC, and SAE+ITO, warm-started from SAE
latents, climbs in visible steps to the best score of the three. When neither is known, sparse coding beats
the field by more than 10% on latent MCC and, with the MLPs, about 10% on dictionary MCC, at a compute cost
comparable to the widest MLP. The gap widens at the larger synthetic scale (N = 1000, M = 200, K = 20), where
all MLPs overtake the SAE at about 3×10^14 FLOPs regardless of width. On GPT-2 layer 9, the MLP encoder
trained with the same sparsity penalty reaches normalised MSE 0.055 at mean L0 31.13 with 22% dead latents,
against the SAE's 0.061 at L0 35.66 with 11% dead; the LCA arm reaches 0.070 with an effective L0 near 18.56
after thresholding and no dead columns. Interpretability separates the same way: SAE and LCA features sit at a
median F1 near 0.6, MLP features at 0.83 with a tighter distribution, and the post-hoc tests put both the SAE
and the LCA significantly below the MLP. Inspecting decoder Gram matrices, sparse coding learns nearly
orthogonal columns while the SAE and MLP retain substantial off-diagonal overlap — superposition where there
was spare capacity for none.

## Limits and open problems they name
The LCA arm was not tuned for this scale, so its low interpretability score is explicitly attributed to
training rather than to the method. The MLP's advantage is left unexplained as a mechanism: the authors flag
it as requiring investigation rather than claiming to know why a more expressive encoder yields more
human-legible features. Synthetic data use constant sparsity and uncorrelated features, and the main body does
not model the varying feature importance that the toy-models-of-superposition framing supplies; the Zipf
appendix is described as a start. The comparison is with pre-top-k, pre-JumpReLU-style architectures, so
whether the gap survives modern sparse dictionaries is listed as future work, and the SAE+ITO arm used a
simpler optimiser than matching pursuit, which the authors say probably understates it. Traditional
dictionary learning (LARS) is run in an appendix and beats all the vanilla implementations, a result the
authors accept as a statement about how unoptimised their own baselines are.

## What we take from it
A **warning plus a measure**. The warning: any white-box account that treats a dictionary as
"the model's features" plus a single forward pass as "the model's activation of them" is quoting two objects
that provably cannot coincide in general, and the proof is short enough to restate — a rank bound on a
product through the measurement dimension, confronted with a full-rank diagonal code set. For our predictive
model this means the coefficient we read out is a function of the *encoder's* capacity budget, not a property
of the network, and a surrogate that predicts activations must be scored on inference quality separately from
dictionary quality. The measure: NCC/MCC against a matched ground truth with Hungarian matching, computed
separately for codes and for directions, and plotted against FLOPs rather than steps — that is the only
comparison axis on which "my method is better" and "my method got more compute" come apart. The real-model
arm also supplies a concrete result worth stealing for our evaluation design: a deeper encoder improved
automated-interpretability F1 by more than twenty points while slightly *improving* reconstruction, so on
this axis fidelity and legibility moved together — the opposite of what entries 445-453 in this folder
reported for dictionary-level fixes, which is a distinction worth carrying (encoder capacity versus
dictionary geometry) rather than a contradiction. And a cheap diagnostic that needs no ground truth:
visualise the decoder Gram matrix; near-identity means the dictionary is spending its spare dimensions on
orthogonal directions, large off-diagonals mean it is spending them on interference.
