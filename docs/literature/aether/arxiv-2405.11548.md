## What they wanted, and why

Almost every result in the interventional-design line this folder has collected quietly assumes that an intervention, once chosen, yields as much data as anyone wants — infinite samples from the interventional distribution. In a lab that is false: each randomised experiment costs money and yields a finite batch of subjects. The authors keep the design question from that line (how do I orient what observational data leaves ambiguous) but change the currency from "how many interventions may I run" to "how many interventional *samples* may I draw before I am confident". They cast this as an online learning problem in the style of bandit pure exploration: at each step pick one intervention, observe one sample, and stop once the posterior answer is good enough. The larger goal is the sample-cost economy of probing, which is the quantity none of the folder's earlier design entries price. Their stated headline is a terminating algorithm with a guarantee on expected samples, not a better estimator.

## What they studied

Discrete variables on a causally sufficient directed acyclic graph. The starting point is a *graph separating system* — a family of intervention sets whose cuts hit every undirected edge at least once — which the earlier literature established as sufficient for identifying a DAG in the worst case given unlimited data. The unit of analysis is the *action*: a specific intervention (target set plus realisation) drawn from that separating system. Intervention values are drawn from a finite domain per target, and each variable's observational conditional is assumed known exactly and faithful to the true graph. No neural networks are trained; the objects being compared are allocation policies over actions and the KL divergences between candidate interventional distributions.

## How they did it

Setup is in four steps. (1) From the observational distribution they recover the completed partially directed acyclic graph, and from it construct an (n,k)-separating system by the string-labelling construction of the earlier literature, so every target is small and the number of cut orientations stays bounded. (2) For each target set they enumerate *all* candidate interventional distributions compatible with the equivalence class: assign every orientation to the undirected edges leaving the target, propagate with Meek rules, and apply the causal-identification formula for maximally oriented partially directed graphs to each resulting graph; the claim is that the resulting set of distributions is in one-to-one correspondence with cut orientations, which is what makes the targets individually informative. (3) The online algorithm: the action space is the union of target realisations. Every action is forced once at the start; thereafter the algorithm tracks an allocation vector computed by an online convex optimisation (AdaHedge-style exponential weights) whose objective is the worst-case KL separation between the currently most probable graph and any competitor, then selects the action that is furthest behind its cumulative allocated share (allocation matching). A forced-exploration floor keeps the least-sampled action at root-t of the time so every empirical distribution keeps converging. (4) It stops when a KL-divergence confidence statistic, compared against a concentration bound for categorical distributions, drops below the requested delta. A practical variant splits the global problem into one local task per target (learn that cut's orientation) and allocates between targets in proportion to how slowly each local task is resolving, which avoids enumerating every DAG in the equivalence class but, as the authors note, can emit a set of orientations that is not itself a DAG. Analysis uses divergence decomposition, the Bretagnolle–Huber inequality and Wald's lemma to get an instance-dependent lower bound, then shows the exact algorithm's ratio of log(1/delta) to expected stopping time converges to that bound and the practical variant to a weaker constant computed per cut.

## What counts as an explanation here

Not a description of the system: the output is a *stopping rule plus a chooser*, and the "account" is the tuple (policy, stopping time, selection rule) together with a sample-count guarantee. The paper also produces a hard floor — an instance-dependent constant built from the best achievable mixture of KL separations — against which any sound algorithm's expected cost can be measured. Nothing here predicts an internal state; the predictive claim is about cost: "with probability at least 1−delta the returned graph is correct, and it takes about log(1/delta)/c samples to get there". It is one of the few places in the design line where the answer is a *budget with a proof attached* rather than a plan.

## How they judged success

Three quantities, precisely: the probability of returning the true DAG (claimed at least 1−delta, plus termination with probability one), the expected number of interventional samples normalised as log(1/delta) divided by expected stopping time (compared to the paper's own lower bound constant), and — experimentally — structural Hamming distance between learned and true graph as a function of samples drawn. Baselines are: random targets inside the separating system with one sample per step and independence tests on accumulated samples, the earlier adaptive greedy algorithm based on directed clique trees (Squires et al.), the adaptive sensitivity-search algorithm of Choo and Shiragur, and greedy interventional equivalence search as a regularised-maximum-likelihood interventional learner. Testbed: connected chordal DAGs sampled by a modified Erdős–Rényi scheme (random ordering, in-degree drawn from a truncated-and-floored binomial, parents from the preceding nodes, then chordalised by elimination in reverse order with the elimination algorithm), conditional probability tables drawn at random from the sampled DAG, and — as the semi-synthetic case — the SACHS protein-signalling network from bnlearn (they report its order and edge count as 13 and 17, where the SACHS paper cited in their references is the biological source of the data). For independence tests on finite samples they use the chi-square test from the Causal Discovery Toolbox. Each curve plots structural Hamming distance against interventional samples, with a band of two standard deviations either side of the mean.

## What they found

Track-and-stop sits below every baseline across the whole sample range on the synthetic graphs, and the *shape* of the gap is the result worth keeping: on complete graphs of 5, 6 and 7 vertices the number of samples the other algorithms need before their SHD falls is said to grow much faster with the vertex count, and on 10-vertex graphs at the three density settings the competing algorithms' SHD rises visibly with density while the authors' curve changes little. On the SACHS network the same ordering holds over the plotted range. The theory side supplies the two statements the algorithm's credibility rests on: both variants are *sound* in their definition (the probability of returning something other than the true DAG is at most the requested delta, and stopping time is finite with probability one), the exact variant's asymptotic sample cost matches the instance-dependent lower bound, and the practical variant matches a weaker constant computed target-by-target. Their appendix experiment on this point is the interesting one: starting from an *incorrect* CPDAG — which is what limited observational data would actually give — the algorithms no longer settle at zero SHD, and the authors' claim reduces to converging faster to a non-zero floor.

## Limits and open problems they name

The setting is the narrowest kind: discrete variables only, exact and faithful observational distribution taken as given, an actual density table. The practical variant's guarantee is weaker by construction and it can return a set of cut orientations that is not a valid DAG at all — their remedy is to lower delta and keep sampling, which is a workflow instruction rather than a fix. The exact variant's computational cost is exponential in the number of unoriented edges because it enumerates DAGs in the equivalence class, which is exactly why the practical variant exists. Sparse targets are bounded by the (n,k) construction rather than optimised. Observational-data error enters only as a starting point: nothing in the algorithm repairs a wrong equivalence class, and the mismatch is treated as a fixed floor the algorithm cannot see past. Cost per intervention is not modelled at all — the currency is samples per action — so a probe that is cheap per sample and expensive per experiment is not distinguished.

## What we take from it

A *measure*, a *policy*, and a *floor*. Measure: report a probing campaign as log(1/delta) divided by expected samples, so the cost of reaching a stated confidence is quoted instead of a raw draw count — exactly the denominator the earlier design entries in this folder lack. Policy: allocation matching — spend the next sample on whichever probe is furthest behind the share that the current best-account comparison says it deserves, with a root-t forced-exploration floor so no probe's estimate goes stale — is a cheap, model-free sampler over a fixed battery, and its forcing rule is the thing that makes the empirical estimates usable at all. Floor: the supremum over mixtures of the minimum KL separation between the true account and any competitor is computable from candidate accounts alone, and it is the right bar for any budget claim of ours, the same instinct as the vertex-cover floor of `arxiv-1810.11867` but denominated in samples. Two warnings carry over directly: a design sufficient in the infinite-data sense says nothing about sample cost, and confidence is only as good as the starting class — a wrong hypothesis is a ceiling the algorithm will never cross, which is why our own batteries need an explicit step that reopens the hypothesis rather than more samples of the same kind.
