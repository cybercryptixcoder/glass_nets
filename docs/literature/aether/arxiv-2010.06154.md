# On the Power of Abstention and Data-Driven Decision Making for Adversarial Robustness

- **Key:** arxiv-2010.06154
- **Authors / organisation:** Maria-Florina Balcan (CMU), Avrim Blum (TTIC), Dravyansh Sharma (CMU), Hongyang Zhang (TTIC)
- **Date:** 2020-10-13
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.06154
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (arXiv HTML v1: main body, Appendix A related work, Appendix C-V proofs, Appendix I Algorithm 3, Appendix J toy calculation, Appendix L experiments; references section read only as a citation list)
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

The paper asks what a classifier is allowed to do when the adversary is not norm-bounded but is free to move a point a very long way inside a randomly chosen low-dimensional slice of the feature space. Under that threat model the authors can prove something unusual: any classifier that must always name a class loses for at least one class of data, with probability approaching one half, no matter how well behaved the data distribution is or how good the embedding is. The remedy they propose and analyse is the ability to say "don't know," and the paper's larger ambition is to give a theory for the empirical line of work on rejecting or detecting adversarial inputs, which until then had only engineering evidence. This sits inside the field's broader interest in representation learning — they use contrastive learning as the concrete embedding family — and it is a step toward characterising which *output alphabets* are robust rather than which *network class* is.

## What they studied

No weights are analysed and no network is taken apart. The unit is the inference-time decision rule applied to an embedding: an arbitrary feature map `F` (a ResNet-18 in the experiments, 512-dimensional features) plus a nearest-training-neighbour classifier with a distance threshold τ and an outlier-removal parameter σ. The object under study is the triple (embedding, decision rule, adversary model) at the level of the whole model, and the adversary is a distribution over affine subspaces of dimension n₃ in the feature space, with unbounded perturbation magnitude within the chosen subspace. Data: derived CIFAR-10 512-d representations under SimCLR-style self-supervised contrastive learning and under supervised contrastive learning, each with an ordinary linear probe as the baseline.

## How they did it

Three moves. First, a hardness argument: model the adversary as picking a uniformly random unit direction and a distance, then reason about classification volume — for any classifier that partitions the space into at least two labels, some class occupies at most half of a large ball, so a randomly directed move of a moderate size lands in another class's region with probability near one half for a `1 - δ` share of that class's examples. Second, a positive result for the abstaining rule: a test point is labelled only if its nearest training embedding is within τ; otherwise the algorithm abstains. They show the adversarial win probability is bounded by roughly the ratio of a τ-ball's surface to the distance to the nearest differently-labelled training point, raised to the power `n₂ - n₃`, and they handle an arbitrary n₃-dimensional subspace by peeling it into n₃ successive random directions, each analysed by the one-dimensional argument, then taking a union bound over the m training points. Third, they attach two auxiliary guarantees: an abstention-rate guarantee under a covering assumption on the per-class feature distribution (with an alternative proof via doubling dimension), and a covariance-removal variant that drops training points sitting closer than σ to another class, which weakens the separation requirement. For setting τ, they treat the abstention-versus-error trade-off as an online problem and run a low-regret multiplicative-updates scheme over a discretised threshold grid, converting regret into a validation-set guarantee. Experiments evaluate the abstaining rule against an *exactly computed* worst-case adversary under this feature-subspace model (a convex program in n₃ dimensions with m constraints), plus a greedy projected-training-point attack used when n₃ is large enough to make exact computation impractical.

## What counts as an explanation here

A theorem-backed decision procedure plus an upper bound on its error, not a description of a model. The "explanation" of robustness is a closed-form expression in a small number of geometric quantities — the distance r from the test embedding to the nearest differently-labelled training embedding, the threshold τ, the feature dimension n₂, and the adversarial subspace dimension n₃ — and the claim is that this expression, and not the network's internals, governs whether the classifier can be fooled. It is a partial account by construction: it says nothing about which weights produce a large r, only that a large r is what buys robustness.

## How they judged success

Theoretical: probability of adversarial misclassification for classifiers without abstention (Theorem 4.1, in terms of `1/2 - δ`), the robust-error bound for the abstaining rule (Theorem 5.1 and its asymptotically improved form in Appendix C), the abstention-rate bound under the covering assumption (Theorem 5.3) and under a doubling-dimension assumption (Theorem G.3), the outlier-removal bound (Theorem E.1), a per-point threshold guarantee via an independent calibration set (Theorem I.1), and regret plus online-to-batch conversion for the threshold learner (Theorem 6.2, Corollary 6.3). Empirical: natural error and robust error at a fixed threshold, plus the abstention rate on natural data, all on CIFAR-10 with a linear probe as the no-abstention baseline and two thresholds τ = 3.0 and 2.0 at three outlier-removal settings. They also report the exact optimum of the trade-off objective in a two-cluster toy problem, as a function of cluster diameter, gap and sample count.

## What they found

The hardness result holds as stated: without abstention, every classifier is defeated on at least one class for a `1 - δ` share of examples at probability `1/2 - δ`, and the proof is a volume argument, not a construction tuned to any network. With abstention the picture inverts sharply. On supervised contrastive features with no outlier removal, the linear probe's robust error is 100% while the abstaining rule reports 60.5% at τ = 3.0 and 33.4% at τ = 2.0, paying 0.0% abstention in both cases because the margin is large enough that no natural point falls outside τ. Adding removal at σ = 0.9τ pushes the supervised robust error to 18.9%, and at σ = τ to 2.8% — but abstention on natural data stays at 10.6% in the last case, which is the price. Self-supervised features are worse behaved throughout: 40.7% robust error at τ = 3.0 with σ = 0, 9.4% at σ = 0.9τ with 12.9% abstention, and 1.2% at σ = τ with 33.4% abstention. In the large-subspace sweep, the untuned nearest-neighbour rule degrades with the adversarial dimension as expected (supervised adversarial accuracy 93.5% at n₃ = 1 down to 0.7% at n₃ = 200) while the abstaining variant holds 74.7% at n₃ = 200 — at 74.5% abstention among the points the plain rule gets wrong. The toy computation shows the optimal threshold scaling like `D log(βm)/m` in the sample size, and collapsing to zero below a sample-count cutoff, i.e. the right threshold is a function of data geometry and sample size rather than a constant of the method.

## Limits and open problems they name

The threat model is narrow: the adversary must move inside a random subspace, and the guarantee degrades exponentially in the subspace dimension, so at n₃ comparable to n₂ the bound is vacuous even though the empirical numbers stay respectable. The abstention-rate guarantee needs a covering-style assumption on the per-class feature distribution, and the outlier-removal bound needs a separation σ larger than twice τ — both are statements about the embedding, which the paper takes as given and does not learn toward. The threshold is tuned against a *proxy* misclassification count in the greedy regime, and the authors say so. There is no principled connection between the robustness guarantee and the training objective that produced the embedding; the loss they point at (a soft nearest-neighbour / InfoNCE form) is motivated by the theorem but not shown to optimise the quantity the theorem bounds. The paper also has no dedicated limitations section — the concessions are scattered through the discussion.

## What we take from it

Three things, all of them structural rather than numerical. (a) **A guard against reading robustness numbers without their decision rule.** Here the same embedding scores 100% robust error under one output alphabet and 2.8% under another, and nothing about the network changed — the difference is that the second classifier is allowed to decline. Any white-box account we build that predicts a label or a decision will be compared against baselines that are free to abstain, and the comparison is meaningless unless the output alphabets match. (b) **A concrete cheap measurement worth stealing:** for each test point compute the ratio of the distance to the nearest same-class training embedding over the distance to the nearest other-class embedding. The paper's own appendix reports this ratio is below one for 84.5% of points (self-supervised) and 94.3% (supervised) — that single per-point number is the empirical counterpart of the theorem's r, and it can be computed for any embedding with no retraining, which makes it a natural sanity check for the "is this embedding well-formed" question. (c) **A framing for prediction under intervention:** the paper's bounds are stated over a *distribution of interventions* (random subspaces) and not a worst case, and the theorem is only meaningful because of that — an expectation over intervention directions, with the worst case over perturbation magnitude. That is precisely the shape our own predictions should take if we are to say anything quantitative, and it is run 653's lesson (a reference that is itself measurable) arriving from the theory side.
