## What they wanted, and why
Two of the most-cited observations about attention in pretrained language models — induction heads forming at a loss-curve bump, and trained models dumping attention on the first token — are usually described in the same vocabulary, and are sometimes written up as one "attention emergence" event. This paper asks the developmental question directly: over the course of pretraining, when does each of them actually appear, and are they the same transition? The larger ambition is to give a mechanistic theory of sinks a set of constraints it must satisfy — where sinks can never form, where in the stack they form first, and how many formation shapes a single phenomenon can take — because a theory that only describes a final checkpoint has nothing to predict about a training run. The author positions this as the activation-space half of a two-part programme whose parameter-space half tracks the spectral geometry of weight matrices.

## What they studied
Three 1B-class open checkpoints chosen so that two of their differences isolate one variable each: Pythia 1B (dense GPT-NeoX, 16 layers × 8 heads, The Pile, 300B tokens), OLMo 1B-0724-hf (dense Llama-style, 16 layers × 16 heads, DCLM, 3048B tokens) and OLMoE 1B-7B-0924 (mixture-of-experts, 1B active of 7B total, 64 experts with top-8 routing, DCLM, 5117B tokens). Pythia versus OLMo swaps the corpus at fixed scale and dense architecture; OLMo versus OLMoE swaps dense for MoE at fixed corpus. Ten log-spaced published revisions per model (eight for OLMoE, for cost) give a 30-run panel. The unit of analysis is the (layer, head) pair, characterised two ways: a participation-ratio (PR) statistic on each head's attention output across a batch, and a selectivity ratio on its attention pattern to canonical target positions for six classes (induction, previous-token, duplicate-token, first-token/BOS, self, local).

## How they did it
Each head's attention output at a fixed query position is collected over a synthetic batch of 2,000 sequences of length 256 built from a filler–A–B–filler–A template with random vocabulary ids, giving a matrix whose singular spectrum yields an effective dimension (PR as the exponential of the spectral entropy). The per-revision quantity plotted is the amount by which PR exceeds one, and a per-head trajectory score integrates that excess over log-token-time. Separately, each head is scored by how much of its attention goes to the canonical target position of each class relative to a uniform-other baseline: a class assignment at 30×, circuit admission at 50× for induction and BOS and 100× for previous-token. Because the BOS class comes to dominate (52–81% of all heads by the end), a second "all-head capability-specific" screen is used that takes every head whose target-class selectivity clears the threshold regardless of its best class. Circuits identified by the screen are verified by group-ablation of the per-head slice at the attention output projection against same-layer matched-random controls (4–8 seeds), scored by top-1 accuracy on the synthetic induction batch. Pythia's earliest revisions need fp32 because attention values at random initialisation fall below fp16 range. One extra measurement probes a bridge to weight space: the singular spectrum of each head's output-projection slice, summarised by stable rank, top singular value and the participation ratio of singular values, correlated with the activation-side PR across 640 heads. A null-selectivity calibration (500 random non-special target draws) is recomputed at five revisions to test whether a threshold set at the final checkpoint stays valid during training.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not a circuit diagram and not a feature dictionary: the paper's product is a set of **developmental constraints and a measurement recipe**. The recipe is a two-step pipeline (spectral PR as a general flag for specialised computation, then class-specific selectivity to say which specialisation), which the author is explicit is necessary in the sink-dominated 1B regime and sufficient alone only in smaller, sink-free models. The constraints are the falsifiable part: a first two layers that never hold a sink, a mid-layer-outward order of first appearance, an induction-transition/ sink-transition separation of an order of magnitude on DCLM, and a single corpus delivering two different formation shapes under two architectures. It is predictive about timing and location only — the author offers a qualitative "residual-stream null-space" sketch for the floor and says plainly it is not developed quantitatively.

## How they judged success
First revision crossing for each of seven token-count milestones (three induction head-count levels, four BOS-class fractions, previous-token and self thresholds). Recall of the final-checkpoint induction circuit at intermediate revisions (33%, 67% and 100% recall, and the training fraction at which 67% is reached). Jaccard overlap between the formation-revision circuit and the final circuit, swept across screen thresholds from 30× to 100×. Causal verification by group ablation against matched-random same-layer controls, reported as the accuracy change in points and as an effect standardised against the seed distribution of the controls, with the low final-checkpoint synthetic accuracy (4–5% top-1) flagged as the reason a multi-seed control distribution is required. Null-selectivity p99 versus null-selectivity max, recomputed per revision, as the test of threshold stability. Spearman correlations for the weight-space bridge, with the author stating the scope limit (general computation level, not capability level) in the same sentence as the numbers.

## What they found
The most load-bearing result is a floor. Layers 0 and 1 produce **zero** BOS-classified heads at every sampled revision of all three models, from random initialisation through trillions of tokens — the separation Xiao et al. reported for one final 7B checkpoint is present from the first checkpoint onward and is treated as an architectural rather than a learned outcome. Where sinks do appear inverts the naive bottom-up picture: they crystallise in mid-to-late layers and spread outward, while the layer just above the floor lags badly or never participates — in Pythia no head in L0–L3 is ever BOS-classified, in OLMo layer 2 first does so about 26× later in tokens than layer 4, and in OLMoE layer 2 arrives after more than a hundredfold longer than the mid-layer sinks. Formation shape is not a property of the sink but of the (data, architecture) pair: Pythia ramps smoothly, OLMo jumps from 7.4% to 70.3% of heads between two adjacent published checkpoints, and OLMoE — same DCLM corpus as OLMo, but MoE — ramps smoothly again, and also saturates lower. On the two DCLM models the induction circuit is complete at 20–23B tokens while the BOS class reaches half of all heads at 264B and 419B, an 11.5× and 21× separation, so the paper's headline is that these are two transitions and not one. The screen recovers two-thirds of the final induction circuit within 0.3–2% of total training. PR is elevated at or before the selectivity threshold crossing for every final-checkpoint induction head sampled (in the worked example, PR rises an order of magnitude by about 0.5B tokens, roughly twelvefold before selectivity crosses 50×), but the author states that ranking by the PR integral alone in this regime surfaces BOS and generic early-layer heads ahead of the induction circuit. The turnover finding is the strongest: screening every head independently, the formation-time circuit overlaps the final circuit by only about a third (Jaccard 0.29–0.33, stable across thresholds), and group ablation shows the fate of each decaying head decides whether it was retired or merely moved — heads that became sinks go inert (Pythia's decay-outs end at +0.3 points against random controls, z = +1.0), while heads that became duplicate-token heads stay causal (OLMoE's L6·H1, collapsed out of the induction screen, still costs 3.1 points, z = −5.5), and a head that was never an induction head at all but only a duplicate detector is causally necessary for induction (L3·H15, −3.2 points against −0.48 for matched-random controls). So the selectivity screen both over-includes transient heads and under-includes the duplicate-detection half of the circuit. Null-selectivity p99 stays in a narrow band across training while null-selectivity max wanders by two orders of magnitude, so the threshold should be carried by the percentile, not the maximum. The weight-space bridge is modest and the author says so: pooled Spearman +0.32 between activation PR and output-projection stable rank, with no relation to induction selectivity (all |ρ| ≤ 0.29).

## Limits and open problems they name
Checkpoint resolution: ten log-spaced revisions is what HuggingFace publishes, and the Pythia co-emergence of induction with the 10% BOS milestone at 6B may be two transitions at finer granularity, while OLMo's "sharp" jump is only sharp relative to the between-checkpoint gap. A single seed per model, with no within-architecture re-pretraining at this scale, so the findings describe these specific runs, not the configuration in expectation. The fate mechanism has only one model exhibiting the induction-to-duplicate transition, so the author cannot say whether it is a property of MoE, of the data, of scale, or of that run, and OLMo therefore cannot serve as a dense-DCLM control for it. The route from early emergences to late ones is unmodelled too: the paper measures that a head's PR rises before its capability selectivity, and separately measures the activation/weight spectral association, but it does not say what the early PR elevation is a forecast *of*, beyond "specialised computation of some kind" — the case where a head peaks in PR and then decays to a sink is exactly the case its own data contains, and no account is offered of why the same early signal precedes both outcomes. Selectivity ratios are computed on a synthetic template batch, with natural-text validation exercised at the final checkpoint only. The class taxonomy is not exhaustive and has no composed tasks such as IOI. The MoE forward pass costs about twice a dense model at equal nominal size, which is why OLMoE gets eight revisions instead of ten.

## What we take from it
Three things to adopt. First, the **L0/L1 zero-BOS floor is a free null for any sink claim**: if a white-box model of ours predicts where sinks will sit, it must predict that the two bottom layers never host one, at any point in training, across corpora and architectures — and a prediction that places a sink in layer 0 or 1 is falsified without an ablation. Second, **a formation-time circuit is not the final circuit**, so whenever we identify a mechanism at one checkpoint and describe it as the mechanism, we owe a Jaccard overlap between the two screens and a per-head fate for everything that drops out; the fate distinction (retired into a sink versus slid within the circuit into the duplicate step) is a cheaper and sharper test than the aggregate ablation, because the same ablation result means opposite things under the two fates. Third, **the same statistic must be read at the same revision grid across the models being compared**: the difference between a sharp transition and a gradual ramp here is a property of the (data, architecture) pair, so any cross-model claim we make about a curve's shape has to carry the checkpoint spacing it was measured on, or it is a claim about checkpoint publication. Warning worth carrying: the Pythia-versus-OLMo and OLMo-versus-OLMoE contrasts are designed one-variable-at-a-time and the paper still refuses to read them causally when the three models have one seed each — a discipline we should copy rather than outdo.
