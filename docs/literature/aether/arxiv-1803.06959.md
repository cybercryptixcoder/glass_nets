# On the importance of single directions for generalization

- **Key:** arxiv-1803.06959
- **Authors / organisation:** Ari S. Morcos, David G.T. Barrett, Neil C. Rabinowitz, Matthew Botvinick — DeepMind, London, UK
- **Date:** 2018-05-22 (v4; v1 2018-03-19, v2 2018-05-15, v3 2018-05-21; ICLR 2018 conference paper)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1803.06959
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

Two literatures had grown up side by side without touching. One asks why some networks generalize and others merely memorize, and answers with properties of the *loss landscape* (flatness, PAC-Bayes bounds, the information in the weights). The other is the practice of reading networks by their single units — the neuron that fires for faces, the sentiment unit, the coordinate direction whose activity image looks like a car. The first literature treats generalization as a property of the whole parameter vector; the second treats individual directions in activation space as if they were the carriers of meaning. The authors ask the question that connects them: does a network's *reliance* on single directions tell you anything about whether it will generalize, and does a direction's *selectivity* tell you whether the network needs it? The answer to the first is yes and to the second is no, and the paper is careful about which of the two is the finding. This is the paper whose class selectivity index the later causal audit in this folder builds on, so it is the source document for that argument rather than a companion to it.

## What they studied

Three image classifiers: a two-hidden-layer MLP (widths 128, 512, 2048, 2048) on MNIST; an 11-layer convolutional network (channels 64 through 512, all 3x3 kernels) on CIFAR-10; and a 50-layer ResNet (top-5 accuracy) on ImageNet. Analysis of the convolutional models is at the level of the **feature map**, with its activity for an input taken as the mean over the map; the MLP is analysed unit by unit. The central quantity is a *single direction in activation space*, defined broadly as one unit, one feature map, or any linear combination of them — so the paper's claims are stated for the whole class of coordinate directions and random mixtures of them, though in practice only the coordinate-aligned members are ablated. The property measured on a direction is its **class selectivity index**: the difference between the highest class-conditional mean activation and the mean over the other classes, divided by their sum, hence in [0, 1] with 0 for a class-agnostic direction. Datasets are varied along one axis — the fraction of training labels randomly shuffled, in the style of Zhang et al. — to produce a controlled ladder from structure-finding to memorizing.

## How they did it

The measurement instrument is a **cumulative ablation curve**. Take a trained network, order its directions randomly, clamp the activity of the first k of them to zero, and record accuracy; sweep k from none to all. The curve must start at training accuracy and end at chance, and its *shape* is the read-out: a network that has spread its computation broadly degrades slowly, one that has concentrated it in a few directions falls off a cliff. For conv models only the last three layers' maps are ablated in the corrupted-label experiment; for the MLP every unit in every layer is. Because the choice of ordering adds variance, every curve is averaged over ten random orderings. A parallel, rotation-sensitive variant adds Gaussian noise to *all* units with variance scaled (per unit, per element) by the empirical training-set variance, which perturbs along random bases and so tests reliance on directions that are not axis-aligned.

With the instrument in hand the experiments form a ladder of increasing control over confounds. (1) Train each architecture on datasets with graded fractions of shuffled labels and compare ablation curves — but here memorizing is *forced* by the data, so a difference could be a property of the task rather than the solution. (2) Therefore train **200 identically-specified CIFAR-10 networks** on the *unmodified* dataset, differing only in initialization, data order and learning rate, and test whether reliance on single directions still tracks generalization — this is the experiment that removes the corrupted-label confound. (3) Turn the read-out into a predictor: measure the area under the ablation curve (AUC) over the course of training a single MLP and see where it turns, and over a hyperparameter sweep (96 settings, two repeats each, so 192 models) and see whether it ranks models the way held-out accuracy does. (4) Intervene on training to see whether regularizers move the quantity — dropout at 0.1/0.2/0.3 on corrupted and clean MNIST, and batch normalization on/off for CIFAR-10. (5) Finally, test the selectivity-implies-importance assumption directly: ablate each direction, record the change in test loss, and correlate that impact against the direction's class selectivity, both pooled and per layer, with mutual information substituted as a control metric and the filter weight L1 norm as a pruning-literature benchmark.

One methodological detail is load-bearing and reported in an appendix: clamping a direction to **zero** is *less* damaging than clamping it to the empirical mean activation, so the paper ablates to zero, matching pruning practice. The authors note this rather than dwell on it, but it means the ablation curves understate how much a direction contributes on average.

## What counts as an explanation here

Not a circuit, not a dictionary, not a mechanism. The form of understanding is a **causal-role claim about a single direction**, established by intervention and stated as a scalar relationship: reliance-on-single-directions, measured by the area under an ablation curve, covaries with generalization; class-selectivity does not covary with importance. The selectivity index itself is the predecessor of the entry's central object — a neuroscience-style tuning statistic ported to networks, justified here as a measure of how discriminable classes are *along a given direction*, with the explicit caveat that it is not an information measure (a unit carrying a little information about every class scores low). The paper says outright that the mutual information results are reported precisely because MI, unlike selectivity, rewards multi-class information. So the explanation offered is partial and comparative: it ranks directions and ranks networks, it does not describe a computation.

## How they judged success

Reliance is quantified as the **normalized area under the cumulative ablation curve**, with error bars as standard deviation across unit orderings (and across model instances where several are trained). The generalization-reliance relationship is read from a scatter of AUC against generalization error over 200 networks. The model-selection claim is judged by **Spearman correlation** between AUC and test loss/accuracy, and by the practical test of subsampling 48 of 96 hyperparameter settings and asking how often AUC picks a top-1/top-5/top-10 setting, together with the accuracy gap between the AUC-best and the truly-best model. The selectivity-importance claim is judged by Spearman correlation between a direction's selectivity and its ablation impact, computed pooled across units and separately within layers (the per-layer breakdown being the control that isolates the depth confound), with the same analysis repeated using mutual information and compared against the filter L1 norm. Comparisons between network families (BN vs no-BN, dropout fractions, corrupted vs clean labels) are visual and qualitative on the ablation curves rather than tested.

## What they found

**The key numbers, as printed.** Over the 200 CIFAR-10 networks the best-generalizing five are markedly more robust to cumulative ablation than the worst five, and the AUC-versus-error plot shows networks sitting in separated clusters rather than a smooth line — the authors flag that this clustering may reflect the optimization procedure's discrete set of solutions, and that within one cluster the correlation is actually negative, so the relationship is *not* monotone across the whole population. Over training, the AUC begins to fall at the same epoch at which train and test loss diverge, and AUC is negatively correlated with test loss (Spearman −0.728). Across the hyperparameter sweep AUC is strongly related to test accuracy (Spearman 0.914), and random 48-of-96 subsampling picks a top-1 setting 13% of the time, a top-5 83%, and a top-10 98%, with the chosen model within 1 ± 1.1 points of the optimum.

**Regularizers.** Dropout buys robustness only up to the dropout fraction it was trained with: past that number of removed directions, networks trained on fully corrupted labels fall away much faster than those trained on clean labels, exactly as the redundancy-copying argument predicts, while clean-label networks at different dropout fractions are indistinguishable. Batch normalization, by contrast, makes CIFAR-10 networks consistently and substantially more robust to cumulative ablation. It also *lowers* the distribution of class selectivity across feature maps while *raising* mutual information — the paper's proposed mechanism for how BN might help generalization, namely by pushing information out of single maps and into multi-class mixtures.

**Selectivity versus importance — the negative result.** Class selectivity is a poor predictor of a direction's importance throughout. In the MNIST MLP the correlation is positive but negligible (Spearman 0.095), with many highly selective units having almost no impact when ablated. In the CIFAR-10 network the correlation is *negative* (Spearman −0.428), and the per-layer breakdown shows this is driven almost entirely by early layers, with later layers showing no relationship at all. In the ImageNet ResNet, ablating highly selective maps is no more impactful than ablating unselective ones. Mutual information, substituted as the competing metric, is likewise a poor predictor, with minor exceptions in the very earliest and latest CIFAR layers. Class selectivity also fails to correlate with the filter L1 norm — uncorrelated in ImageNet, negatively correlated in CIFAR-10 — so it is not a proxy for the quantity pruning uses. Separately, and consistently across all three models, **early layers are more important than late ones**: ablating early maps hurts more. And class selectivity rises with depth, matching the concept-selectivity observations in the network-dissection literature.

## Limits and open problems they name

All three of the paper's headline relationships are *empirical and unproven*; the mechanism sketch (a memorizing network has a longer description length, therefore uses more of its capacity, therefore uses more directions) is offered as intuition, with an explicit assumption that the memorizing network uses a non-negligible fraction of its capacity. The population-level clustering in the 200-network experiment is unexplained and contains a sign reversal within clusters, so "reliance predicts generalization" is a between-regime statement, not a monotone law. The mutual information control is described as less consistent than selectivity (with some relationship visible in the extreme layers of CIFAR-10), which weakens the claim that the failure is specific to selectivity rather than to single-direction statistics generally. Only *class* selectivity is measured — feature selectivity, which is what most mechanistic interpretability actually means by a selective unit, is explicitly not examined, and the authors say it is unclear whether the conclusion transfers. Only in-distribution generalization is studied; shift is named as future work. The AUC predictor is demonstrated on MNIST and CIFAR-10 only, with the viability on harder data left open. The paper also concedes that its own negative result does not show there are no important directions, nor that important directions are unpredictable — only that selectivity is not a good predictor of importance.

## What we take from it

**A measure, and a warning about it.** The class selectivity index is (μ_max − μ_rest)/(μ_max + μ_rest), computed on class-conditional means, averaged over a feature map's spatial extent for conv channels. Two things to import with it: the index is a *discriminability* statistic, not an information statistic — a direction with a little signal about every class scores near zero — so it systematically under-reports distributed coding, which is exactly the regime the paper finds generalizing networks occupy. And the paper's own downward result on the index is the reason the causal audit in this folder had to intervene on training rather than ablate a trained net: **ablation of a direction answers "what happens if this is removed now", not "was this needed to learn the solution", and redundancy makes the two questions diverge.** Coreference `arxiv-2003.01262`, which is this paper's own next step — it takes the index, puts it in the loss with a signed scale, and finds the property is neither necessary nor sufficient.

**A method: the cumulative ablation curve as a shape statistic.** Single-unit ablation gives a number that redundancy makes uninformative; the *curve* of accuracy against the number of directions removed gives a curve whose area is a coarse measure of how concentrated the network's computation is. Two implementation choices from this paper decide whether the number means anything: ablate to zero rather than to the mean activation (the gentler option, so the curves are conservative), and average over several random orderings, because the ordering variance is large. This is the ancestor of the reliance measure the regularizer-based audit replaces with a training-time knob.

**A pattern, and the sharpest version of it in this folder: selectivity is a description of tuning, not a measure of use.** A direction can be maximally class-selective and contribute nothing when removed — 0.095 pooled correlation in the MLP, outright *negative* in the CIFAR-10 conv net, concentrated in early layers where directions are most important. For our probing work this is a direct warning about a move that is easy to make: fitting a probe, finding that a direction cleanly separates classes, and inferring that the network represents the class *there*. Discriminability and necessity are different properties, and the correlation between them has been measured — it is negligible to negative. Every "direction D is the concept" claim needs a second instrument that asks about use, not about separation. Coreference `arxiv-2310.17611` (a cosine score used as evidence of a feature's presence — the separation side of the same gap) and `arxiv-1704.05796` (concept selectivity across depth, where the paper notes the count-versus-fraction confound that makes cross-architecture comparisons unsafe).

**A layer-localisation lesson that recurs across this folder.** Three times over the paper finds the effect largest in *early* layers: ablations there hurt most, the selectivity-importance anticorrelation is driven by early layers, and the later layers show no relationship. This is the same direction as the causal audit's first-three-layers result, and it is a counterweight to the habit of reading interpretability conclusions off final layers — the directions that actually carry load sit earlier, where selectivity statistics are lowest.

**A cheap predictor worth knowing about and policing.** Area under the ablation curve correlates strongly with test accuracy across a hyperparameter sweep (0.914) and turns at the epoch where overfitting begins (−0.728 over training) — and AUC needs no held-out labels, only training data and a forward pass. That is a genuinely useful instrument for model selection. The policing is this: the 200-network scatter contained both a visible regime structure and a within-cluster sign reversal, so a single correlation coefficient over a heterogeneous population of networks would have been misleading. Report the relation as a scatter with clusters named, not as one Spearman number.
