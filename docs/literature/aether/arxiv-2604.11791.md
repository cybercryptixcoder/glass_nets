## What they wanted, and why

Looped ("recurrent-depth") language models spend extra test-time compute by re-running a fixed block of layers, and they reason better — but almost nothing was known about what the loop actually computes, and in particular whether an explanation written for a feedforward stack still applies when the same weights are applied eight or thirty-two times. The authors take the "stages of inference" picture from feedforward interpretability (depth-wise changes in what attention does, chiefly how much it mixes information across positions) and ask the structural question: with weight tying, can layer-wise specialisation survive repetition at all? Their motivation is honest about the stakes — if depth-stage explanations break under looping, then either the field's depth-based accounts are about something other than effective depth, or looped models need a different vocabulary. The larger goal is to turn a mechanistic observation into architectural guidance for people designing recurrent-depth models.

## What they studied

Five pretrained looped models plus two families of small models trained from scratch. Pretrained: Ouro 1.4B (24 recurrent layers, no prelude/coda, trained from random init), Huginn-0125 3.5B (2 prelude, 4 recurrent, 2 coda, with input injection), and retrofitted Llama-3.2-1B, OLMo-2-0425-1B and TinyLlama (recurrence retrofitted into a pretrained stack, 6 or 8 layers in the recurrent block, input injection on). Behaviour is measured on a fixed subset of 256 GSM8k test examples, with HellaSwag as a non-reasoning replication. The unit of analysis is the **latent state after each layer at each recurrence**, i.e. a (layer, recurrence) grid — this is the entry's key methodological move, because it converts a depth axis into a two-dimensional one. Architectural variables: norm placement (pre-norm versus sandwich, residual-normalised versus sublayer-normalised), block size, prelude/coda presence, and input injection.

## How they did it

They first establish the limiting behaviour. Two propositions do the work. The first: if a cyclic block reaches a fixed point, then every cyclic shift of its layers reaches *a* fixed point too (so per-layer convergence is consistent with the block as a whole tracing a cycle rather than collapsing to one state). The second: because the block's weights are tied across recurrences and its inputs are bounded by the pre-attention norm, the map from latent state to attention pattern is Lipschitz with constant proportional to the product of the layer's QK matrices, so small per-recurrence state changes imply small attention-pattern changes. They then measure: per-recurrence residual-stream difference norms and cosines, Frobenius norms between realised attention matrices at every pair of layers (the "diagonal" test), and the distance of each layer's state from an approximate fixed point defined as that layer's state after 128 recurrences. They classify each token's limiting behaviour with a small spectral heuristic — detrend the cosine-to-final series, Hann-window it, largest non-DC FFT bin gives an "orbit" if its amplitude clears a threshold, a positive linear slope gives a "slider" — and report the rates. For the stage analysis they reapply the same (layer, recurrence) decomposition to a mixing statistic (normalised entropy of the column-summed attention matrix, "ColSum concentration") plus sink rate and row-entropy mixing score, and compare the shape across one recurrence of the loop against a feedforward control. They then reproduce the whole study on randomly initialised 12-layer models across norm and injection variants, and finally pretrain 512-dimensional models from scratch with a constant four recurrences, a single final-state loss, and no training-time bias toward feedforward behaviour, against a depth-12 feedforward control.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **decomposition of where computation lives in depth**, not a mechanism: "the middle stages of a feedforward stack are performed inside each pass of the recurrent block, and the first and last stages once, by the prelude and coda". It is partial and explicitly shape-level — the object compared is a scalar time series (mixing concentration) over the (layer, recurrence) grid, and the claim that a model "has stages" is a claim about the shape of that curve, not about any head or circuit. The fixed-point results are structural statements about the map the block implements. There is no prediction of behaviour under intervention anywhere in the paper; the predictive content is instead about *stability*: which architectures will keep the same stage structure when run for more recurrences than they were trained on.

## How they judged success

Success is qualitative-shape agreement plus convergence diagnostics: does the per-layer attention pattern at recurrence t match itself at t+1 more closely than it matches other layers (the diagonal pattern in the Frobenius-norm matrix); does the distance to the approximate fixed point go to zero; does the ColSum-concentration trajectory within one loop resemble the feedforward control's trajectory across depth; does it hold for 128 recurrences rather than the training-time count. Controls are the important part: randomly initialised models as a null for "training learned this", feedforward control stacks of matched total depth, a padded system prompt as a length-matched control for the persona prompt, an ablation that zeroes the MLP in the layer responsible for a model's massive activations, and five different pretrained models with different attribution lineages (from scratch, retrofitted, upcycled). No statistics, no confidence intervals, no held-out quantitative benchmark — this is a descriptive paper and the appendix says so.

## What they found

**Fixed points are real but not universal.** Huginn-0125 and the retrofitted models converge quickly to a distinct fixed point *per layer*, so the block traces a consistent cyclic trajectory; Ouro does not reach a fixed point even though successive differences are small — the authors exploit exactly that gap to separate the two claims. In randomly initialised models, input injection produces stable per-layer fixed points for every norm type tried except Ouro's own, and without injection only pre-norm converges — but to a *degenerate* fixed point where every layer lands on the same state (cosine similarity between the first layer's fixed point and the least similar other layer's still reaches one). Since this appears in untrained models, the cyclic behaviour is attributed to the architecture, not to what training learned. **Looping buys surprising speed**: for Ouro attention patterns look converged after the first iteration, and for Huginn and retrofitted Llama immediately after the prelude.

**Non-fixed-point behaviour is rare and prompt-dependent.** Only about 0.02% of tokens show orbits or sliders with no system prompt, and about 0.14% with the long persona prompt used by the Huginn authors — and a length-matched padding prompt produces fewer of them than the persona prompt, so it is not just sequence length. At the example level, 2.81% of examples show non-fixed-point behaviour at least once on a question token under the long persona prompt versus 0.76% with no prompt. The authors flag that the absolute numbers depend on hand-chosen classifier hyperparameters and that only the ordering of these rates is robust.

**Stages of inference are mirrored, not created.** ColSum concentration within each loop repeats the familiar feedforward shape: a rapid change in the early block layers, a middle regime of low mixing, a late return. Crucially, (i) Ouro, trained from scratch, reproduces Llama-like stages, (ii) the retrofitted models reproduce *their own base model's* stages, with the first and last stages happening once in prelude and coda while the middle stages repeat, and (iii) Ouro 2.6B, built by duplicating the 1.4B's 24 layers into 48, shows the stage pattern twice — once per half — i.e. the upcycling duplicated the stages as well. **Huginn-0125 is the negative case**: it shows no clear stages at all, and the authors attribute this to its norm placement (it normalises the residual stream rather than the sublayer outputs, preventing the growth in residual magnitude that they say compression and sink formation require). An ablation that zeroes the MLP producing retrofitted Llama's massive activations removes its stages, which supports the same explanation.

**Stability predicts extrapolation.** Run for 128 recurrences, fixed-point models hold their per-layer mixing values constant while Ouro's drift continuously; the authors read this against published results that Ouro degrades when run beyond its trained recurrence count while Huginn's performance is reported flat. This is the paper's one forward-looking claim and it is correlation across two published model cards, not a controlled test.

## Limits and open problems they name

They analyse only cyclic recurrence, not the sequential multi-block setting; they do not establish analytically why stable limiting behaviour arises; and they explicitly do not know whether fixed-point convergence is *desirable* for reasoning or a restriction on it — a converged loop computes the same thing forever. The from-scratch stage experiments are 512-dimensional, 3.7B-token runs, and the authors themselves say they must be treated with caution; the appendix adds that adding input injection there makes the *final* recurrence resemble feedforward stages but the earlier ones less so, which is a mild tension with the fixed-point argument for injection. Everything is descriptive: no intervention on the loop, no probe, no circuit.

**What I add, and it matters for how the folder should read this:** the stage metric is a single shape statistic averaged over heads, sequences and a 256-example subset, and stage claims are made by eye from curves the reader cannot see in the HTML — the paper never states a decision rule for "this model has stages", so the difference between Ouro and Huginn rests on the authors' visual judgement rather than a threshold. Second, the fixed-point notion is per-token per-layer with an approximate fixed point taken from the 128th recurrence, so "converged" means "the trajectory is close to where it will end up at step 128", which cannot distinguish a genuine fixed point from a very slow drift. Third, every stage claim is under-determined by one statistic: ColSum concentration, row-entropy mixing, sink rate and residual entropy do not agree everywhere (Ouro's residual entropy departs from the feedforward shape while its attention statistics match it), so "mirrors feedforward stages" is true of the attention-based metrics specifically.

## What we take from it

**A framing and a measurement template.** (1) Recurrence makes a model into a two-axis grid — *which layer* and *which pass* — and every read-out in our project should be reported on both axes rather than compressed into depth; the single most transferable result is that per-layer convergence plus weight tying implies near-constant attention patterns across recurrences, so for a looped model an attribution graph has to be a graph over (layer, recurrence) or it silently assumes the repeated edges are distinct. (2) An untrained-model control is again doing the decisive work: the cyclic fixed-point structure appears without training, which is exactly the class of null the folder keeps insisting on — a structural pattern that survives random initialisation cannot be evidence that a task was learned, and this paper converts that into a design constraint rather than a caveat. (3) The cheap diagnostic is one line of linear algebra: perturb the state, re-run one block, and check whether the map's Lipschitz constant (here, the QK product times a bound on the state norm) is small enough for the loop to be treated as a contraction — that is what predicts whether a model keeps its stage structure at unseen recurrence counts, and it is a weight-only computation we can do without any forward passes. (4) For a predictive white-box model this paper is a warning about *what depth means*: if the middle stages repeat inside the block, then "layer 18 does X" is not a statement about effective computation in a looped model, and any predictor trained on feedforward stacks inherits an assumption that a converging loop actively violates at large recurrence counts. (5) A borrowed metric can quietly carry its conclusions: ColSum concentration was built to detect attention sinks, and the authors' own residual-entropy results diverge from their ColSum results on the one model with a different norm scheme, which is the folder's standing lesson about one number deciding one story.
