# Fine-Grained Erasure in Text-to-Image Diffusion-based Foundation Models

- **Key:** arxiv-2503.19783
- **Authors / organisation:** Kartik Thakral, Tamar Glaser, Tal Hassner, Mayank Vatsa, Richa Singh — IIT Jodhpur; Glaser at Harman International; Hassner at Weir AI
- **Date:** 2025-03-25 (v1, single version)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.19783
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 1 (background)

## What they wanted, and why
Unlearning a single concept from an image generator is supposed to be local: remove the target and leave everything else working. In practice the neighbours break too — erase one dog breed and the adjacent breeds come out worse — and the authors name this collateral damage *adjacency*. Their goal is to turn "stay local" from a hoped-for side effect into a stated training objective with its own data split, so that a method can be scored on erasure and on neighbour preservation at once rather than on erasure alone. This sits in the safety/editing branch of interpretability rather than the explanation branch: nothing here reads the model, it edits it and then measures the edit's blast radius.

## What they studied
Stable Diffusion v1.4, edited through LoRA adapters they call Mesh Modules (low-rank updates, original weights frozen). The unit of analysis is the prompt embedding as it reaches the cross-attention layers — concept behaviour is whatever the noise prediction conditioned on a prompt does. Data are fine-grained image sets where neighbours are genuinely close: Stanford Dogs, Oxford Flowers, Caltech-UCSD Birds, and ImageNet-1k for the fine-grained protocol; Imagenette and the I2P prompt set for the coarse-grained protocol. There is no probe, no feature dictionary and no internal read-out anywhere in the paper.

## How they did it
They first build an adjacency set for each target concept without a taxonomy: generate a batch of images per class with the intact model, embed them with a pretrained image encoder, average per class, and take the top-K classes by cosine similarity to the target's mean embedding (K = 5 in the fine-grained runs; a k-NN-to-Naive-Bayes limit theorem is offered as justification for treating embedding proximity as semantic proximity). Images of a target, of its adjacency set and of unrelated classes are then split into three disjoint sets, and training optimises three losses at once. The erasing loss is a margin term: it pushes the target's predicted noise away from that of the adjacent classes harder than from the unlearning set's own images, by at least a margin δ. The adjacency loss is a plain L2 anchoring term holding the updated model's noise predictions for adjacent classes at the original model's values. The guidance loss drags the target's prediction toward a neutral "null" concept so no hand-picked surrogate class is needed. Baselines are five published erasers (ESD, FMN, CA, UCE, SPM, Receler) run from their official repositories with their standard settings. Success is scored by fine-tuning a ResNet-50 classifier on model-generated images and measuring accuracy on the erased class (erasing accuracy) and the mean accuracy across the adjacency set (adjacency accuracy), combined in a harmonic mean they call the Erasing-Retention Balance score; rectified for the coarse-grained setting with a target-class accuracy, average other-class accuracy, NudeNet counts on I2P and FID on COCO30K.

## What counts as an explanation here
None. This is a training-time intervention, not an explanation: the object produced is a set of LoRA weights plus a procedure for deriving the adjacency set that those weights were fitted to protect. The nearest thing to a claim about mechanism is the negative one stated in the conclusion — existing erasers work by displacing a concept's representation, which is effective at removal and damages the surrounding manifold — and the paper's remedy is to regularise that displacement rather than to characterise it. It does not predict what else an edit will change; it constrains the edit so that fewer things change.

## How they judged success
Erasing accuracy (higher is better), adjacency accuracy (mean over the K neighbours), and their harmonic mean ERB (higher is better, with a small constant to avoid division by zero), on three target classes per fine-grained dataset and four ImageNet-1k targets; target-class accuracy against average other-class accuracy on Imagenette; NudeNet exposure-category counts and FID on I2P/COCO30K. Controls: an ablation of each of the three losses on one target class; an alternative anchor class for the Concept Ablation baseline; a radar presentation where methods are only compared if their erasing accuracy is at or below 20%, so retention is never credited to methods that failed to erase; adversarial prompts from the Ring-a-Bell procedure; a 40-participant human study scoring erasure and retention on generated and adjacent images. All comparisons are against the same five baselines re-run in their own official code, not against re-implementations.

## What they found
The baselines split into two failure modes and the split is the paper's most useful result. Every method that exports well scores near-zero on neighbours: Receler reaches ~100 on erasing accuracy in all nine fine-grained cells while adjacency accuracy sits at 2.40, 2.80, 1.16, 6.32, 0.52, 0.88, 0.68, 0.12 and 1.28, and FMN is nearly as bad (adjacency 0.20 to 7.44). Concept Ablation is the mirror image — adjacency 55 to 84 with erasing accuracy of 27 to 79 — and SPM sits between them with the highest baseline ERB, up to 82.73. FADE's cells run adjacency accuracy 89.76 to 97.28 at erasing accuracy 99.60 to 100.00, giving ERB 94.60 to 98.62 against a best-baseline ERB of 82.73 — the abstract's "at least 12% improvement in retention" and the results section's "at least 15%" refer to this gap. On ImageNet-1k ERB for the four targets FADE scores 87.08 to 97.93 against SPM's 72.30 to 86.02. On I2P the exposure-count reduction is 87.88% (their highest) at FID 13.86, marginally behind FMN's 13.52 with FMN reducing 44.2%. Ablating the adjacency loss collapses ERB from 95.97 to 38.58 and erasing accuracy from 99.60 to 25.40; removing the erasing loss gives 43.26; removing guidance leaves erasure perfect at 100.0 but drops adjacency accuracy to 76.12 and ERB to 86.44. The human study ranks the methods the same way but at a much lower level — FADE 59.49, CA 49.38, SPM 49.13, FMN 43.07, ESD 38.43, Receler 0.06 — with FADE's judged erasing accuracy only 51.94% against its classifier score of 99.60. Their robustness sweep finds baselines degrading from roughly 70–80% embedding similarity upward while FADE stays flat; in the appendix, retention improves monotonically as the neighbour moves away from the target, which is the curve one would expect and is reported only in that direction.

## Limits and open problems they name
They concede that creating a true "hole in the manifold" is still open — FADE trades a displaced representation for a hole surrounded by a regularised collar, not for a clean excision. The adjacency set is not ground truth: it is whatever the chosen image encoder says is close, certified only by agreeing with one hand-curated taxonomy on four ImageNet targets, and the same encoder defines the thing being protected. There is no evaluation of whether the protected neighbours are still *composed* with other concepts after editing, only whether they are still classified. Additional things the paper does not supply: no random-adjacency-set control (protecting a set of deliberately irrelevant classes at the same weight) to show the collateral damage is specific to semantic proximity rather than general drift; no repeat runs or seed variance anywhere, so a single ablation table on a single target class carries the mechanism claim; the human study's judged erasure rate being half the classifier's is left unexamined; and the "at least 12%" and "at least 15%" claims in abstract and results are inconsistent with each other on the same table.

## What we take from it
A **warning and a template**, both about how to state the blast radius of an edit. The warning: erasure scores and retention scores are anti-correlated across methods, so any single "success" column in this literature is a statement about which side of that trade the method bought — the same lesson this folder's LLM-side entries draw from probes that correct some behaviours and break others. The template: they named the neighbourhood *first*, derived it from the model's own embeddings rather than from labels, and then made it a term in the objective, which is the cleanest available instance of "the set you promise not to damage is a declared part of the method, not a post-hoc caveat". For our batteries the transferable move is to declare the equivalence class around each edit up front and score inside it and outside it separately, with a deliberately irrelevant set as the control — and to distrust a claimed improvement whose headline rests on an evaluation instrument the authors also designed, since the human study here falls to roughly half the automated number on the same objects.
