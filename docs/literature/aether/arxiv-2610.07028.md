# Identifiable World Models from Pretrained Diffusion Representations

- **Key:** arxiv-2610.07028
- **Authors / organisation:** Ruchi Sandilya, Conor Liston, Logan Grosenick; Department of Psychiatry and Brain & Mind Research Institute, Weill Cornell Medicine, New York
- **Date:** 2026-10-04
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.07028
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (arXiv HTML v1, body plus Appendices A.1-A.12)
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
The paper starts from a distinction the folder keeps running into: a generative model can reproduce or forecast a trajectory without its internal coordinates meaning anything in particular. Diffusion world models of videos are good at the first and say nothing about the second. The authors ask whether a frozen pretrained diffusion backbone can be given a coordinate system that is identifiable up to a named equivalence class, that preserves the lagged causal model over the underlying state, and that is therefore usable for analysing responses to control inputs - all without retraining the generative model. The larger goal is a world model whose latent space can be read as state variables and whose structure can be interrogated by intervention, rather than one judged only by reconstruction and rollout error.

## What they studied
A pretrained conditional latent diffusion model (cLDM) trained on three video datasets: CausalVerse Physical Fall (falling bodies, six-dimensional true state of three positions and three velocities), CausalVerse Robotics Study (a 25-dimensional robot state: joint positions and velocities, end-effector position and orientation, gripper position and velocity) and Microduck, a MuJoCo bipedal-locomotion environment built on the Pollen Robotics RL framework with a six-dimensional state (forward and lateral body velocity, yaw rate, trunk height, roll, pitch) and a three-dimensional command input (forward velocity, lateral velocity, yaw rate). The unit of analysis is a low-dimensional embedding learned on top of frozen diffusion latents, compared to the true state, its blocks, and its lag-1 dependency graph. Data volume is modest and stated: five thousand videos for Physical Fall and Microduck with one thousand held out, twenty-seven hundred for the Robotics Study with five hundred forty held out.

## How they did it
Three levels are kept separate: the true dynamical state, the high-dimensional diffusion latent, and the observed video frame. The generative chain runs state to latent to frame; estimation runs the other way, frame to latent to an embedding. A frame is encoded with the Stable Diffusion VAE and then inverted into the frozen diffusion model with eight steps of a reversible Runge-Kutta solver, giving the diffusion latent, which is where everything downstream operates. A small map is then trained on those latents using one of three contrastive objectives (time-contrastive, generalized contrastive, or CEBRA), with positive and negative pairs defined by temporal or auxiliary context such as gravity and time for the falling bodies, object position and velocity for the robotics data, and commands for the robot. The population optimum of that objective is read as a density ratio, which is what lets known auxiliary-variable nonlinear-ICA identifiability results be transferred to this pipeline. Graphs are then estimated from the aligned embeddings two ways: residual Lasso with trajectory-level bootstrap stability for the CausalVerse sets, and for Microduck an average absolute transition Jacobian of a controlled transition model that takes state and command to next state, binarised using the reference parent count per child so that graph density is fixed across methods. Intervention testing uses one hundred matched trajectories reset to the same state at a fixed time, compares positive and negative command perturbations against the zero-command baseline, and rolls out for twenty-five steps at twenty-five hertz.

## What counts as an explanation here
Three complementary objects: an identifiability guarantee (the embedding equals the true state up to a permutation and per-coordinate invertible scalars, and the dynamics expressed in the embedding keep the same parent sets and the same conditional factorisation); a graph (a lag-1 adjacency matrix over the embedding or over an aligned readout, claimed to be recoverable from the sparsity pattern of the transition-noise Jacobian, which for additive noise equals the sparsity of the forward Jacobian); and a predicted intervention response curve, the dynamic intervention effect as a function of rollout horizon. The paper is explicit that this is partial: the guarantee is population-level and conditional on assumptions, it does not cover the CEBRA objective, and the authors state that when assumptions only approximately hold the embedding may recover mixtures or blocks rather than coordinates. So the explanation predicts - a graph, a response sign, a trajectory of effect sizes - but it does not claim to be complete.

## How they judged success
Coordinate-wise identifiability by MCC, the mean absolute Pearson correlation between true and learned coordinates under an optimal injective matching; block-wise identifiability by R-squared of a regression from the embedding onto a true state block, so that information content and coordinate alignment are scored separately. Graph recovery by F1, accuracy, normalised structural Hamming distance, area under the precision-recall curve on the continuous edge scores, and normalised structural intervention distance. Intervention prediction by RMSE and relative error of the predicted dynamic intervention effect against matched simulator rollouts, at horizons of one, two, five, ten and twenty-five steps, with the true-state dynamics model used as the reference that separates representation error from rollout error. All methods run over five random seeds with mean and standard deviation; baselines are IDOL, CaRiNG, TDRL, a temporal SuaVE variant, and iVAE.

## What they found
On the falling-body system the time-contrastive and generalized-contrastive variants reach essentially perfect block recovery and exact graph recovery (F1, accuracy and AUPRC at one; both distance metrics at zero), while the strongest baseline scores 0.714 MCC on coordinates and a normalised structural Hamming distance of 0.044. On the 25-dimensional robotics data the same two variants reach state reconstruction of 1.000 on blocks with MCC near 0.68, and the baseline iVAE is slightly better on graph metrics. Microduck is much harder: the best MCC among the reported methods is 0.629, and the strongest graph recovery, from the time-contrastive variant, gives F1 of 0.769, accuracy 0.888, normalised Hamming distance 0.111 and normalised intervention distance 0.076. Across all three sets block recovery substantially exceeds coordinate recovery - the paper reads this as the embedding retaining the information while individual coordinates mix state variables - and it argues that near-perfect block recovery can be enough for the graph. In the intervention test the representation preserves the sign and temporal shape of the response in all four conditions but attenuates magnitude: at the one-second horizon the forward-velocity effect is 0.0281 predicted against 0.0461 in the simulator (relative error 0.571), and the lateral effect is 0.0107 against 0.0084. The true-state baseline is consistently better on forward velocity and for the negative lateral perturbation at long horizons the representation model is slightly better in RMSE, which the authors attribute to the small effect size against rollout variability.

## Limits and open problems they name
The guarantees are population-level and rest on informative auxiliary variables, separability, regularity, and - for the graph claim - structural faithfulness and additive noise. Empirical graph recovery aligns embeddings to known state coordinates, so "identifiability" is measured against labels that a real analysis would not have. Interventions are open-loop, in simulation only, and drawn from within the training command distribution. The authors leave partially observed, higher-order, nonstationary and real-world systems to future work.

## What we take from it
Three things. (1) A metric split we should copy: scoring information-in-the-representation (block R-squared) separately from coordinate alignment (MCC) is what let these authors report honestly that their near-perfect numbers are block-level, and it is the cheapest way for us to avoid claiming a one-to-one state readout when we have only proven recoverability. (2) A graph criterion that is checkable: lagged edges as sparsity of the transition-noise Jacobian, with the additive-noise shortcut that the forward Jacobian has the same pattern - a concrete, falsifiable structural claim for a white-box model of a dynamical system rather than a description. (3) A warning about the credential: exact graph recovery was obtained on the simplest system in the set (analytically known linear time-lagged physics) and the same machinery fell to F1 0.769 on the only system with non-trivial coupled dynamics, so we should not read "exact recovery, distance zero" as a property of the method; it is a property of the benchmark, and the paper is careful enough to say so in the results paragraph rather than only in the limitations.
