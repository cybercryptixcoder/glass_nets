## What they wanted, and why

Compositional reasoning, as the paper defines it, means deriving meaning by combining the parts of a sentence while staying indifferent to how that sentence is arranged. The authors take one concrete test of that indifference: take a maths word problem, keep its meaning, and change its word order. Hindi and Malayalam tolerate considerable constituent movement without changing what is said, so they are natural places to try this. The larger goal is to decide whether multilingual reasoning skill is semantic or statistical — whether the model has learned to relate entities to quantities, or has learned the written shapes those relations usually arrive in. The paper's own framing is that if meaning is preserved and behaviour changes, the behaviour was not being driven by meaning.

## What they studied

Six instruction-tuned models spanning families and scales — Gemma-2-9B-it, Gemma-2-27B-it, GPT-OSS-20B, Llama-3.1-8B-it, Param-2-17B-A2.4B (non-thinking) and Qwen3-30B-A3B — plus Gemma-4-12B-it on a second benchmark. Data: the Hindi and Malayalam GSM8K test sets (1,319 items), and a Hindi ARC-Challenge set (1,150 items). Two perturbations are constructed: a constrained constituent reordering, and an active↔passive voice conversion, together released as IndicReStruct. The interpretability arm narrows to one model, Gemma-2-9B-it, chosen because it is dense while most of the suite is mixture-of-experts and therefore harder to read internals through. The unit of analysis there is the residual stream, sliced two ways: by layer, and by a nine-way token category (subject, object, verb, connectors, operation words, number, unit, entity–quantity combinations, punctuation).

## How they did it

Each question is first segmented into semantically cohesive word groups by a prompted strong multilingual LLM (Gemini-2.5-Pro), following an interlingua grouping convention with practical adjustments so that, for instance, an adjective travels with the noun it modifies; the grouping is then what moves, so indivisible units are never split. Each sentence has its groups permuted by a pseudo-random order subject to hard constraints: punctuation and parentheses act as walls, and a printed list of conjunctions and discourse markers may not be crossed because they govern scope and attachment. Five seeds (42, 50, 123, 321, 999) give five reordered banks per language. The voice arm prompts the same model to flip every sentence's voice while holding tense, number and named entities fixed. Data quality is checked three ways: cosine similarity to the original under four multilingual encoders, compared against a fully random reordering of the same groups; an LLM judge rating each item excellent, good or reject on meaning preservation and solvability; and a human check on a 60-item sample drawn across the three rating bands. Every model then answers original, reordered and voice-changed questions under four prompting regimes (zero-shot chain of thought, one-shot, three-shot, plan-and-solve), greedy decoding by default, scored by exact match of the final number. One model (Gemma-2-9B-it) is additionally adapted with LoRA and DoRA on Hindi reordered and voice-changed training splits, and finally run through denoising activation patching: examples are selected where the model is right on the original and wrong on the reordered variant, then residual-stream activations from the clean run are injected into the perturbed run at positions belonging to one token category and one layer, and the authors record whether the correct answer returns.

## What counts as an explanation here

Two things, at different strengths. The first is a benchmark and a taxonomy: nine labelled error categories (overthinking, wrong operation, skipped step, chain-of-thought language mismatch, question-reordering semantic drift, entity/quantity misalignment, answer extraction error, irrelevant reasoning, other), assigned by an LLM judge to each failure. The second is a causal-relevance map over layers and token categories, in the sense of causal tracing: a category's importance is how often injecting it from the clean run repairs the perturbed one. Neither is a description of a mechanism or a predictive model of the computation; the explanation is a locus plus a failure label, and the paper is explicit that the layers it finds are where recovery happens, not what the recovery computes.

## How they judged success

Accuracy under exact final-answer match, with mean and standard deviation across five generations for the perturbed condition; absolute drop versus the original as the headline number. Two interpretability measures: restoration efficiency, the count of repaired examples divided by the number of patched tokens in that category (a normalisation the paper states is there to correct category size imbalance), and restoration rate, the share of examples repaired. Analysis is on 65 Hindi and 67 Malayalam examples that the judge had rated excellent and where the model had flipped from right to wrong. Data validity rests on encoder cosine similarity against a random-reordering control, the judge's three-way rating (642 good, 553 excellent, 124 reject of 1,319), and the human sample (25 / 26 / 9 across the bands, with disagreements running toward the higher rating, i.e. the judge is conservative).

## What they found

Reordering degrades every model under every prompting regime in both languages. Across the 48 Hindi and Malayalam cells I totalled from the table, the reordered drop averages 11.9 points from a mean original accuracy of 66.4, is at least 2 points in 47 of 48, and peaks at 33.1 (Param on Hindi plan-and-solve, 66.5 to 33.4) and 31.7 (the same model on Malayalam plan-and-solve). Llama-3.1-8B-it is the extreme case, dropping 31.2 on Hindi zero-shot (56.6 to 25.4) and collapsing from 0.53 to 0.13 on Malayalam plan-and-solve — a near-floor baseline where the paper says little can be concluded. Robustness ordering is not the parameter ordering: GPT-OSS-20B is both strongest and least damaged in most cells. Voice change hurts too, but unevenly: Gemma-2-9B-it loses 1.8 in Hindi against 8.5 in Malayalam, Gemma-2-27B-it 6.3 against 23.3, while Qwen3 loses under half a point in either language. On ARC-Challenge the same reordering costs Gemma-2-27B-it and Gemma-4-12B-it only about 2 to 4 points (87.1 to 83.7, 91.6 to 88.0), so the size of the effect is benchmark-dependent. Fine-tuning does not fix it: DoRA beats LoRA (41.6 to 46.1 on reordered, 40.9 to 51.3 on voice-changed) yet both sit below the 61.8 zero-shot CoT baseline on reordered Hindi. In the patching arm, intermediate layers carry the restoration (the paper points at roughly layers 5 to 25), and by token category entity–quantity combinations restore most in both languages, followed by numbers, verbs and punctuation — which matches the error taxonomy, where entity/quantity misalignment and skipped steps are prominent. The authors' reading is that the failures are not arithmetic failures but relational ones: the model loses track of which quantity belongs to which entity.

## Limits and open problems they name

Two Indian languages and two problem types only, with an explicit warning against transferring to typologically different or less free-word-order languages, and against transferring to commonsense, symbolic or long-context reasoning. The perturbation pipeline itself is LLM-generated (grouping, voice conversion, judging) and the authors lean on an encoder-similarity control and a human sample rather than a full human annotation. The interpretability arm is one dense model on a few dozen selected examples, so no claim about other architectures is made, and the mixture-of-experts models are explicitly set aside as too opaque for this analysis. The paper does not name a mechanism by which reordering breaks entity–quantity binding; it localises where repair succeeds and says the cause is relational. The fine-tuning experiment is small and domain-bound (about 7.5k examples after augmentation), which the authors offer as their own explanation for why it overfits perturbation-specific surfaces.

## What we take from it

- **Method (reuse).** A semantics-preserving input change is the cheapest invariance test available to us: our account of a computation should predict which changes leave the output alone, and reordering-type changes are exactly the class where a genuine mechanism claim can be falsified without touching weights. Their construction discipline — move only cohesive units, forbid crossing scoped markers, fix seeds, and report a variance across the perturbation draws rather than one number — is the operational form of that test.
- **Measure (borrow, with their weakness attached).** Restoration count divided by patched token count is the wrong denominator for us. A category can win because it is common, or because it is patched at many co-located positions; the number says where repair is easy, not which unit carries the quantity–entity relation. Our version of this measure should divide by *opportunities* — examples in which the category is present and the model failed — and print the raw repair count beside it.
- **Framing (adopt).** The paper's own evidence is consistent with ours: its strongest single finding, entity–quantity combinations restoring most, is never checked against a category of matched frequency and never checked against a no-op patch. Without that control, "these tokens matter" and "these tokens are many and co-located with the answer" are the same table. This is the same failure run 566 recorded on the input side: an uncalibrated change set is a graph painting, not a localisation.
- **Warning (adopt).** The aggregate-drop table hides a fixed-component story that is worth carrying. Prompting strategy moves accuracy by more than the perturbation does in several cells — Qwen3 on Hindi scores 80.1 one-shot and 44.9 plan-and-solve on the same original questions — while the reordered drop sits in a similar band regardless. So a robustness comparison across models is partly a comparison of prompt sensitivity, and the honest unit is the change in the original-condition baseline as well as the change in the difference.
