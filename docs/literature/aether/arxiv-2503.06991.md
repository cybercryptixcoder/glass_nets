# Are We Truly Forgetting? A Critical Re-examination of Machine Unlearning Evaluation Protocols

- **Key:** arxiv-2503.06991
- **Authors / organisation:** Yongwoo Kim, Sungmin Cha (equal contribution), Donghyun Kim (corresponding); Korea University and New York University
- **Date:** 2025-03-10 (v1); v3 2026-01-29; accepted at *Engineering Applications of Artificial Intelligence*
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.06991
- **Kind:** paper (benchmark / evaluation protocol)
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Unlearning is certified by output-level numbers — forget accuracy, retain accuracy, membership-inference success — computed on small benchmarks such as CIFAR-10. The authors argue that this grading is structurally too easy: a model can satisfy it by bending its classifier while its encoder keeps representing the forgotten class, so the field's reported successes may be a false sense of security for the privacy claims they are used to support. Their question is whether the internal representation actually moves, and their proposal is a protocol that reads the representation rather than the logits, at a scale (a hundred to three hundred classes out of a thousand ImageNet classes) where the older benchmarks are simply too small to say anything. The larger goal is an evaluation standard for unlearning that a privacy auditor could defend, and a scenario design (their "Top" forgetting) in which the honest answer is genuinely hard rather than free.

## What they studied
Models: ResNet-50 trained from scratch on ImageNet-1K, extended to ConvNeXt-T and Swin-T (initialised from ImageNet-21K). Forget sets are 100, 200 or 300 of the 1000 classes, plus the whole training set as retain. Unit of analysis: **the encoder's pooled feature vector** (after average pooling), and through it the Gram matrix of features over a held-out sample. Ten unlearning arms spanning the usual families: fine-tuning on retain, gradient ascent, random labelling, pseudo-labelling (label each forget image with the original model's best guess among the retained classes), SalUn (salient-weight update), DUCK and SCAR (push forget features to the nearest wrong centroid), contrastive unlearning, and SCRUB (teacher-student distillation). Reference points: the original model θ_o and a model retrained from scratch on the retain set θ_r, treated as the gold standard.

## How they did it
1. For each arm, produce an unlearned model θ_u under the same forget set, and produce θ_r by full retraining. 2. Logit side: forget accuracy (on train and on test sets), retain accuracy (train and test), and an Average Gap of Logit-based metrics (AGL) formed as a product of one-minus-gaps against θ_r over the four accuracies — so a single arm that misses badly on one of the four collapses the product. 3. Representation side: extract encoder features on held-out data and compute **CKA** (similarity of normalised Gram matrices) between θ_u and θ_o, and between θ_u and θ_r; separately run a **5-NN** classifier on the features over three transfer datasets (Office-Home, CUB-200-2011, DomainNet-126) using 80/20 splits, and take the accuracy gap to θ_r's features. 4. Combine those into Average Gap of Representation-based metrics (AGR) = (1 − kNN gap) × CKA-to-retrained, and finally a harmonic mean of AGL and AGR called H-LR. 5. Second scenario: **Top-N forgetting**, where the N forget classes are chosen by cosine similarity of class-mean features to the downstream dataset's classes, so the forget set and the transfer task share semantic content and the retrained model's representation cannot quietly absorb the knowledge through a neighbour class. 6. Controls and ablations: three architectures, N from 100 to 300, kNN/CKA across three downstream sets, five runs per arm with a standard deviation table, a membership-inference probe, a differential-privacy noise sweep on one arm, Nesterov momentum on one arm, and a computational cost table.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not a mechanism and not a circuit — an **evaluation instrument and a two-variable diagnostic**. The explanatory objects are (a) the pair of similarity scores (to the original, to the retrained) per model, which places each arm in a plane where the honest answer is "near the retrained point", and (b) the H-LR scalar that refuses to reward a model which passes one side by failing the other. The paper's headline causal claim is of the form "these methods only touch the last layer", evidenced by comparing a fully unlearned model to a model where only the final layer was unlearned. It is deliberately partial: it says nothing about which internal units hold the knowledge, only about whether the feature geometry moved.

## How they judged success
Metrics named precisely: CKA between unlearned and retrained / original encoders; 5-NN accuracy on three downstream datasets and its gap to the retrained model; AGL from forget/retain accuracy on train and test; AGR as their product form; H-LR as the harmonic mean of AGL and AGR; MIA efficacy from an SVM trained on retain-versus-unseen confidence features; retraining time in minutes. Baselines are the original model, the retrained model, and each other arm; standard deviations are given for every entry in the main tables over five runs, stated as 95% intervals. The "gap" numbers printed in blue are all against θ_r, and θ_r is by construction 1.00 on both AGL and AGR — an advantage to note when reading the tables.

## What they found
- **No baseline beats a weighted copy of the original.** Every arm is closer to the original model than to the retrained one.** the unlearned-versus-retrained CKA never exceeds the unlearned-versus-original CKA for any baseline in the random-class setting, and Table 3's final column marks every arm but the retrained model with a cross on exactly that comparison. The clearest instance is the original model itself: on Office-Home its CKA to the retrained model is 89.8 while pseudo-labelling's is 91.6, so a near-weighted-copy of the original encoder looks *more* like the gold standard than the model it came from.
- **The last-layer shortcut is measured directly.** Unlearning only the final layer reproduces the fully unlearned model's features at high CKA while closing most of the logit gap, which the authors read as evidence that the objectives take the cheapest route to a good accuracy number.
- **The two rankings disagree, and by a lot.** Gradient ascent, random labelling and SalUn are indistinguishable from the retrained model on logits (AGL 0.12-0.15) and catastrophic in representation (AGR 0.04-0.06), with 5-NN accuracy on Office-Home falling to 29.7, 44.0 and 38.5 against the retrained model's 77.3. Pseudo-labelling, the least sophisticated of the retain-free arms, is competitive with the best retain-using arms: H-LR 0.89 against DUCK's 0.90 in the random setting, and 0.84 against 0.87 in Office-Home Top at N = 100. Retaining-set arms do not automatically win.
- **Top-N forgetting is the harder test and it reorders the arms.** With semantically matched forget classes, contrastive unlearning's AGR collapses to 0.09 in Office-Home Top and 0.05 in CUB Top while its AGL stays at 0.69 and 0.49 — a method that looked mid-table becomes near-bottom. SCRUB is the extreme: AGL 0.80 / AGR 0.60 at N = 100 random, then H-LR 0.03 / AGR 0.02 in Office-Home Top.
- **Scale hurts everything, and the fall is not uniform.** AGL drops from 100 to 300 forget classes for the bounded objectives much less than for the unbounded ones: pseudo-labelling's H-LR goes 0.89 → 0.86, while SCRUB's falls from 0.69 to 0.33. Under CUB Top at N = 300, fine-tuning's test forget accuracy reaches 46.7, against 28.4 at N = 100 — so the fixed-budget arm gets *worse at forgetting* as the forget set grows and its representation-side AGR falls to 0.60.
- **MIA and the representation metric are not proxies.** MIA efficacy is 98-99% for most methods (i.e. the attack *fails* to identify forgotten samples as members, so it reports successful unlearning) while the same models retain most of the original representation; random labelling is the exception at 8.0. The authors argue a confidence-based per-sample attack is the wrong instrument for class-wise removal.
- **Cost is not the barrier.** Under CUB Top-100, the representation side costs 74.41 TFLOPs and 37.89 seconds against 5477.46 TFLOPs and 1002.18 seconds for the logit side. Retraining as gold standard costs 4666.7 minutes; the retain-free arms run in 24-35 minutes against 330-913 for the retain-using ones.

## Limits and open problems they name
Classification only, with an accessible encoder — nothing is said about generative or black-box models, and the authors doubt CKA and kNN capture all semantic shifts. **Class-wise only**: single-sample unlearning is explicitly out of reach because removing one point cannot move a distribution-level similarity measure, and they note the logit metrics are equally ill-suited there. The protocol depends on a retrained oracle, and they concede retraining is expensive while offering CKA(θ_u, θ_o) only as an untested proxy; they call an oracle-free protocol an open problem. They also warn that a benchmark invites its own overfitting and recommend pairing it with formal privacy guarantees and adversarial checks — a self-aware caveat that the H-LR scalar could become a new target to game. The differential-privacy sweep shows that Gaussian noise strong enough to buy a real guarantee (σ = 1e-1) collapses kNN accuracy and both CKA values at once, so the privacy-utility trade-off is not a dial they can turn.

## What we take from it
**A method, and the cheapest instrument in the folder so far:** similarity to a reference model in feature space, with *two* references — the original and the "corrected" one — so the score is a position in a plane rather than a scalar that can rise for the wrong reason. AGR's (1 − gap) × similarity form is a generalisable construction for us: whenever our white-box model is scored against a target that is itself hard to compute, pair the "did we move toward it" term with a "did we keep the ability" term and take a product, because a product cannot be satisfied by improving one and neglecting the other. **A measure to steal outright:** the nearest-neighbour retrieval check on the corrected model's features — the paper's Figure-9 argument is that an unlearned model still returns the *original* model's neighbours for a query. That is a behavioural readout of an internal state which needs no labels, no training and no oracle, and it is exactly the kind of check we can run on any surrogate to test whether it reproduces the original's geometry or only its outputs. **A dataset-design rule, and the sharpest transferable idea:** make the held-out evaluation task share semantic content with the thing you removed, otherwise the model's remaining knowledge *substitutes* for what was deleted and the measurement reads as success. Read against the two runs that precede this one (a clamp that leaves behaviour recoverable, a restoration that reopens a class at a fixed coefficient), the recurrence is that **the folder keeps finding that output-side removal and representation-side removal are different operations — this paper is the systematic version, and it adds that the gap is largest exactly where the objective is cheapest to satisfy, at the classifier.** Mechanical rule for us: **every claim our model makes about a unit's role is reported with both an output-side check and a geometry-side check, and the geometry-side reference is a model that should have the unit absent, not the model itself before the edit.** **A warning:** the retrained oracle is load-bearing and, as the authors say, expensive; our version of the concern is that the "reference model" in any comparison we build must be obtained by retraining rather than by editing, or the reference inherits the thing we are testing for.
