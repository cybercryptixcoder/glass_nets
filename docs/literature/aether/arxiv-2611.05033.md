# Whose Effect Is It? How Objectives Inflate Learned Subspace Interventions

## What they wanted, and why

The localisation literature ranks methods by how far an intervention moves the model's output, and learned methods routinely beat the training-free baseline of copying the *whole* state. On CausalGym the learned method's score is a median two to three times vanilla, and one published one-dimensional sentiment direction is reported at 109.8% on a metric where the complete copy is 100%. Since the whole contains the part, that ordering needs an explanation. Their question is whose effect the excess is — something the network computes, or something the training objective selects — and their answer is a decomposition with a training-free reference so that "bigger effect" can never be read as "better evidence". The larger goal is a reporting rule: every learned effect gets compared to what the network itself produces at the same site.

## What they studied

Ninety tasks from six sources (the 29 CausalGym tasks, re-paired number-only and lemma-only variants, seven filler–gap and eight negative-polarity constructions, sentiment templates, SST pairs, RAVEL, and IOI in two sizes plus a custom frame), fourteen base checkpoints from 124M to 32B over six families, with Pythia, Qwen2.5 and Llama at three or more sizes each. The unit is a **subspace of the residual stream at a (layer, region) cell**, rank one inside the network and ranks 1–64 (plus the label rank) at the readout, chosen either by a trained method (distributed alignment search under four objectives) or by a training-free rule (difference of means, probe, PCA, k-means, LDA, the readout gradient). The measured quantity is a log-odds difference between source and base answer tokens, expressed as a multiple of the total effect T that replacing the whole input produces.

## How they did it

- **One exact result first.** At a *complete cut* — every position of one layer, or the readout state — the output is a function of the cut's states plus shared side inputs, so copying the whole source state there reproduces the source run exactly: whole-state patching at a complete cut equals T, pair by pair. At a *partial* cut (one region of a layer) it need not, and they show a case where it lands above T (Pythia-410m adjective/verb positions at layers 14–15 restore 120.3% and 121.7%, interval lower bounds ≥104%).
- **First-order theory.** Writing the observable as a function of the state at a site, expanding about the midpoint between base and source states, a rank-one interchange along unit `d` has mean effect `d^T M d` with `M` the symmetric part of `E[w Δ^T]`; whole-state patching has first-order effect `tr M`. By the Rayleigh quotient a part can beat the whole exactly when the remaining parts sum negative, i.e. when they pull the output back — no cleaner isolation of the variable is needed. Rank k gives the Ky Fan bound. Every first-order statement is machine-checked in Lean 4 with Mathlib; the measurements are not.
- **Objective taxonomy.** Effects are grouped by what the objective demands of them: *effect-seeking* (cross-entropy, logit difference, the mean effect itself), *target-aimed* (per-pair squared error to T, or to the cell's own copy effect), *representation-only* (difference of means, probe, PCA, k-means, the readout gradient — none of which runs the intervention).
- **Readout arm.** Rank-one DAS trained at the readout under four objectives from three shared initialisations, one pass and ten, exact effects on test pairs, plus a gradient-ascent search on the training pairs scored on test pairs (the out-of-sample maximum) and a predicted maximum λ₁ computed before training. 95% bootstrap intervals resampling test clusters (a pair with its mirror); a population rule keeps cases with ≥20 clusters and T's interval above zero.
- **Inside the network.** Maps over every region at every second layer in six small models; the reference is the cell's own copy effect, and a harder reference G* is the copy effect "in best context" — the cell alone or the cell plus one other region of its layer, whichever is larger on the training pairs. A cell where the learned effect is significant, G* is not, and the learned effect exceeds G* is called **off-route**. Freezing every downstream attention pattern separates effect that runs through attention weights from effect that runs through values.

## What counts as an explanation here

Not an explanation of a model but a **decomposition with a named reference**: a learned effect plus the training-free copy effect at the same site plus an excess, with a first-order theory that says where the excess can live before anything is trained (upper end λ₁ = largest eigenvalue of M; the copy sits at the trace; ratio of maximum to trace `½(1 + 1/cos θ)` under one shared readout direction, maximised at the bisector of the mean state change and the readout direction). It is partial by construction — first order, rank one inside the network, one extra region of context — and it is falsifiable in the strongest sense available here: the predicted maximum is computed before training and compared to an out-of-sample search.

## How they judged success

Exact forward-pass effects on held-out pairs, never a proxy, as ground truth throughout. The theory is scored by the trace's distance from T (median 0.05–3.8% error per model) and by a sharp signed prediction: on mirrored sets the midpoint-gradient trace error should be −½ of the base-point error (observed in 880 of 882 cases; least-squares slopes −0.49 to −0.60). The angle prediction is scored against the out-of-sample maximum (median 2.1–5.6% error; rank correlation 0.71–0.98 per model). Controls: same initialisations across objectives so only the loss changes; ten passes to exclude underfitting; an exact optimum of the cross-entropy objective started from the top eigenvector (cosine 0.986–0.998 with the trained direction, ≤24% more effect); number-only re-paired tasks to remove a lemma confound; held-out templates and out-of-distribution splits; false-discovery control over map cells; and Gemma-2's logit soft cap handled explicitly.

## What they found

**The excess is large and mostly the objective's.** At the readout, cross-entropy DAS lies above T in 1096 of the 1124 cases with a margin (1103 of 1168 overall), at a median 1.64–3.00 T per model and 0.76–0.97 of the maximum effect. From the same initialisations, a per-pair squared error aimed at T lands a median 3.0% from T, and the CDAS distribution-matching divergence at 0.91–1.06 T. Inside the network, cross-entropy DAS exceeds the cell's copy effect at 16,489 of 18,221 live cells (median excess 0.14 T) and the harder G* at 15,998; 0.51–0.64 of its summed effect lies beyond G*, and 4300 cells are off-route with mostly small effects (median 0.04 T).

**Attention is the route and freezing diagnoses it.** Freezing downstream attention patterns removes 0.58 of the summed excess over the plain copy but only 0.10 of it survives that freeze — and 0.43 of the excess runs through the readout position's own attention row. Whole-state patching, by contrast, keeps 0.90 of its effect when attention is frozen. So the part a learned direction adds is mostly routed through attention weights, at sites where the network's own transfer does not go that way.

**The objective aims wherever you point it.** The identical squared error, aimed at the cell's own copy effect instead of at T, lands on the copy (slope 0.91, intercept 0.00 T), while the same loss aimed at T sits a median 0.09 T above it. Untrained rules behave as the theory predicts: difference of means and the cell gradient give 0.88 and 0.93 times the copy effect, but their bisector already gives 2.11 times — so the excess depends on which direction is patched rather than on training as such.

**One angle predicts the ceiling before training.** On two-label tasks with uncapped logits the readout gradients share almost one direction, and the median angle between the readout direction and the mean state change is 79.7° (interquartile 73.1–83.2), which is why the ceiling is far above the copy. That angle predicts the maximum within a median 2.1–5.6% per model, and DAS ends up close to the predicted top eigenvector (median |cos| 0.89–0.98). Model size moves both: from smallest to largest size within a family the maximum grows a median 24–41% and cross-entropy DAS 16–29%.

**Published results, re-examined.** CausalGym's DAS-to-vanilla gap (median 2.1–3.1 by size) is 1.02–1.08 times DAS's excess over G* in the same regions — an excess, not a better region; they also report that the released CausalGym code passes base and source token positions to its interventions in swapped order, which changes what is read on 19 of 29 tasks, and say they have informed the authors. The published sentiment direction re-runs at 114.7% with a layer-accounting band of [85.0, 137.4] that contains 100%; five models' bands include 100%, and Pythia-410m's 198.8% includes 100% only with a small-sample factor the authors say they chose after seeing one initialisation. Accuracy-based benchmarks show the excess only while the copy effect still gets pairs wrong: where both are already at 1.0 (317 readout cases, 322 cells) accuracy cannot see the difference, and two directions with equal accuracy can differ in how much of their effect the network itself produces.

**Higher ranks and the failure cells.** In the thirteen uncapped models no trained run at ranks 1/2/4/16/64 or the complement rank lies significantly above the first-order rank-k maximum (none of 41,286 tests). Rank one can *understate* the excess: on IOI with twenty names the rank-one maximum lies below T in every model, while from rank 16 DAS exceeds T in every case. Where cross-entropy does not exceed T (27 of 28 such cases) the base label's probability is a few thousandths, so the loss's derivative on that label is nearly zero — cross-entropy misbehaves exactly where the token it must lower is already improbable.

## Limits and open problems they name

First order throughout; the residual deviations are explained but not modelled (Gemma-2's logit cap — in-sample maximum exceeds the predicted maximum in 47 of 71 tasks, none once the cap is removed — and inside the network, where the excess above the predicted maximum is mostly attention and freezing removes 0.85 of it). Base checkpoints only, no instruction-tuned models; inside the network rank one only, with the 7–9B models mapped on three syntactic tasks. The reference is bounded: G* patches the last token of one region plus at most one other region of that layer, so an effect the network produces through earlier tokens of a region, or through three or more regions jointly, is invisible to it. They state explicitly that the account concerns objectives rather than one method, that the decomposition covers orthogonal subspace patches (not sparse dictionaries or nonlinear featurizers, for which only the copy-at-the-site comparison is available), and that their numbers come from interchanges, not from steering, ablation or weight edits. Open: which property of a task decides where off-route effects arise; how the attention share scales with model size; whether *inputs* (not only edits) can open those routes; and whether a safety-motivated edit suppresses a behaviour through a route the network does not use.

## What we take from it

- **Adopt the copy-at-the-site reference as a reporting requirement, not a baseline row.** For every direction, probe or dictionary feature we credit with an effect, report the training-free copy effect at the same site, the ratio, and the share that disappears when attention is frozen. At a complete cut the reference has a known exact value, which makes this sharper than a control task (which adjusts for a whole task) or a random-network baseline (a whole network), and it is the only local control in this folder's inventory. Cost is a handful of forward passes per pair.
- **"Predicts" and "produces" are different quantities.** A first-order estimate of the copy effect (attribution patching built from gradients at the base point is, to first order, the trace of M) estimates the reference; a learned direction trained toward a bigger effect reports the ceiling λ₁. Literature comparisons routinely set one against the other. Any of our surrogate-vs-trained-method comparisons should say which of the two it is comparing.
- **A computable prior for interventions, in the shape our own predictor should imitate.** λ₁ comes from one gradient pass per pair before any training, and it lands within a few percent of the out-of-sample maximum. The evaluation pattern — search the unit sphere on training pairs, score the found direction on held-out pairs — is exactly the "predict then test out of sample" protocol a white-box model owes, at a cost we can afford.
- **The geometric warning to carry into every steering claim.** When the mean state change and the readout gradient are far from parallel (79.7° median here), a direction between them moves the output further than the full change does, and it is not a better handle on the variable: it moves the state along a coordinate the input never changes and the rest pulls back. So "our direction has a bigger effect than the full patch" is evidence about the objective that chose it, not about the network. Paired with the extreme case: when the two directions are near-orthogonal, the ceiling is large while the copy effect is ~0 — the direction is impressive precisely where the network does nothing.
- **Held-out evaluation does not fix this.** The paper's own decomposition of a held-out effect into a shared part and an entity-specific part is the argument to reuse: testing on fresh entities removes the per-entity residual but not any shift every entity shares, which is exactly what an objective-induced excess is. Held-out templates on IOI still exceed T in 13 of 13 cases.
- **Also a discipline for edits and ablations.** An edit trained to remove a behaviour may suppress it through a route the network does not use for it, leaving the network's own route intact — the same "pernicious divergence" signature the off-route cells have here.
