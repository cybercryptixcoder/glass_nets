# Translation Circuit Discovery and Targeted Optimization for Low-Resource Multilingual Translation

- **Key:** arxiv-2610.21456
- **Authors / organisation:** Atai Waris Khan, Abdullah Mazhar, Raghu Kumar, Md Shad Akhtar — Indraprastha Institute of Information Technology Delhi (IIIT-Delhi); four addresses printed on one affiliation line with no per-author mapping
- **Date:** 2026-10-15
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.21456
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Most published circuit work is English-centric and high-resource, and most low-resource adaptation work (LoRA, language-specific subspaces, pivot modelling) tunes parameters without asking *which* components are responsible for the translation behaviour it is trying to fix. This paper puts the two together for two low-resource Indic directions, Hindi→Urdu and Hindi→Sindhi, and asks a single compound question: can a sparse, causally identified set of transformer components be found, and can restricting training to exactly that set beat both full fine-tuning and parameter-matched adapters? The larger goal is twofold — a mechanistic account of how a multilingual model routes and transforms between closely related South Asian languages, and a practical demonstration that a discovered circuit is a *usable* object rather than a diagram. A secondary question, and the one this folder cares most about, is whether the routing is English-pivoted, which earlier multilingual interpretability work reported.

## What they studied
Unit of analysis: **attention heads and MLP blocks** (components), plus **SAE features** in the residual stream as a finer-grained second level. Model: **Gemma-2 9B**, used for every circuit-discovery and targeted-optimisation run; baselines are NLLB-200, IndicTrans2, LLaMA-3.1, Qwen-3.5, Mistral-3 and Gemma-2, each under zero-shot, LoRA SFT and LoRA SFT+DPO. Data: the Hindi–Urdu and Hindi–Sindhi (Devanagari) subsets of BPCC, 60K and 35K parallel pairs after preprocessing, split into SFT / DPO / contrastive partitions (Hi–Sd: 20K / 15K / 10K; Hi–Ur: 30K / 30K / 10K; about 20 whitespace tokens per sentence on average). Transfer tests use Hindi–English, English–French, Hindi–Tamil and English–Irish.

## How they did it
(1) **Contrastive prompts.** Each source sentence yields one valid prompt `[SRC]: text → [TGT]:` and four counterfactuals that change only the directive region, one of four perturbation types (target nullification, action distortion, semantic obfuscation, paradox insertion), each instantiated with three lexical variants and averaged. Counterfactuals must keep identical token layout and must score **zero translation accuracy** on a held-out set, with two annotators agreeing that the directive was removed; failed templates are discarded. The arrow glyph is used rather than a hyphen because it tokenises as a single piece, which keeps the query position stable. (2) **CIRCE: subspace-constrained patching.** For each component, activation differences between positive and counterfactual prompts are collected into a matrix and factorised into one translation-relevant direction plus a residual subspace constrained to be orthogonal to it; the intervention *replaces only the projection onto that direction* with the counterfactual's, leaving the rest of the positive activation intact. Component importance is read from the induced change in translation logits, and retention is thresholded not by a convention but by a **P99 noise-floor criterion over the empirical intervention distribution**, landing near 0.25%. (3) **SAEs** (latent dimension 14336) are trained only on residual activations at CIRCE-retained layers, and features are scored jointly by a *selectivity* term computed on semantically matched bilingual prompts and a *causal lift* term from intervening on the feature during decoding. (4) **Targeted optimisation.** SFT is applied to the retained components with everything else frozen, then DPO on the same components, with a KL term to the base model, curriculum-ordered word → phrase → sentence. (5) **Causal validation** is progressive knockout by mean-activation replacement of retained components against randomly chosen components, and — the important control — the same retained components are knocked out *inside a general LoRA SFT+DPO model that was never restricted to them*, to break the circularity of validating a circuit that training itself optimised.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **circuit plus a feature dictionary**: a sparse set of named attention heads and MLP blocks, categorised into source, indicator and positional heads by their dominant routing behaviour, plus ranked SAE features carrying a selectivity and a lift number. The circuit is explicitly partial — the paper says the analysis covers the identified translation circuitry, not the model's whole multilingual representation space. It *does* predict: the same components are used as an intervention target and the prediction is that training them alone should suffice, which the parameter-matched LoRA contrast tests. The interpretive layer on top is a narrative — label-anchored routing heads upstream, semantic transformation in MLPs downstream, a shared Hindustani subspace rather than an English pivot — supported by cosine-similarity projections of MLP states onto language-token embeddings and by the SAE layer profile.

## How they judged success
Two families, never joined. **Behavioural:** BLEU, ChrF2/chrF++, TER and COMET under a unified evaluation, plus relative-percent deltas over the strongest baseline, plus the parameter-controlled comparison that matters most — **LoRA SFT+DPO matched to TITAN's 3.14% trainable-parameter budget**, so that a component-selection gain can be told apart from a parameter-count gain. Plus human evaluation: native bilingual experts scoring adequacy and fluency 1–5 on randomly sampled outputs, reported as percent of ratings ≥ 4. **Mechanistic:** progressive knockout of retained versus randomly selected components, with the number of components removed and the resulting BLEU printed; and the anti-circularity check inside an unrestricted LoRA model. There is no ablation of the P99 threshold, no sweep of the sparsity level, no seed replication of the circuit set, and no set-overlap statistic between the two language pairs' circuits.

## What they found
Targeted optimisation of a sparse circuit outperforms everything in the table: Hindi→Urdu BLEU 31.82 against Gemma-2 DPO's 25.94, and Hindi→Sindhi 23.25 against 18.01. The paper's headline relative numbers are **+33.75% Hi→Ur and +45.86% Hi→Sd** over the strongest multilingual baselines, while training **3.14%** of parameters; at that same budget, LoRA SFT+DPO reaches 26.37 BLEU against TITAN's 31.82 (+5.45). CIRCE-only (targeted SFT, no DPO) already reaches 28.03 and 22.19, i.e. most of the gain is attributable to circuit restriction rather than to preference optimisation. Knockout: in the unrestricted LoRA model, ablating eight CIRCE-identified components drops BLEU 25.53 → 16.54 (35.2%) while eight random components drop it 25.53 → 25.41 (0.45%); on Hindi→Sindhi a single key MLP takes accuracy from 100% to 54.4%, and the same intervention on Hindi→Urdu gives about 100% → 87%. Head ablation degrades gradually, MLP ablation collapses sharply — routing distributed, transformation concentrated. Transfer is positive in all four extra directions (Hi→En 29.33 → 34.21, En→Hi 28.71 → 31.94, En→Fr 30.58 → 33.57, Fr→En 33.09 → 36.40, Hi→Ta 23.01 → 26.71, En→Ga 29.52 → 33.19 against DPO), each with its circuit re-identified on that pair. Mechanistically: heads attend to **language-indicator tokens rather than source content**, monotone with depth (separators and boundaries early, language labels mid, direction routing and target commitment late); MLP alignment to English is negative in nearly every layer of both directions, which the authors read as evidence against an English pivot; and highly selective SAE features are routinely low-lift, so the causal features are a sparse subset spread across both language populations.

## Limits and open problems they name
Circuit discovery is computationally expensive even though the resulting fine-tune is cheap; single base model and one architecture; validation across more languages, scripts and domains is called for; the analysis covers the circuit rather than the whole representation space, so the global-organisation claims are scoped to these models and pairs; residual errors are named specifically (untranslated Hindi loanwords in Sindhi output, Unicode inconsistencies around implosive characters, fluency loss on long multi-clause sentences) and production use is told to include human linguistic review.

## What we take from it
Three things. **A calibration rule worth copying:** the retention threshold is not a chosen number but a quantile of the *empirical null of the intervention distribution itself* (P99), which is the same move as this folder's calibrated-null discipline arriving at the component-selection step rather than at the comparison step — and the entry should be read knowing that the quantile is never swept. **The circularity control is the transferable instrument:** knock the nominated components out inside a model that was *not* trained on them. Without it, "these components matter" is confounded by the fact that these are the components you trained; with it, the claim survives a targeted-training intervention that could have moved responsibility elsewhere. **A warning the paper prints without resolving:** the retention score is *availability* (how much the translation logit moves when you swap this direction), and the paper separately shows that selectivity and lift dissociate — so the component set is a set of available components, not of necessary ones, and the knockout numbers are the only evidence of necessity, measured after optimisation. Read alongside `arxiv-2611.17879` (compound versus single-unit scope, and a local gain that does not reach the endpoint) and `arxiv-2609.03511` (a restoration score divided by patches applied rather than by opportunities): the missing measurement here is the same one both of those flag — a **matched-frequency, matched-layer random-component set of equal size**, which the knockout study approximates but never matches on layer or on intervention count.
