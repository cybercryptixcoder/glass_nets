# Revisiting Robustness for LLM Safety Alignment via Selective Geometry Control

- **Key:** arxiv-2602.07340
- **Authors / organisation:** Yonghui Yang, Wenjian Tao, Jilong Liu, Xingyu Zhu, Junfeng Fang, Weibiao Huang, Le Wu, Richang Hong, Tat-Seng Chua; National University of Singapore, Hefei University of Technology, University of Science and Technology of China, ST Engineering Ltd.
- **Date:** 2026-02-07
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.07340
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Safety alignment built from preference data degrades when the deployment distribution differs from the training prompts, and when some preference labels are wrong. The authors observe that most remedies attack the data side — pick cleaner pairs, reweight by margin, or optimise over a worst-case neighbourhood of the preference distribution — and treat the resulting fragility as an annotation problem. Their argument is that a second source is never touched: a sharp objective landscape makes the trained safety behaviour move a lot under a small change of weights, so noisy or shifted supervision gets amplified by the optimiser rather than by the data. The paper therefore asks where in the parameter space the alignment loss is sensitive, and proposes to flatten the loss only there. The larger goal is robustness that is orthogonal to data curation, so the two can be stacked.

## What they studied
Four open backbones spanning two families and two scales: Pythia-2.8B, LLaMA-3.2-3B, LLaMA-3-8B and Qwen2.5-7B. Training is PKU-SafeRLHF-30K; evaluation spans that set plus four out-of-distribution safety benchmarks (Do-Not-Answer, HH-RLHF-Safety, HarmBench, SaladBench). The unit of analysis is the **parameter coordinate**: specifically the individual dimensions of the value-projection matrices of every transformer block, ranked by their alignment with a probe direction. A separate probe (one per backbone, linear, on the final-layer residual stream, trained with the harmless label of PKU-30K, weights frozen afterwards) supplies the direction; its weight difference between the two classes is the direction vector.

## How they did it
First a motivating measurement: train the linear safety probe, score every value-vector dimension by the absolute inner product with the probe (cosine similarity per the appendix), and perturb the top-ranked coordinates versus a random set of the same size, recording how much of the total worst-case alignment-loss increase each set accounts for. Then the method: keep the same ranking rule, take the top 1% of dimensions as a subspace, and run sharpness-aware minimisation restricted to that subspace — compute the gradient only on those coordinates, normalise it and step a fixed radius along it to get a perturbed parameter point, then take the real update from the loss evaluated at the perturbed point. The inner perturbation step is applied every fifth training iteration to save cost; the outer updates are ordinary. Two instantiations differ only in the loss being flattened: a token-level one on the DPO-style log-likelihood surrogate, and a reward-level one that converts a pretrained external safety reward model's score gap into a soft preference target and flattens the cross-entropy against that target, which is meant to be more robust to corrupted labels because the target does not come from them. Controls: the same pipeline with no geometry control, with random coordinates, and with uniform (all-parameter) control under the DPO loss on LLaMA-3-8B. Noise robustness is tested by flipping training preference labels at controlled rates and evaluating on clean validation data. A second-order Taylor argument and a PAC-Bayes bound in the appendix connect shrinking the subspace's top curvature eigenvalue to a wider radius of parameter perturbation that leaves safety loss under threshold; the bound's constants are not calibrated against measured curvature anywhere in the paper.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **partial geometric explanation plus a locality claim about the network**. The object is a selected parameter subspace (a set of coordinates), and the explanation is the statement that the alignment loss's worst-case sensitivity is concentrated in that subspace, so flattening there buys robustness cheaply. It is not a circuit, not a feature description, and not a predictor of individual outputs. The probe is used purely as a *selector*, and the decoded probe tokens in the appendix read as harmful/abusive lexical fragments on two backbones and as unrelated or byte-garbled fragments on the other two — so the paper never claims the probe direction means anything in particular, only that it correlates with safety labels well enough to locate sensitive coordinates.

## How they judged success
Win rate against the chosen response, judged by GPT-4.1-mini; attack success rate under two independent safety judges (MD-Judge-v0.2-internlm2-7b and NVIDIA Llama-3.1-Nemotron-Safety-Guard-8B-v3); averaged ASR over four OOD benchmarks as the headline robustness number; win rate as a function of label-flip rate for noise robustness; wall-clock training time against the DPO family. Baselines are vanilla, SFT, DPO, IPO, cDPO, rDPO and Dr.DPO. The key controls are the three geometry regimes (none / random / uniform / selective) and the composability check that adds ShaPO on top of the data-robust baselines.

## What they found
The motivating concentration is strong: perturbing under 0.5% of probe-ranked dimensions accounts for more than 80% of the cumulative worst-case loss increase, versus substantially less for a random set of the same size. In-distribution, table 1 shows the method with the highest or near-highest win rate and the lowest ASR on all four backbones with no visible trade — on LLaMA-3-8B (largest win rate)/MD/NV goes 69.19 / 26.57 / 14.41 for DPO to 85.45 / 2.76 / 0.13 for the token-level variant and 87.45 / 1.13 / 0.13 for the reward-level one; Qwen2.5-7B goes 75.81 / 20.55 / 7.27 to 89.26 / 0.75 / 0.13 (token) and 89.23 / 1.13 / 0.00 (reward). Under domain shift (table 2), averaged ASR on LLaMA-3-8B falls from 22.85 for DPO and 2.09 for Dr.DPO to 1.37 (token) and 0.70 (reward); on Qwen2.5-7B from 14.39 and 1.05 to 0.42 and 0.55. The smaller backbones (appendix table 6) show the same ordering, most dramatically on Pythia-2.8B where Dr.DPO averages 23.90 and the method averages 7.71 / 6.91. Noise robustness is reported as curves only: win rate stays highest for both variants at every flip rate up to 40%, with the reward-level variant strongest — and the paper attributes that to the external reward model being independent of the flipped labels rather than to geometry. The most informative row is the geometry-regime ablation (table 3, LLaMA-3-8B): in-distribution all four regimes are comparable (MD 25.56–27.32, NV 13.03–14.41, with selective actually the highest MD), but out of distribution selective control has the lowest MD (26.31 vs 28.78 none, 28.56 random, 26.83 uniform) and the lowest NV (14.12 vs 16.91 / 16.87 / 15.12) — a 2.5-point MD gap over no control that is smaller than the 20-point gaps reported for the full method in table 2, because table 3 isolates the geometry step on the DPO loss alone. Runtime roughly doubles to triples (LLaMA-3-8B: 46 min for the DPO series, 89 for token-level, 99 for reward-level).

## Limits and open problems they name
Compute overhead from the extra sharpness step is called out as the main practical obstacle and left to future work. The subspace estimate is admittedly approximate, built from one probe on one layer, and the authors read their own result as evidence that even coarse geometric cues help rather than as a validated localisation. They never test the robustness of the selected coordinate set itself — a different probe, seed or layer would give a different 1% — and the appendix's decoded tokens show the probe on two backbones is not tracking anything the authors can name. The capability side is not reported at all: there is no MMLU, perplexity or utility benchmark anywhere in the paper, so "no trade-off" rests on win rate and ASR moving together. The theorem-level claims (PAC-Bayes bound, bypass-boundary expansion) are given under local convexity and a Laplace approximation with no measurement of the curvature eigenvalue they predict should drop.

## What we take from it
A **cheap, reusable way to rank coordinates, and a warning about what such a ranking is worth.** The method part we can steal directly: score every parameter coordinate by its alignment with a probe direction and take the top fraction; the paper's own motivating figure is the template for a first experiment on any model we study, because it is a one-pass measurement over frozen weights and it produces a curve (fraction perturbed versus share of worst-case loss) that either shows concentration or does not. Two framings are worth carrying: (a) the geometry control and the data robustness are presented as *orthogonal axes that compose*, and the composability check — adding the geometry step on top of methods that already handle noise and getting further gains — is the correct way to argue that a new mechanism is not a relabelling of an existing one; (b) the regime ablation is the honest version of an ablation, because it holds the loss and the training budget fixed and varies only *where* the flattening is applied, which is what separates "regularisation helps" from "regularisation in the right place helps". What we should not copy: the direction is selected by a probe whose decoded vocabulary is uninterpretable on half the models, and the paper never asks whether the selected 1% is stable across probe refits — for us that is the question, since a coordinate ranking we cannot reproduce is not a white-box claim. Also note the missing capability table: a safety method that reports no utility numbers cannot be compared to one that does, and our entries should always carry both axes.
