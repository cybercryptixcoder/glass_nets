# Right Labels, Wrong Reasons: Signed Feature Attribution for Multi-Label Explainability

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.22515
- **Authors / organisation:** Kenton White — AC Online, Algonquin College (Ottawa, Canada; present address Sunshine Coast, QLD)
- **Date:** 2026-11-17
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.22515

## What they wanted, and why
Multi-label classifiers are asked to attach several labels to one instance from one shared feature space, so as the label set grows each label's own positive examples become a thin slice of the data. Under that scarcity a classifier can lock onto a feature that is predictive but weak and brittle, and still get the label right. The explainability problem inherits the same thinness: an explainer fit per label draws on that label's few positives, so its reported importances wobble across models. The authors want an explanation that is not fit per label, so that labels can be compared inside one coordinate system, and a diagnostic the practitioner can read without opening the classifier to tell a genuine reason from a spurious one. The framing question is "right label, wrong reason" — accuracy is not evidence that the decision rested on the intended feature.

## What they studied
Six classical multi-label classifiers over five standard benchmark datasets from the Madjarov et al. comparison, spanning genomics, music, image and email. Label cardinality runs 1.07–4.24 labels per instance and label-space size 6–53. Classifiers: binary relevance with logistic regression, with a 200-tree random forest, and with XGBoost (200 rounds); an ensemble of ten random-forest classifier chains with random label orderings, averaged and thresholded at 0.5; ML-kNN with k = 10; and RAkELo, random overlapping labelsets of size 3 built from overlapping sets and classified with random forests. The unit of analysis is a **feature weight per label** — a signed coefficient, not a neuron, direction or circuit. No transformer, no neural network, and no residual stream appears anywhere.

## How they did it
One Fisher linear discriminant is fitted to the black box's **binary predictions**, not to ground-truth labels: the explainer receives the raw feature matrix and the prediction matrix and nothing else. For each label the positive-group and negative-group centroids are taken over predicted members, and their difference is the right-hand side. A **single** within-class covariance is pooled over every label's positive group (scaled by the total positive count), regularised with a ridge term set to the median diagonal of that pooled matrix floored at 1e-6, and one batched Cholesky solve produces the whole weight matrix in closed form. A feature enters a label's attribution only if a two-sample F-test between that label's predicted-positive and predicted-negative groups is significant at 0.05 after Bonferroni correction within the label's own feature family; the sign of the weight then splits it into a positive list (counts toward the label) and a negative list (counts against it). Because columns are solved independently, a feature can land on several labels' lists, which the authors read as genuine co-occurrence structure rather than a defect. A feature-budget variant keeps only the top-k weights per label by magnitude and rescores, which is what produces their complexity sweep. Experimentally: 50/50 splits with iterative stratification to keep rare labels distributed, ten seeds, and fidelity measured three ways against the classifier's own predictions — precision, recall and ROC-AUC.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **global linear surrogate with signed per-label attribution lists**, plus two scalar diagnostics derived from it. The explanation is the weight matrix: for each label, a short list of features with signs. It is explicitly partial and is a surrogate — it reproduces the black box's decision rather than describing the black box's internals, and the black box is never inspected. The predictive claim is narrow: given an instance, the surrogate's score is meant to rank against the classifier's own prediction, and the reported AUC is the check. The diagnostics are the interesting part: the **norm** of a label's weight vector is offered as a bound on that label's separability (Theorem 1 gives a two-sided bound by the whitened centroid gap scaled by the extreme eigenvalues of the regularised pooled covariance, hence a cross-label proxy good up to the square root of that matrix's condition number), and a **concentration** statistic — largest weight over mean weight — is offered as a black-box signal that the label rests on one dominant feature.

## How they judged success
Fidelity of the surrogate against the classifier's own predictions, pooled across labels: ROC-AUC, precision and recall, mean ± std over ten seeds. A controlled comparison of covariance scope (shared pooled vs per-label, identical in every other respect — same Fisher family, same ridge rule, same right-hand side, same attribution protocol) so that any difference is attributable to pooling alone. Condition numbers of the covariance under both scopes. A fidelity-versus-complexity sweep (features per label, log scale) against binary-relevance decision trees and binary-relevance Lasso, with the tree and Lasso curves stopping where their own depth or regularisation path ends. And the concentration diagnostic tested against a matching per-pair cross-classifier disagreement measure, with the measure chosen per dataset by feature type (top-1 match on the two binary-feature datasets, correlation-cluster-aware top-1 on Yeast and Scene, full-vector Spearman on Emotions), averaged over all fifteen classifier pairs and ten seeds.

## What they found
AUC of the surrogate reaches at least 0.90 in every dataset–classifier cell and exceeds 0.96 in 25 of 30, with the two low cells both on Yeast — ML-kNN 0.901 and XGBoost 0.911, the two most non-linear boundaries. Recall is never below 0.77 and is 1.00 on Genbase; precision is the variable column, from 0.876 (logistic regression on Yeast) down to 0.245–0.579 on Genbase despite perfect recall. **Covariance pooling** is the paper's cleanest result: shared beats per-label AUC in four of five datasets, and the margin tracks label cardinality — +0.180 AUC on Enron's 53 labels, where per-label matrices are on average about 184,000× worse conditioned (condition number 6.32e7 against 3.44e2), with each Enron label carrying on average only 64 positives in 1,001 dimensions. Genbase is the one reversal (per-label 0.999 against shared 0.980) and the authors explain it by its near-orthogonal binary Prosite features, where a label-specific covariance carries signal that pooling blurs. On the **budget sweep** the surrogate is monotone in features per label and reaches the best AUC of any method on four of five datasets at full budget (0.964 Yeast, 0.969 Emotions, 0.984 Scene, 0.995 Enron); decision trees peak shallow and then decay (Emotions 0.858 at about three features down to 0.764 at about twenty; Yeast 0.828 at six down to 0.741 at fifty); Lasso is erratic at small budgets. Genbase again is the exception, where a single feature is nearly sufficient and the tree already scores 0.993 or better at one feature. On the **concentration diagnostic**, all five datasets show the predicted direction and two are significant (Enron ρ = −0.568, p = 0.001; Genbase −0.545, p = 0.029), while the three continuous-feature datasets do not reach significance (−0.486, −0.193, −0.086). Mean pairwise discrepancy between classifier predictions is small (0.4% to 10.7%), which is the load-bearing point: the classifiers agree on *which* instances to label, so the instability the concentration statistic tracks lives in the explanations, not in the predictions.

## Limits and open problems they name
The explanation is one linear surrogate with one covariance estimate, and the authors name two ways that breaks: strongly non-linear decision surfaces (the two Yeast cells) and an ill-conditioned pooled covariance (Genbase, where fidelity drops even though the boundary is simple). They argue, without proof beyond the cases shown, that failure is at least visible — it arrives as low concentration and low precision rather than as a confident wrong explanation. Open problems the framing implies rather than states: the significance test of the concentration diagnostic rests on 6–32 labels per dataset, so three of five correlations are uninformative rather than negative; the surrogate is fit and scored on the same test predictions throughout, so nothing in the paper measures out-of-sample fidelity for the method itself; and the diagnostics are validated against classical classifiers' internal importances, never against a network whose internals could be read directly.

## What we take from it
**A framing and a warning, both cheap to apply.** The framing: a surrogate explanation is a model of a model, so its fidelity and its scope are two different claims, and this paper keeps them apart by *measuring the thing that sets the scope* — the condition number of the shared covariance — rather than assuming it. That is the pattern to copy for any instrument we calibrate against another model's outputs: name the quantity that decides whether the surrogate is well-posed, report it per dataset, and let it carry the exception (Genbase) instead of hiding it. The warning is sharper and applies directly to us: **the entire fidelity table is in-sample** — the surrogate is fitted on the same predictions it is scored against — which is stated in the caption and honestly flagged in the complexity figure, but which means the high AUCs bound nothing about generalisation. Any read-out we report as "the surrogate reproduces the model" must say whether the scoring items were in the fitting set, and if they were, must not be compared against an out-of-sample baseline as this paper's Pareto figure compares against the tree and Lasso curves. The transferable instrument is the **concentration statistic** as a self-audit: when a weight vector is diffuse, the explanation is not to be trusted and the honest report says so — and the test of that claim is cross-model disagreement. Under our setting that becomes: fit the same linear surrogate to two models' behaviour and ask whether their diffuse directions coincide; where they do not, the surrogate is describing noise that both models happen to share. Weight-analysis framing translates into a reusable measure — with the caveat that Theorem 1's bound is a statement about separability in the *feature* space the surrogate sees, not about any internal direction.
