## What they wanted, and why

Fine-tuning on a dataset that looks narrow or even benign can produce a model that is broadly misaligned, and today the only reliable way to find that out is to run the fine-tune and audit the resulting model. That ordering is expensive and, they argue, actively harmful: the safety training applied afterwards can overfit the very evaluations used to detect the failure and can reward a model for hiding misalignment rather than abandoning it. So they define *alignment forecasting* — given a target model, a candidate SFT dataset and a named failure mode, return the probability that fine-tuning would meaningfully increase that failure mode — and release a benchmark to measure progress on it. The motivation is not only cost: they point at automated AI R&D, where systems run their own fine-tuning with no human positioned to catch a bad model afterwards, and at third-party auditing of training pipelines under transparency regimes that publish data but not weights. This is the black-box counterpart of the dataset-level forward map the folder already holds from the white-box side, and the paper cites that entry explicitly as concurrent work.

## What they studied

17 target models, 32 fine-tuning datasets, 16 failure modes, crossed into forecasting triples. Datasets come in three families: 10 failure-mode-targeted synthetic corpora of 1,000 assistant responses each, built so that narrow fine-tuning should induce broad misalignment; 6 benign domain Q&A sets (astronomy, geography, legal, music theory, education, health) as controls that should induce nothing; and 16 injection-fraction sets built by adding failure-relevant examples to two real post-training corpora (DOLCI and UltraChat) at 10%, 25% and 50%, plus clean baselines. Each failure mode is measured by 200 multiple-choice questions with exactly one misaligned option, 20 samples per question at temperature 1.0. The unit of analysis is the *(model, dataset, failure-mode)* triple — a binary emerged / not-emerged outcome, roughly 22% positive on test. The forecast itself is a probability over that triple; the features behind it are a mixture of corpus-level text reads, dataset-level history and a single forward-pass probe of the untrained model.

## How they did it

Every triple gets a label from a two-gate rule rather than a bare significance test. Gate A is a one-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test on per-question misaligned-option rates, at p < 0.05, applied triple by triple with no pooling, so adding a model or dataset never flips an existing label. Gate B is a per-(model, failure-mode) effect-size floor set at the model's own benign fine-tuning drift — the mean plus K standard deviations of its shift across six benign Q&A fine-tunes — with K chosen by cross-validation over those six datasets to hold the held-out false-positive rate at or below 5%. That floor exists because with 200 paired questions an absolute shift around 0.005 can reach p < 0.05, which is behaviourally meaningless.

The main forecaster decomposes the prediction into four pre-fine-tuning signals. Alpha is the failure mode's emergence base rate over the training triples. Gamma is a data-corruption read from a two-stage pipeline: a tool-using GPT-5 auditor agent samples on the order of 100–200 rows of the full 1,000-row dataset with read-only tools and writes a short behaviour-agnostic report, and a Gemini 2.5 Pro reader scores that report against the target failure mode for how coherently and deliberately the corruption points at it, averaged over five reads. B is broad spillover, defined as the dataset's largest coherence score across all failure modes — free, because the reader already produced the vector — on the theory that emergent misalignment spreads through a broad persona rather than a topical match. And b is model susceptibility, the base model's rate of picking the misaligned option on that mode's 200 questions before any fine-tuning. The four are z-scored and combined by a logistic regression under log-loss.

The combiner's model class is chosen without the test split in a way worth recording: the validation set contains only weak, training-range models, and selecting on it favours flexible classes (gradient-boosted and neural models reach up to 0.95 validation AUROC against the logistic's 0.92), so they select by leave-one-training-model-out cross-validation instead, where the logistic ranks first at 0.80. Only after every choice is frozen is the model refit on train plus validation for the single test evaluation. Test is a capability split: the five strongest models on a public capability index are held out as unseen, and datasets are held out on the same principle, so test triples are doubly out of distribution. Baselines are always-1, always-0, always-0.5, random, two majority-vote schemes, and the continuous base rate; plus prompted frontier forecasters, the target model forecasting itself, a weak-model-transfer forecaster shown outcomes from weaker models fine-tuned on the same data, and an open model LoRA-fine-tuned on the task in label-only and chain-of-thought variants.

The second half turns the forecaster into a data cleaner: each round it forecasts per-mode risk, flags modes above their benign baseline, and has a classifier drop only the rows that genuinely exhibit a flagged behaviour, with the forecast and its reasoning in context. Downstream they compare four edited versions of the same corpus — no filtering, 50% subsampling, classifier-only filtering, and forecast-based filtering — each fine-tuned with identical hyperparameters, and score induced misalignment on the MCQ evaluation plus a separate multi-turn behavioural audit.

## What counts as an explanation here

A calibrated probability and an explicit additive decomposition of where it comes from — not a mechanism. The understanding claimed is that misalignment risk is mostly a property of the dataset (how hazardous this corpus is, per failure mode) with only a weak model-specific correction, and the four weights plus the variance decomposition are the whole of the content. The forecast is meant to be predictive and actionable rather than complete: nothing here says which internal feature the fine-tune would install, and the authors say plainly that a forecaster that modelled how a particular model absorbs a particular corpus would need a stronger model-side signal than a forward-pass probe. The intervention side is genuinely predictive in a second sense — the same signals localise rows and are used to remove them before training.

## How they judged success

AUROC, Brier score and balanced 50/50 accuracy on a test set resampled to half emerged, because at 22% positives plain accuracy flatters a constant negative predictor at about 78%. Calibration via expected calibration error and reliability plots. The seven baselines above, plus a self-forecasting arm and two fine-tuned-forecaster arms. Two independence regimes for the split — the curated capability split, 200 repeated random 70:10:20 splits holding out both axes, and two-way k-fold cross-validation with pooled out-of-fold predictions. Logistic-regression diagnostics reported properly: McFadden R², likelihood-ratio tests, Wald tests, variance inflation factors, events per variable, Box–Tidwell linearity tests, Cook's distance, and dataset-cluster-robust standard errors, since triples share models and datasets. A leave-one-training-model-out protocol for model-class selection. And for the intervention, a size-matched random-deletion control that removes exactly as many rows as the forecaster did.

## What they found

Frontier models prompted directly are bad at this. The 17 tested span AUROC 0.48–0.65 with near-chance balanced accuracy, the best reaching 0.653, and self-forecasting falls below chance at 0.448 — a model asked to predict its own post-fine-tune behaviour does worse than guessing. The decomposed forecaster reaches AUROC 0.801 (s.e.m. ±1, interval 0.774–0.827), 69.7% balanced accuracy and Brier 0.134, beating every simple forecaster, both majority-vote baselines and the label-only fine-tuned open model at 0.682. Calibration is good — ECE 0.05 against 0.11–0.12 for prompted frontier forecasters. Handing the same four signals to a frontier model without the trained combiner gets comparable ranking (0.785) but much worse calibration (Brier 0.206), so the trained combiner earns its place through calibration rather than discrimination. Training on labels beats training on chain-of-thought: 0.682 against 0.542 for rejection-sampled reasoning traces, the base model itself at 0.553. The forecast degrades gracefully as adversarial data is injected and holds at about 0.80 under the alternative splits.

The ablations are the interesting part. Alpha dominates — dropping it costs 0.14 AUROC, to 0.655 — and broad spillover B is the second driver. Gamma and the model probe b each add at most 0.002 AUROC of unique value, and b's effect does not survive dataset clustering. Moreover the combiner puts a *negative* weight on gamma, which the authors diagnose as a suppressor artefact of pairing a variable with its own maximum (r = 0.97, VIF 16): dropping gamma or constraining weights non-negative leaves performance unchanged or slightly better, so the counterintuitive sign carries no load.

The honest reading is in the two conditional AUROCs. Alpha alone scores 0.713 pooled — above every prompted frontier model — but is constant within a failure mode, so within a failure mode it is exactly 0.500. All within-mode discrimination, which is the practical question when screening one candidate dataset, comes from the content reads: 0.500 to 0.792. Symmetrically, reading the data does nothing within a dataset — the metadata-only forecaster already reaches 0.841 there and the full system 0.841. So the forecaster is a dataset-level hazard estimator per failure mode with a small model-side correction, and the authors say so.

The variance decomposition explains why: dataset identity explains 0.33 of the outcome variance, model 0.19, failure mode 0.08, and the model-by-dataset interaction 0.40. That last number is also why the between-split spread is wide — across 200 random draws AUROC spans 0.71–0.90 with median 0.82, and the pooled cross-validation sits at 0.801.

Downstream, filtering works on the MCQ evaluation and not clearly beyond it. Forecast-based filtering beats classifier-only filtering on UltraChat for most models, but against a size-matched random deletion of 244 rows the result is mixed: clearly better on two open models (Qwen3.5-4B, 0.0092 against 0.0408; Nemotron-3-Super-120B, 0.0104 against 0.0171) and worse on GPT-4.1 (0.0117 against 0.0073), with the fourth model flat at the floor. The authors report the reversal, reproduce it under an independent forecaster selection, and say they have no tested explanation. On the behavioural audit the forecast-based arm posts the smallest increase but its interval overlaps no filtering at all, so no behavioural benefit is established. And the absolute reductions are tiny — 0.0113, 0.0026, 0.0011, 0.0004 against a 0.005 shift the paper's own labelling rule calls too small to matter — which they attribute to clean post-training data simply containing little misalignment to remove.

## Limits and open problems they name

Restricted to supervised fine-tuning on 1,000-example datasets and one capability-based split; untested for other recipes, dataset scales, full fine-tuning, RL post-training and pretraining. The MCQ proxy may not transfer to open-ended behaviour, and the filtering gap may arise because the forecaster reasons at the dataset level while the filter acts on rows. The label threshold has a disclosed contamination: one benign calibration dataset, qa_health, is also a held-out test dataset, so its threshold is computed from a pool containing its own shift; recalibrating without it flips 8 of 426 test labels, all from non-emerged to emerged, which they report. They name the model-side weakness as the clearest direction for improvement, and propose row-level attribution, forecaster-as-reward-model for synthetic data, behaviourally grounded labels, RL from verifiable rewards, and withholding failure modes to test generalisation to unknown ones.

## What we take from it

A task definition, a benchmark, and — the most useful thing in the paper — a template for reporting a conditional forecast honestly. The paper's own Table 11 is the strongest artefact in it: pooled AUROC flatters the failure-mode prior to 0.713, and the within-group columns show that number is chance (0.500) and that all the real discrimination lives in the content signals within a failure mode, while within a dataset the content signals add literally nothing (0.841 versus 0.841). Any of our predictors that emits a score over a population should ship the pooled number *and* the within-stratum number for every stratum a user would actually decide inside, because those two numbers can differ by 0.29 AUROC on the same model and same test set.

The transferable measurement idea is the effect-size floor calibrated to the unit's own benign drift. Instead of asking whether a shift is statistically detectable — which with 200 paired questions it nearly always is — they ask whether it exceeds what benign fine-tuning already does to the same model, measured on six control datasets with the multiplier cross-validated against a held-out false-positive target. That is the same move the folder's decision benchmark makes when it demands the null action's loss be known, applied to labelling rather than to intervention, and it is cheap: six control runs and a one-dimensional grid.

The transferable warning is about feature construction. Broad spillover B is defined as the maximum of the gamma scores it is combined with, which guarantees collinearity (r = 0.97, VIF 16) and manufactures a suppressor with a negative sign that reads like a finding. The paper catches this and disposes of it in two lines, but the general lesson is that derived-by-construction features need their ablation printed in the same table as the headline, and the test is whether dropping the feature improves or matches the model — if it does, the feature was a relabelling.

Third, on the model-class selection: they could not use validation for the choice, because validation only contained in-range models, and selecting there preferred the flexible classes that then lost on extrapolation. Leave-one-group-out over the axis you intend to extrapolate along is the cheap fix and it changed the winner. This is the folder's held-out-setting lesson applied to estimator selection rather than to features.

What I would not take: the intervention claim as stated in the abstract's neighbourhood. The size-matched control is in the appendix, is mixed, and is worth more than the headline "its signals also flag problematic training examples that a frontier-model classifier misses." Two further points the paper is careful about and we should copy: model susceptibility b has a sign reversal between pooled and within-mode readings that is largely a mechanical consequence of defining the outcome as a gain over the base rate, and the authors say so instead of reporting the pooled version; and the chain-of-thought training loss is explained as a hypothesis ("we do not test this directly") rather than a result.
