# Decomposing Earth Embeddings with Sparse Autoencoders

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.11398
- **Authors / organisation:** Vitus Benson, Fanny Yang, Markus Reichstein — (EarthNet / ELLIS-type Earth-observation interpretability group; the acknowledgements thank the EarthNet team, and funding is the Horizon Europe WeatherGenerator grant)
- **Date:** 2026-11-08 (arXiv v1; NeurIPS 2026 paper checklist included)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.11398
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (arXiv HTML v1, ~164K clean chars: abstract, §§1–4, references, Appendices A–G including every supplementary table; the NeurIPS checklist was skimmed. Figures were **not** inspected pixel by pixel — every number below comes from prose sentences or from a supplementary table cell that rendered as an intact grid, and this is stated wherever a figure is the only place a result lives.)
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful) — not a language-model interpretability paper, but the sharpest published accounting in this folder's corpus of *which* SAE scores survive a frozen-random-decoder control, which is a question every dictionary-based claim in our own battery has to answer.

## What they wanted, and why

Earth-observation foundation models now release global, per-pixel embeddings (64 or 128 numbers per 10 m pixel) that downstream scientists treat as a reusable substrate. The dimensions are known to be entangled: one axis can respond to forest in one region and to irrigated cropland in another, which is both an interpretability problem and a robustness problem, because a downstream model trained on one region and applied globally can silently carry a forest mapping onto cropland, and a distance-based shift check will not notice. Sparse autoencoders are the obvious remedy and have already been used on protein models, transcriptomics, weather models and vision transformers. The obstacle the authors set out to address is not the method but the evaluation: language-model SAE protocols lean on an LLM reading the text a feature fires on, which has no established analogue for a satellite pixel, and Earth-science concepts are spatially autocorrelated, so the length scale at which a feature varies is part of what it represents. The larger goal is a scientifically inspectable Earth embedding whose features can support discovery and trustworthy global mapping.

## What they studied

Two public per-pixel embedding datasets, AlphaEarth (D = 64, one unit-norm vector per 10 m pixel per year) and Tessera (D = 128, per-pixel annual time-series fusion), plus a transfer check on three further representations: Copernicus-FM (D = 768, one vector per 0.25° cell), OlmoEarth and MMEarth (D = 768 / 320, 80 m tokens). Data is a random sample of 210,000 patches of 64×64 pixels (640 m on a side) drawn from the ~5.4M land cells of a 5 km global grid, split 100,000 train / ~10,000 validation / 100,000 test; reference labels come from a registry of 14 categorical and 3 continuous published maps (sub-metre roads up to 500 m land cover), aggregated into 20 concept groups and 159 scoreable product classes. The unit of analysis is the individual SAE latent (an "atom" activation map over pixels), compared against raw embedding dimensions, PCA/ICA/NMF components and a frozen-random-decoder SAE.

## How they did it

Three SAE variants — ReLU plus a sparsity penalty, BatchTopK, and Matryoshka BatchTopK — are trained with dictionary width 1024 (and 512/2048 at matched sparsity) and an average of 8, 16 or 32 active features per pixel, three seeds per cell, 90 SAE runs plus 18 frozen-decoder runs, 73 GPU-hours total. Every score is computed on a fixed sample of test pixels so that models are compared on identical data. The control that carries the paper is a **frozen-decoder SAE**: same width, same sparsity, decoder atoms drawn once as random unit vectors and never trained, with only the encoder trained — the authors describe it as the sanity check of comparing an interpretation on learned versus random weights. Three criteria are then applied. (1) Reconstruction at matched sparsity: fraction of variance explained against the mean number of active features. (2) Spatial scale: for each feature a normalised semivariance curve over pixel separations spanning 10 m to the antipodes, summarised by the correlation length (first separation where the correlation falls to 1/e) and by the share of the feature's variance lying inside a 640 m patch. (3) Concept alignment: each feature's activation is used as a membership score for each product class, folded so that a feature marking a class by staying silent also counts, and the feature is "aligned" with a concept group if any of its classes clears a folded AUROC of 0.65; features never reaching 0.51 on any class are called dead and excluded. Monosemy is summarised as the number of concept groups a feature aligns with. Two applications follow: (a) sub-concept discovery inside a coarse class, checked against three independent maps held in neither the registry nor the taxonomy; (b) using the distance to the five nearest training pixels *in the sparse code* as an area-of-applicability estimate for a downstream ridge head, with hold-outs graded by distance from the training data (88 folds per target), and every distance scored against the errors of one **common** head fitted on the raw embedding.

## What counts as an explanation here

A sparse feature dictionary: each atom is presented as an approximately monosemantic direction whose activation map, viewed at patch scale and at 2°-cell scale, is the explanation, and whose interpretation is triangulated against published maps and against the climate zone / biome / potential-vegetation stratum it concentrates on. It is explicitly partial and per-feature — there is no attempt at a circuit, at a computation, or at a model of how features combine. The paper's own framing of what an explanation is worth is unusually strict for this literature: a feature score counts as evidence about the *dictionary* only if it can be attributed to training the atoms rather than to the sparsity of the code, and most of the paper's results are statements about which of those two things a given score measures.

## How they judged success

Reconstruction: MSE per embedding dimension, fraction of variance explained, and cosine similarity, all at matched number of active features, against raw top-variance dimensions, PCA, ICA, NMF, a frozen-decoder SAE, and — in an appendix ablation — a "choose k largest coefficients per pixel in a fixed PCA basis" rule that isolates how much of the gain comes from per-pixel selection rather than from a learned dictionary. Spatial scale: median correlation length and within-patch variance share, cross-checked with patch-mean Moran's I. Concept alignment: distribution of the number of aligned concept groups per feature, the folded AUROC of the best feature per group against the best raw dimension per group, unique coverage (how many product classes *any* feature clears a threshold AUROC on), and two published monosemanticity scores (image-similarity and geographic-similarity variants), each reported with a "uniform level" — the score a feature that fires equally everywhere would obtain — and against the frozen decoder. Applications: qualitative galleries plus a size-matched, spatially compact random-partition null model with family-wise correction for sub-concepts; for applicability, paired fold counts and median gain in Spearman correlation between distance and absolute prediction error, against both the raw-embedding distance and an untrained *random sparse code* of the same width and sparsity.

## What they found

**Reconstruction is the one criterion that survives the control.** At ~16 active features every SAE beats every baseline on both embeddings (AlphaEarth FVE 0.939–0.973 for the three variants against 0.775 for the frozen decoder and 0.696 for PCA-16; Tessera 0.828–0.919 against 0.534 and 0.519), and with 16 features trained SAEs leave 2.7% versus 22% of AlphaEarth unexplained — about eight times less. The frozen decoder already knows more per-pixel selection than PCA (0.775 against 0.696), so the decomposition of the advantage is: choosing which components to keep is worth a lot, and learning the atoms is worth the rest. Holding the PCA basis fixed and keeping each pixel's largest coefficients closes 67% (AlphaEarth) and 50% (Tessera) of the gap to the trained SAE, which locates most of the remaining credit in overcompleteness, not in learning per se.

**Monosemy scores do not survive the control.** A raw AlphaEarth dimension is aligned with a median of 12 of 20 concept groups, a PCA component with 9; 88% of the Matryoshka features align with at most one group (8% with exactly one, 80% with none). But the frozen decoder scores the same (89%, and it has *more* strictly-aligned features than the trained SAE, 234 against 136), and the reason is arithmetic rather than representational: with 16 of 1024 features active per pixel, a feature is on for about 1.6% of pixels, so it clears a membership threshold only if it is concentrated on one class — specificity is a property of sparse codes, not of learned atoms. The same holds for the spatial-scale result (median correlation length ~20–33 km for SAE features versus ~1,240 km for a raw dimension, 23–32% of variance inside a patch versus 9%), which the frozen decoder matches (median 23 km), and for Moran's I, which does not distinguish trained from frozen. Two scores do weakly separate trained from frozen — unique coverage (140 against 129 of 159 classes at one threshold, single runs) and the published monosemanticity scores measured *above their uniform level* (the frozen decoder already reaches 69–87% of the trained value on AlphaEarth, 50–66% on Tessera) — and the paper's recommendation is that these must be reported with both the uniform level and the frozen baseline beside them.

**The interpretable features encode location more than concept.** The six highest-energy Matryoshka features carry 40% of the code energy and, for five of the six leading principal components, the closest atom belongs to one of those six (absolute cosine 0.51–0.79, against ~0.41 for the best of 1024 random vectors in 64 dimensions), with feature activations correlating with the corresponding component score at r = 0.69–0.83. Because activations are non-negative, one feature covers only one end of a signed component. The consequence is stated plainly: the dominant features are effectively discretised, regional pieces of the leading principal components, so a single concept spread over several continents is split across several regional features.

**Sub-concept discovery is suggestive and, on its own test, not established.** Three woody-vegetation features concentrate on wet tropics (80% of top patches in tropical rainforest climate, lift 15.6), dry savanna (39% hot semi-arid), and boreal/conifer (91% in two continental classes), and follow the matching strata of three independent maps. The strict version of the test — partition all forest patches among the aligned features and compare each sub-concept's lift against 50 size-matched spatially compact random partitions — **cannot distinguish the SAE partition from the random ones** (family-wise p = 0.14 against potential vegetation, 0.31 against biomes). The frozen decoder's woody features are more concentrated on a climate pair (99/98/99% of top patches against 96/93/96%) but cover a smaller part of each stratum, so the paper concludes that concentration alone is no evidence of a learned sub-concept.

**Applicability estimation helps for one target and not the other, and the help is not from the learned dictionary.** With one common head, the ridge-weighted distance in the sparse code ranks canopy-height errors better than the raw distance in 77 of 88 hold-outs on AlphaEarth (median gain in Spearman correlation +0.17) and 75 of 88 on Tessera (+0.21), but not for elevation (41 of 88, median 0.00; 49 of 88, +0.02). On AlphaEarth an **untrained random sparse code** of the same width and sparsity gains as much (75 of 88, +0.19). Leaving Europe out, the raw distance is slightly anti-correlated with error (−0.04) while the sparse distances are positive (+0.12 to +0.40), and the two spaces disagree about whether a pixel is inside the applicability region for 11.5% of pixels (Cohen's κ = 0.32). The authors' reading of the canopy/elevation split is that canopy height is tied to landscape types a sparse code marks with few features, while elevation varies continuously within every type and is spread across many dimensions, so no small set of features can mark a pixel as novel for it; they note this condition is untested as a predictor.

## Limits and open problems they name

Statistics computed on single features — alignment counts and spatial scales — do not separate trained from random dictionaries, and the paper states this in its own checklist. Many atoms are regional rather than concept-pure, so a continent-spanning concept fragments. Both applications are proofs of concept, not tools: sub-concept discovery has no reference to score the interesting divisions against, and the applicability estimate depends on the feature weights and on how features are scaled (unweighted, the sparse distance gains in only 67% of cells, and on one variant in 29 of 52), can hurt (it ranks errors worse than the raw distance in 11 and 13 of 88 canopy hold-outs), addresses covariate shift only, and still requires a threshold whose calibration the rank correlation does not validate. A direct test of the motivating mechanism — that polysemantic dimensions create spurious neighbours — is left undone, and the authors name it: hold out a concept that shares a polysemantic dimension with a training-region concept and ask whether the sparse distance flags it where the raw one does not. They also note embeddings that are already quantised codebooks (channels with designed meaning, discrete values) violate the superposition-and-continuity premise an SAE assumes, and are outside this method.

## What we take from it

**A control we should adopt verbatim: the frozen-random-dictionary baseline, reported beside every dictionary-quality number.** The portable form is one sentence of accounting — split each score into the part that sparsity alone produces and the part that training the atoms produces — and this paper runs that split on four different scores and gets two different answers (reconstruction: training matters a lot; concept alignment and spatial scale: training adds nothing measurable; coverage and the published monosemanticity scores: training adds a small, single-run margin). For our own read-outs this is the same question as "is this probe reading the computation or the input statistics", with a cheaper operational test, and it costs one extra training run we already need the code for.

**A second control, sharper than the usual permutation test: the untrained random sparse code.** It keeps the sparsity and throws away the dictionary, so it separates "the sparse code is a better space to measure distance in" from "the features mean something". Here the application that looked best — applicability estimation for canopy height — is fully explained by the random code on one embedding and about a third explained on the other. This is a warning for every downstream use of a sparse decomposition we build: if the downstream win survives replacing the dictionary with random atoms, the win belongs to the code's geometry, not to the interpretation we attached to it.

**The "which layer of the pipeline caused the gain" ablation, applied to a representation rather than a model.** Keeping k coefficients per pixel in a fixed PCA basis, and comparing that to an overcomplete learned dictionary, is the cheapest available attribution of an SAE's advantage to its three design choices (basis learned / overcomplete / per-input selection). We have no equivalent in the folder for feature dictionaries, and it is one afternoon of compute.

**A measurement primitive we did not have: spatial scale as a feature property.** Normalised semivariance and correlation length are a clean way to say at what length scale a representation varies, and the finding that a dense dimension is dominated by its coarsest component (median correlation length on the order of a thousand kilometres) while a sparse code carries dedicated fine-scale features (tens of kilometres) is a general statement about superposition hiding fine structure from any distance or probing analysis done in the dense basis. It is also a warning for locality claims: our own late-layer features may look global because the coarse component dominates the axis, not because the fine structure is absent.

**Sample-size honesty worth copying.** The paper names its own numbers as single runs where they are (unique coverage, the four checkpoints per statistic), reports a bootstrap interval over *folds* while stating that folds sharing a centre are not independent and that the interval is therefore optimistic, and gives the synthetic nature of its shift as a limit. That is the reporting posture the folder keeps asking for.
