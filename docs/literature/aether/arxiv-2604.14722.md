# A Mechanistic Account of Attention Sinks in GPT-2: One Circuit, Broader Implications for Mitigation

- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
The attention sink — a large, content-independent share of attention mass landing on the first position — is treated in the literature as one phenomenon with several competing explanations. The authors want the GPT-2-specific version of it taken apart down to named weight objects: not "why do sinks exist in general" but "which exact terms in the attention score, produced by which parameters, make position 1 win in this family of models". Their stated larger goal is mitigation and architecture design, and the argument has a twist: if the sink is carried by one identifiable circuit in GPT-2, then the right fix is to target that circuit; but if the same sink survives in architectures that lack the circuit's parts, then pre-training interventions aimed at preventing sinks are likely to be defeated by optimisation rebuilding an equivalent from whatever components are available. So the mechanistic account is presented as a caution against both universalist theories and one-size-fits-all fixes.

## What they studied
A single 124M-parameter GPT-2 (the standard Hugging Face checkpoint), a family with learned query biases and learned absolute positional embeddings. Unit of analysis is the **attention score decomposed into additive terms** per head per layer, plus the coordinate level of the residual stream: the individual dimensions that carry abnormal magnitudes. Per-head analyses are restricted to layers 4-11 of 12, on the stated grounds that the earliest and last layers show weaker sinks. Evaluation data: 300 examples, 100 each from SST-2 (natural language), GSM8K (mathematical reasoning) and HumanEval (code), all truncated to exactly 40 tokens. The secondary objects are the first MLP block's parameterisation of the positional embedding and three specific residual-stream coordinates (indices 138, 378, 447).

## How they did it
First an algebra step: expanding the query-key product with explicit biases splits each pre-softmax score into four additive terms — content-content, content-to-key-bias, query-bias-to-content, and a bias-to-bias term — the last three source-independent. The third term, the query bias dotted with the key projection of a specific residual vector, is the one they call the source-agnostic shift: it lifts or lowers the score toward a given target position identically for every query. They hypothesise the position-1 shift is anomalously large, and that it is large because the residual vector at position 1 contains the positional embedding after the first MLP has rewritten it. So they define an "effective positional encoding" — the positional vector plus the first MLP's output computed on it — as a stand-in for the net positional signal the first layer writes into the stream, and then check that stand-in numerically by subtracting the first-layer output of the bare token embedding from the first-layer output of the embedded-plus-positioned input, and taking cosine similarity over all positions on all 300 examples. Next, they test alignment: cosine similarity between each head's query bias and the key-projected effective encoding, per position. Then the coordinate test: find the effective encoding's outlier dimensions, and compare the magnitude of each head's bias-projection weight (the query bias dotted with one column of the key matrix) at those dimensions against all other dimensions. Finally ten forward-pass interventions on the same data, scored by a BOS-attention metric: the mean attention weight on position 1 from the second-half query positions, averaged over all heads and layers 4-11, with ±2 standard errors. The interventions are: nullify the query bias everywhere; replace the position-1 positional embedding with the position-2 one; swap the effective-encoding direction between positions 1 and 2; swap the raw positional direction the same way; nullify the BOS token embedding; skip all MLP blocks; remove all positional embeddings; zero the three key-matrix columns at the outlier coordinates; zero three random key-matrix columns; and baseline. The two swaps are constructed to be norm-preserving — the component along the position-1 direction is projected out and replaced by the same coefficient along the position-2 direction, mirrored at position 2 — so only direction changes. The three random-column and nullify-BOS interventions serve explicitly as controls for the three specific claims.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **circuit named at the parameter level**, with its inputs and outputs specified: a learned query bias, entering the score as an additive per-target-position term; the first-layer MLP's rewriting of the position-1 positional vector, which supplies a vector with a few extremely large coordinates; and the key projection, whose columns at those same coordinates are large in the bias-projected direction. The claim is causal and local — break any member and the sink weakens or moves — and it is explicitly not functional: the authors say outright that they explain how a sink can be implemented in this architecture and make no claim about why the sink is useful or harmful. It predicts an intervention's direction (remove the positional side and the sink goes; keep it and change only the token side and the sink stays), which is what the intervention table tests, but it predicts nothing about sinks in other families.

## How they judged success
The headline metric is the BOS-attention number as a fraction of baseline, with a standard error across the 300-example dataset. The load-bearing logic is a **contrast between a specific intervention and a matched control that spends the same intervention budget**: replacing the first positional embedding versus nullifying the BOS token (same position, different informational source); zeroing the three outlier key columns versus zeroing three random key columns (same number of parameters removed); swapping the effective encoding versus swapping the raw positional embedding (same swap machinery, different object). A mechanism claim counts as supported if the specific intervention moves the metric and its matched control does not. The descriptive claims (the shift is larger at position 1; the effective encoding tracks the net positional signal; the effective encoding aligns with the query bias where other positions do not; the outlier coordinates are where the bias projection is large) are supported by **distribution comparisons across pooled heads and layers** — separated histograms with no reported overlap statistic or test — and by cosine similarity medians with percentile bands.

## What they found
- **Baseline BOS attention is 0.563**, and the control interventions leave it there: zeroing three random key columns 0.563 (100% of baseline), nullifying the BOS token 0.561 (99.7%), swapping the raw positional embedding 0.557 (99.0%). The paper's own read is that the sink is not about the BOS token's meaning and not about the raw positional vector's direction.
- **The three pathway interventions nearly delete it.** Replacing the first positional embedding with the second drops BOS attention to 0.017 — 3.0% of baseline; swapping the effective encoding gives 0.035 (6.2%); removing all positional embeddings 0.099 (17.5%).
- **Partial removals leave a substantial residual.** Nullifying the query bias gives 0.251 (44.7%), skipping all MLP blocks 0.283 (50.3%), zeroing the three outlier key columns 0.367 (65.2%). The authors report this honestly as evidence that components beyond the three they isolate contribute.
- **The effective encoding is a faithful proxy at the sink position.** Cosine similarity to the net first-layer positional signal has a median above 0.82 at every position and above 0.99 at position 1; when the whole first layer (attention plus normalisation) is included at position 1, the median falls to 0.76 with 10th and 90th percentiles of 0.67 and 0.8. They note the comparison is only meaningful at position 1, because under causal masking that position attends to itself alone.
- **The alignment is position-specific and signed.** The per-head cosine similarity between query bias and the key-projected effective encoding concentrates at high positive values for position 1 and is negative for all other positions pooled.
- **The outlier coordinates are three.** The threshold is three standard deviations above the mean absolute value of the effective encoding, which selects dimensions 138, 378 and 447; each sits more than 15 standard deviations out. The bias-projection magnitudes at exactly those coordinates are systematically larger than at all other coordinates across heads and layers.

## Limits and open problems they name
GPT-2 124M only; the authors say scale could strengthen, fragment or replace the circuit. One architecture family with learned query biases and absolute positional encodings — RoPE and ALiBi models are named as future work. A post-hoc static analysis: nothing about when the circuit forms during training, which gradients produce it, or whether intermediate checkpoints use different pathways. The interventions reduce rather than eliminate the sink, and the residual is not explained. Mechanism versus function is explicitly disclaimed. To their list we would add two things visible in the text: **all four descriptive claims rest on histograms pooled over heads and layers with no overlap statistic, no effect size and no per-head test**, so "clearly separated" is an eyeball judgement on a pooled distribution rather than a measured separation; and **the evidence is a single checkpoint with no seed or scale replication**, which is exactly the condition under which the companion findings in this folder (a circuit necessary in one seed and absent in another) would predict the specific parameter-level pathway might not be the one another GPT-2-scale pre-train uses.

## What we take from it
- **A method: introduce a named, checkable proxy for an unobservable intermediate quantity, then verify the proxy before using it.** The effective positional encoding is not directly readable — what the first layer actually writes depends on attention and normalisation — so they substitute the position vector plus the MLP applied to it, and spend one experiment on a cosine-similarity check against a counterfactual difference. The reported 0.82-everywhere / above-0.99-at-position-1 / 0.76-with-the-whole-layer profile is the kind of honesty we want: the proxy is excellent exactly where it is used and visibly worse where it is not. For us this is the template for any "effective" object — effective direction, effective write, effective feature — we introduce into a white-box model: it gets a defined construction, a stated simplification it makes, and a measured similarity to the thing it stands in for, at the positions where it is used.
- **A measure: the matched-control intervention, matched on the *axis of the claim*, not just on size.** The three controls here are chosen so that each kills exactly one alternative story, and the budget matching is on the axis where an objection lives — same number of parameters for the key-column claim, same position for the token-versus-positional claim, same swap machinery for the processed-versus-raw claim. Our own ablations have been good at matched size; this paper is the reminder that a control matched on size but not on the axis tests nothing.
- **A framing worth importing almost verbatim: individually indispensable-in-reverse.** Every member of the circuit is necessary in this model *and* dispensable across the family — architectures without query biases, without MLPs, and without positional encodings all still show sinks. The conclusion they draw is that optimisation finds whatever components are available, which makes the specific parameter-level pathway an *implementation* rather than the *mechanism*. Mechanical rule for us: **when we identify the pathway carrying a behaviour, we report alongside it every known architecture in which the same behaviour persists without that pathway, and if such architectures exist we write "one implementation" and never "the cause" — because necessity in one model and dispensability in the family are both facts, and a mechanism claim that reports only the first is reading a fact about a draw as a fact about the phenomenon.** Cross-reference run 714 (a circuit necessary in s42 and absent in s271), run 713 (ablation damage is architecture-dependent), run 705 (a selector's collapse reveals which part was the mechanism).
- **A warning about the difference between "the sink moved" and "the sink went away".** The most successful intervention here — replacing the first positional embedding — is not a removal, it is a change of which position the model attends to; the sink's target is relocated rather than the phenomenon eliminated, and the paper's own framing (the circuit *controls sink location*) makes that explicit. Read against runs 705-711 this folder's recurring lesson again: a mitigation that moves a statistic has to be scored on where the mass went, not only on the drop at the original address. Concretely, any sink-suppression number we report is accompanied by the first-position attention share *and* the maximum share at any other position, because a 3% reading is compatible with a perfect fix and with a sink at position 2.
- **A shortcut and a caveat about it.** The coordinate-level analysis is remarkably cheap: find outlier dimensions of an effective vector, then check that the bias-projected key columns are large at those same dimensions. That is a two-step read-off from weights and a forward pass with no training and no attribution, and it is exactly the kind of thing a predictive white-box model should reproduce rather than discover. The caveat is that the outlier test is a threshold on a pooled distribution (three standard deviations, three coordinates in 768 dimensions) with no sensitivity analysis at other thresholds, so the three-coordinate finding should be read as a qualitative existence claim rather than a count.
