# TRIAGE-CPG: A Cross-Model Study of Budgeted Static Malware Triage with Large Language Models

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.21331
- **Authors / organisation:** Paul-Andrei Sava, Alexander Küchler, Daniel Kowatsch (Fraunhofer AISEC); Bernhard Grill, Jennifer Pullman, Yanick Fratantonio, Luca Invernizzi (Google Zurich)
- **Date:** 2026-11-16
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21331
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Analysts triaging unknown binaries have to decide, from a first pass, whether a sample is benign, malicious, or worth a human's scarce time. Decompiled Windows binaries contain hundreds to thousands of functions, so an LLM cannot read the whole program, and the authors treat the sensible object of study as a *bounded first pass* rather than a classifier: how a model behaves when it may inspect only a small, graph-selected subset of functions and must then commit or defer. The motivation is that the two error directions are not symmetric — clearing malware removes it from the queue, while escalating a benign sample only costs downstream effort — so a triage instrument should be able to say "not enough evidence" rather than being forced into a two-way call. The larger frame is the error–reject tradition: this is feature acquisition with function reads as the priced unit, and rejection reinterpreted as escalation. The paper's own goal is explicitly comparative rather than competitive: it asks how *different* models behave under one identical evidence budget, not which model is best at malware detection.

## What they studied
Windows PE binaries from VirusTotal, 1,000 in total, balanced 500/500 and stratified across five file-size quantiles within each class, with the label defined by VirusTotal consensus (zero detections for benign, ten or more for malicious, the 1–9 detection band excluded entirely). Splits: 200 train (EPS weight grid), 200 development (all component ablations), 600 locked test touched once for the cross-model comparison. Binaries have a median of about 300 recovered functions and a mean near 1,000 with a tail past 49,000; benign samples are both larger (median 682 vs 354 KB) and function-denser (median 364 vs 207). The unit of analysis is unusual and is the point of the paper: not a token, a function, or a whole binary, but **one model's evidence-acquisition trajectory over a fixed dossier**, with the priced action being the analysis of a single function. Eight models, three closed-source Gemini (3.1 Pro, 3.5 Flash, 3.1 Flash-Lite) and five open-weight (DeepSeek V4 Pro, GLM 5.2, Gemma 4 31B, Qwen 3.6 27B, Gemma 4 26B), chosen for availability on one platform rather than for coverage.

## How they did it
Ghidra decompiles each binary and an open-source CPG implementation builds the code property graph, but the evaluated workflow queries only the **function-level call-graph projection** — one node per recovered function with its decompiled body, internal edges for calls, external nodes for imported APIs — plus PE metadata (headers, section table and per-section entropies, import/export tables, resources, hashes); the richer flow and dependence edges are retained in the serialisation and never queried. Three stages follow. **(1) Seed.** An LLM-free heuristic scores every function with an Evidence Priority Score — a weighted sum of reverse-call-graph PageRank, a boundary flag (calls an imported API and has zero internal in-degree or zero internal out-degree), and log body length normalised within the binary — and the top slice is analysed once each, initially at 10% of recovered functions with a floor of 10 and a cap of 75. Each seed analysis returns a summary, a suspicion score, the capabilities the code supports, and the strongest benign explanations, and these records form the evidence dossier. **(2) Explore.** An agent with a scratchpad may read an unread function, list callers or callees, or find functions calling a given API; only reading costs budget (30 reads), graph queries are free, and the stage ends on the agent's own stop, budget exhaustion, or ten consecutive actions without a successful read. **(3) Report.** A separate agent works from the **frozen** dossier — it cannot read code or query the graph, only inspect what was collected within a 20-action allowance — and must return benign, malicious, or suspicious with confidence, supporting functions, capabilities, rationale and a recommended next step. All eight models get identical zero-shot stage instructions, action schemas and budgets; nothing is tuned per model. Tokens are compared on two readings of the same ternary verdicts: a *strict* reading where only a committed malicious verdict counts as positive, and a *triage* reading where malware is handled if flagged **or** escalated while benign counts only if cleared. Run-to-run noise is estimated by repeating one configuration (Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite) three times on development: 91% verdict agreement and a 1.5-point spread in balanced accuracy, used as the floor below which no cross-model difference is read.

## What counts as an explanation here
Two objects, deliberately separated. The **verdict** is a decision with an explicit abstention channel, and the paper's stance is that abstention is the honest output under a starvation budget — the "suspicious" class is not a hedge but the routing signal that makes the whole thing operable. The **report** is a natural-language behavioural description in four independently scored slots (malware type, family, capabilities, primary role) plus over- and under-claiming flags. Neither is an explanation of *why the model decided*, and the paper never claims one: the evidence dossier is an audit trail of what was read, not of what drove the verdict. So the explanation is partial, externalised and inspectable — you can see the function records and the citations the report builds on — but it is a description of the sample and a record of the acquisition path, not a mechanistic account of the adjudicating model. Nothing here is predictive in our sense.

## How they judged success
The primary metric is **triage balanced accuracy (tBA)**, which the authors define as the balanced accuracy of the binary clear-versus-not-clear decision — a malware sample counts as handled if it is flagged or escalated, a benign sample only if cleared — and they prove the useful property that a model which escalates everything scores exactly 0.5, so tBA cannot be bought by refusing. Alongside it they report the strict reading (recall, specificity, precision over the ternary confusion matrix), and for the suspicion channel itself **coverage** (share marked suspicious) and **queue precision** (share of the queue that is malware, against a 0.5 base rate, so above 50% means the queue is enriched). Their framing is that the strict and triage readings are the two extreme points of a cost-weighted family, so the *gap between them* measures how much of a model's apparent performance rests on deferral rather than on committing. Controls are thorough for the component claims: seven seed selectors compared with the exploration stage off (EPS, largest body, in-degree, out-degree, API count, EPS without its code term, and a random selector run five times), a seed-cap sweep (50/75/100), a PE-metadata on/off ablation, exploration on/off paired across nine seed-and-report configurations with a per-binary sign test clustered by binary, and agent-targeted exploration against random exploration under both full and action-matched budgets.

## What they found
Under the frozen protocol the best model, Gemini 3.1 Pro, reaches strict balanced accuracy 0.805 and tBA 0.855 while reading about 40 of a mean 1,000 functions per binary, flagging 65% of malware, escalating another 7.3–12%, and clearing 93.7% of benign samples. The headline negative result is that the models do not converge on one policy and do not sort by provenance: Gemini 3.5 Flash and GLM 5.2 sit at the high-specificity end with the top model, Gemma 4 26B is the recall extreme (88% of malware committed malicious at 0.370 specificity), and the best open-weight model (GLM 5.2) is 5.3 tBA points behind Gemini 3.1 Pro while agreeing with it on 78.3% of verdicts. Cross-model disagreement is far above run-to-run noise: repeated runs of one model agree on 91% of development verdicts, while Gemini 3.5 Flash and Gemma 4 26B agree on 48.2% of the test set. The suspicion channel is not a portable quantity at all — Gemini 3.5 Flash escalates 12.0% of samples at 0.875 queue precision, DeepSeek V4 Pro escalates 41.0% at 0.443 — so "what a suspicious verdict means" is a property of the model, not of the pipeline. The component ablations are mostly honest nulls. Seed selectors are nearly interchangeable on accuracy (EPS 0.775, out-degree 0.785, largest-body 0.765, random mean 0.724), and EPS earns its place only on *frontier position*: highest recall (0.92 at 0.63 specificity) with the fewest parameters. Targeted exploration beats random exploration on nothing measurable — no difference reaches significance — and its real contribution is economy, stopping after a mean 5.2 additional reads where random arms spend 11.3–25.4 without gain; against seeding, exploration corrects 30 benign binaries and hurts 11 (sign test clustered by binary) while for malware it shows no consistent effect (9 corrected, 18 hurt), i.e. exploration buys specificity and never recall. Rerunning the selectors under the full pipeline shows exactly that: specificities converge to a 0.70–0.72 band while the recall differences persist (EPS misses 11 of 100 malware, largest-body 18, random 13). PE metadata alone almost never supports a committed verdict (strict recall 0.010) but functions as a benign prior — adding it to code evidence cuts false positives from 56 to 30 at nearly unchanged recall (0.795 vs 0.670 balanced accuracy). The cap sweep is a non-result the authors keep: cap 50 gives 0.820, cap 75 gives 0.790, cap 100 gives 0.790, so no limit dominates and each size class prefers a different seed count, which is why the rule is proportional with a floor and a cap. Post-hoc cascading a queue through a second model prunes analyst load rather than improving the classifier — Gemini 3.1 Pro's 44-sample queue through Gemini 3.5 Flash becomes 35 samples at 0.943 precision with two additional silent misses, and the best cascade raises tBA by 0.005, inside the 1.5-point noise floor. Report quality is graded by an automated judge against VirusTotal threat-intel evidence: capabilities agree in 75–96% of cases, role in 66–83%, type in 56–76%, family in only 1–6%, with more under-claiming than over-claiming on malware (45–56% vs 13–22%). Wrapped samples (packers, protectors, installers, approximated by Detect-It-Easy and 31% of the test set) are the shared hard class — 44% of both missed malware and false positives, 61% of escalated malware against a 29% baseline.

## Limits and open problems they name
The comparison is between LLMs and pipeline components, *not* against trained static classifiers, commercial detectors or dynamic analysis — the authors state plainly that they do not establish that LLM triage is more accurate or cheaper than simpler alternatives, and justify it by saying a cost-matched design would be its own study. The base rate is balanced at 50%, which keeps recall and specificity comparable but makes queue precision non-transferable: at an operational prevalence the same behaviour yields far less enriched queues, so queue numbers are a comparison between models, not a deployment estimate. Labels are VirusTotal consensus rather than truth, and the 1–9 detection band where engines disagree is excluded — a band that may be exactly the escalation-relevant population. The budget excludes decompilation and CPG construction, so end-to-end cost is unmeasured; only Windows PE is covered, .NET is excluded, nothing is unpacked or executed, and the workflow assumes non-adversarial input even though instructions embedded in decompiled code are known to manipulate reverse-engineering agents. The report judge is one of the eight evaluated models, so report agreement is measured against threat-intel consensus and not by an independent expert, and no human study shows the reports improve analyst decisions or reduce time. One configuration serves all models, so per-model tuning could move the operating points, and model versions on the serving platform may change results.

## What we take from it
Four things transfer, one of them a warning about the rest of this folder. **First, the metric construction**: a three-way verdict with the middle class re-read as "route elsewhere" gives a score that an always-abstain instrument cannot game (tBA = 0.5 by construction), plus the paired strict reading so the gap between them is published as "how much of the score is deferral" — for our read-outs the analogue is a probe whose "uncertain" band is priced as a routing decision rather than as a threshold on the same score, and the always-abstain identity is a one-line sanity check we should assert per instrument. **Second, the asymmetry argument as an argument about *reading order*, not about accuracy**: a missed region of the artefact and a spurious flag are not equal, because a false alarm is recoverable downstream while an unread function is unrecoverable — that is why the seeder is chosen for recall and the explorer is trusted only to remove false alarms. Our probes decide which activations, heads or layers get a second, more expensive look, and the same asymmetry applies: the cheap first pass should over-fire and never filter, and the downstream stage should be validated for its ability to *retract*, which is the only direction this paper shows exploration actually works in. **Third, the null that earns its keep**: targeted exploration is indistinguishable from random exploration on accuracy and wins only by stopping early, which is the cleanest demonstration in the folder that the *scheduling* of extra evidence can be worthless while the *budget* is everything — any claim we make that a smarter acquisition policy helped must survive a random-targeting control at matched spend. **Fourth, the warning**: a near-identical ranking across five cheap selectors, with random seeding landing 0.724 against 0.775, says that on this task the first-pass signal is largely in the easy tail and the ranking is the wrong thing to optimise — our own "which layer/feature to read first" comparisons should be run against a random-ordering baseline before they are reported as anything but a tie. The caution the paper states about its own judge applies to ours too: a single model grading another model's reports is not validation.
