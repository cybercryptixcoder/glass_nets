## What they wanted, and why
Mechanistic interpretability by decomposition into features and circuits has been built almost entirely on language models, and the authors want the same instrument pointed at a text-to-image diffusion transformer, where two things are unknown: how meaning propagates across denoising steps, and how the separate image and text streams of a multimodal diffusion transformer (MM-DiT) talk to each other. They argue the two existing tools each stop half way: attention maps show where tokens interact but not how features are transformed, and sparse autoencoders find readable features without saying how one becomes another through an MLP. The larger goal is the one this folder is chasing in a different modality: a decomposition that is not just locally readable but predicts what an intervention on the model will do to the output.

## What they studied
FLUX.1[schnell] as the primary model (19 double-stream blocks then 38 single-stream blocks, 1,024 image positions and 512 T5 text positions at 512x512), with FLUX.1[dev] and SD3.5-medium as replication backbones. The unit of analysis is a **transcoder feature** defined by (layer, stream, feature index), traced at a single position and a single denoising timestep. The object that gets explained is the **preactivation of one such feature**, not an image and not the model: a graph is computed per prompt, per target feature, per step. Thirty-two transcoders are trained, one per stream for layers 0-15.

## How they did it
Each double-stream block's MLP sublayer output is approximated by a wide sparse transcoder (3072 -> 49,152 features, expansion 16, ReLU encoder plus linear decoder) trained to predict the MLP output from its input rather than to reconstruct an activation, as an SAE would. Because the MLP has no timestep input, and the residual distribution it sees shifts over the denoising trajectory, each transcoder is **FiLM-conditioned on the timestep**; the appendix reports that dropping this conditioning doubles text-stream reconstruction error at layer 6 and needs 3.5-5.3x more active features at layer 12, while the image stream is unaffected. A local replacement model then linearises the remaining computation without changing its output on the traced input: LayerNorm keeps its per-input variance denominator frozen, joint attention keeps this input's attention probabilities so it becomes affine plus a cached correction, and each MLP is replaced by its transcoder plus the cached reconstruction error. With active features held fixed, the target preactivation is an affine function of the inputs, so a single backward pass gives exact per-source contributions: activation times a "virtual weight" formed from the downstream gradient through the AdaLN gate and the decoder column, plus separate error and input-embedding edges. Graphs are grown greedily under a budget of 1,000 backward passes (about 40 minutes on one H100), then all per-position vertices of the same feature are summed into one, then vertices and edges are pruned by indirect influence (keep the 80% vertex / 98% edge mass). Case-study hypotheses are tested by scaling a feature's activation by a scalar at all positions, either across the whole trajectory or at chosen steps, with the effect judged on the final image by blind annotators.

## What counts as an explanation here
An attribution graph over interpretable features, reconstruction-error vertices and input vertices, with edge weights that sum to the target's preactivation minus a feature-specific constant. It is explicitly **partial and local**: it explains how one feature's preactivation was assembled, at one step, from earlier features in both streams, and it does not explain an image, does not model attention's Q-K pathway (attention probabilities are frozen per input, so the graph says where information flows but not why the model looks there), and does not cover blocks 16-56. The prediction it licenses is directional and interventional: which sources should move the target, in which sign, and therefore which features a user should suppress or amplify to change the output.

## How they judged success
Five separate instruments. (1) Sparsity-faithfulness frontier against an SAE of identical architecture, conditioning and training, differing only in objective, at matched L0. (2) A conservation invariant: before pruning, edge attributions must sum exactly to the target preactivation minus the constant, reported as a relative error on 86 traced targets. (3) Mechanistic faithfulness by **pairwise perturbation in the original model, not in the replacement model**: rank the top 30 feature vertices by indirect influence, ablate each one's contribution at its own position only, and correlate the graph's predicted influence with the observed change in every other vertex's preactivation (870 ordered pairs per graph), reporting Spearman and Pearson. (4) Blind human evaluation of 4,099 baseline/intervened image pairs across ten experiments, with a pre-declared closed label set and shuffled condition labels, three annotators on five of them (pairwise Cohen kappa 0.71-0.80). (5) A matched-budget steering comparison in which the target feature is fixed in advance and only the three companion features vary, against three activation-only selection rules an SAE could apply.

## What they found
Transcoders match or slightly beat same-shape SAEs on the sparsity-faithfulness frontier in all six layer-by-stream configurations, so the circuit-tracing capability is not bought with worse reconstruction. The conservation invariant holds to a median 6.49% relative error on image targets and 4.17% on text targets before pruning, degrading to 10.87% and 8.29% after pruning; aggregating positions is exact. The "what is explained" split is stream-dependent and the authors do not hide it: text-stream graphs put 84% of the explained quantity on feature vertices, image-stream graphs only 46%, with 30% sitting in reconstruction-error vertices - so an image-target graph explains roughly half of its own target. The temporal result is clean and consistent: over 20 targets the text stream's share of absolute attribution falls from 89.9% at the first step to 5.4% at the last while the image share rises from 10.1% to 94.6%, cross-modal edge fraction falls 14.9% -> 2.0%, and all 20 targets move in that direction; suppressing a text supernode removes the object in 72.2% of generations at steps 0-1 and in none at steps 2-3. The per-layer profile is non-uniform - image-stream growth concentrates in layers 0-1 and 4-7 while layers 2-3 stay nearly inactive. Faithfulness in the original model gives medians of 0.69 (image) and 0.56 (text) Spearman against 0.78 and 0.76 Pearson, with the Pearson-Spearman gap explained by vertical scatter at low predicted influence; the image Spearman is close to the 0.72 previously reported for cross-layer transcoders on a language model in a related test. Concept and context steering separate as predicted (identity stripped with shape kept in 87.5% of generations for the context set vs 27.1% for the concept set). A suppressor feature actively holding a competing concept down is found, and switching a dog prompt into a cat requires intervening on both what is present and what suppresses the alternative (36.1% joint against 2.8% for dog features alone and 0% for the suppressor alone). The matched-budget colour result is the sober one: graph-picked companions reach 82.1% requested colour against 76.7% for co-activation selection, a 5.4-point difference the authors call not significant, and what the graph actually buys is **selectivity** - 13.8% object destruction against 21.7%.

## Limits and open problems they name
A graph explains one feature at one layer, stream, position and step - on FLUX.1[dev] also one guidance scale - and on SD3.5-medium only the conditional half of the classifier-free-guidance batch. Attention's Q-K pathway is not decomposed. Only double-stream MLPs are modelled, because they are the only components internal to one stream; the 38 single-stream blocks would need 38 more transcoders. The case studies came out of an exploratory search over roughly 2,000 features and about 150 graphs, so the targets are not an independent sample; interventions were run at one or two strengths with no per-method sweep; the matched-budget comparison stayed inside one dictionary. Five of the ten blind experiments report a single annotator, and the vision-language-model cross-check shrinks the object-destruction gap from 7.9 to 2.5 points and loses significance under the model's labels. Their own note on the suppressor experiment is that the joint condition changes both the suppressor and the strength, so it is evidence that the channel contributes rather than an isolated single-variable comparison.

## What we take from it
Three things. First, a precise and reusable **honesty metric**: the fraction of the explained quantity that the circuit actually carries, split by source type, reported per stream rather than pooled - the authors' own summary is that an image graph accounts for about half of its target, and every circuit we publish should carry the same share table instead of a single "faithfulness" number. Second, the **ablate-in-the-original, not-in-the-replacement-model** rule stated as a principle: consistency inside the surrogate the graph was extracted from is not evidence about the model, so the fidelity test must run in the base model. Third, the Pattern lines below: a surrogate that is exactly faithful on the input it was computed for is not thereby faithful on neighbouring inputs (their preactivation error is exactly zero by cached correction, while the ablation correlation is only 0.69 median), and real gain must be stated as selectivity when a cheap selection rule matches the headline rate.
