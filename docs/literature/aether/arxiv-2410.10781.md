## What they wanted, and why
Attention sinks — heads that dump most of their attention mass on the first token regardless of its meaning — had become load-bearing for streaming, KV-cache eviction, quantisation and inference speed, yet the field was operating on a description of the pattern rather than an account of why it forms. The authors want the causal inventory of emergence rather than a single proposed cause, so they treat pre-training as four separable inputs (optimisation, data distribution, loss function, architecture) and vary each in turn on models they train themselves. The larger goal is the one every entry in this folder shares: a claim about a behaviour that survives intervention, not one inferred from a correlation in someone else's released checkpoint. Their framing reduces the question to what in the pre-training objective and the softmax normalisation is doing the work.

## What they studied
A ladder of open checkpoints (GPT2-XL, OPT, Pythia from fourteen million parameters upward, LLaMA2 7B/13B, LLaMA3-8B, Mistral-7B, each base and chat) plus a family of LLaMA-shaped small models they pre-train from scratch: hidden width 768, ten blocks, eight heads, FFN width 1536, roughly sixty million parameters excluding embeddings, trained on 5B Pile tokens at context 2048. Later experiments scale the same recipe to one billion parameters. The unit of analysis is the *head*: a head counts as containing a sink on token position k if its length-averaged attention mass on position k exceeds a threshold of 0.3, and the whole-model statistic is the fraction of all layer-head pairs meeting that test, measured only at a fixed length of 64 with no BOS in the prompt.

## How they did it
They first define and calibrate the sink statistic, showing that the measured fraction falls as sequence length grows and choosing the strictest threshold that stays roughly stable across lengths. They then check the phenomenon generalises: across seventeen Pile domains, across uniformly random token sequences, across repeated single-token sequences, and across small versus large checkpoints. The core of the paper is a four-way sweep on the small pre-trained family — optimisation (steps, learning rate, batch size, and a learning-rate-compensation run), data (token budget, randomising the first or second token of each packed chunk, fixing a designated token at position one/two/three, prefix language modelling with prefix length p > 1, shifted-window attention of chosen width), loss (weight decay ratio from zero to five, i.e. the regulariser as part of the objective), and architecture (six positional-encoding schemes, pre- versus post-norm, and a set of hand-built attention variants). The architecture sweep is the decisive one: they add learnable key, query and value biases to each head of softmax attention — a learnable sink token, KV biases, key biases alone with value bias fixed at zero, and value biases alone — then generalise attention to a kernel-similarity form divided by a normaliser, and finally remove the normaliser entirely, swapping the exponential for sigmoid or shifted ELU. The read-out is always the same sink statistic plus validation loss, so that every variant is compared at comparable quality rather than in isolation.

## What counts as an explanation here
An enumeration of sufficient and necessary conditions for emergence, tested by training, plus one constructive characterisation of what the sink token *is*: key biases that absorb surplus attention and write nothing to the value path. It is partial by construction — they say the phenomenon stems at least in part from softmax normalisation, and their kernel-family table is the evidence for "at least in part". It predicts a great deal: where the sink will sit under a given data pipeline, that it vanishes when token scores stop summing to one, that adding key biases alone relocates it, and that adding value biases alone does not.

## How they judged success
The threshold sink fraction (ε = 0.3, length fixed at 64, averaged over 100 held-out sequences) as the primary measure, with validation loss on the Pile-CC split as the constraint that every architectural variant must meet so a vanished sink is not just a broken model. Controls are the repeated-token and random-token input regimes, the pre-training variants trained without the component in question, and the bias-type ladder that isolates key-side from value-side contributions. Appendix C.1 supplies closed-form or upper-bound attention scores for repeated-token sequences under NoPE, relative, ALiBi and rotary encodings, so the "repeated input has no sink" observation is derived, not merely plotted.

## What they found
Sinks are near-universal in auto-regressive checkpoints — fractions of 92.47 (LLaMA2-7B base), 97.49 (Mistral-7B), 99.02 (LLaMA3-8B base) on natural text, still present on uniformly random token strings (91.23 for LLaMA3-8B) and, for GPT2-XL with learnable positional embeddings, in repeated-token input too (62.28), while rotary, relative and ALiBi models drop to exactly zero on repeated tokens; instruction tuning moves the number by a few points at most, and even a fourteen-million-parameter model has sinks. In pre-training, the statistic is essentially absent early and rises between roughly one and two thousand steps; smaller learning rates delay it and leave it smaller even with extra steps, batch size alone does nothing, and a token budget cut below the 5B-token default makes it disappear without overfitting being the explanation. Weight decay has a non-monotone effect: at zero the sink still forms, moderate decay (0.5) raises the fraction to 41.08, and heavy decay (5.0) collapses both the sink and model quality. The sink's *position* follows the data pipeline: randomising the first token raises the fraction to 27.03, randomising the first two moves it to position two (14.08 there, 1.98 at position one), fixing a designated token at position two or three puts the sink there instead, prefix modelling spreads it across the prefix, and shifted-window attention keeps it on the absolute first token rather than the first token inside each window. Positional encoding, FFN design, normalisation placement and head count leave it essentially intact. On the mechanism: the first token has huge hidden-state norm but *smaller* key and value norms than other tokens, so the sink is driven by cosine angle rather than by the product of norms; adding key biases alone moves the sink off the first token completely while keeping validation loss at 3.72; KV biases and a learnable sink token relocate it the same way at 3.72-3.73; value biases alone do not, leaving the first-token sink at 17.53 — so key-side bias is the operative part. Scaling the fixed value bias up pushes attention back from the bias to the first token (sink on bias 73.34 → 1.51 at twenty times the length of the unit vector, with the first-token sink rising to 25.88). Replacing softmax by a *normalised* sigmoid or shifted-ELU keeps the sink (30.24 and 53.65); removing the normaliser entirely removes it (0.44 and 0.80 on the proxy measure) at validation losses of 3.70 and 3.69 against 3.73 for softmax; an MLP-kernel attention has none either way. At one billion parameters, sigmoid attention without normalisation reaches validation loss 3.10 against 3.07 for softmax, with the sink fraction falling from 45.11 to 2.46, and supervised fine-tuning on about two hundred thousand UltraChat samples is stable at 2e-5 for the unnormalised model.

## Limits and open problems they name
The paper has no dedicated limitations section. Its future-work paragraph names two gaps: sinks on content tokens such as periods and newlines, whose positions are not fixed and which they did not study, and the entirely open question of whether the sink helps downstream capability — they demonstrate it is cheap to remove, not that removing it is free. Threshold choice is admitted to have no principled optimum; the 0.3 cut and the fixed length of 64 are empirical conveniences. Their scratch models are ~60M parameters, and the 1B scale-up is reported for a single architectural comparison, so the parameter-count claims rest on one rung.

## What we take from it
A **measure** worth adopting directly: a thresholded, length-fixed, head-fraction statistic turns a visual pattern into a number that can be compared across training runs and architectures, and its threshold-stability plot is the honest way to pick the threshold. A **warning** that dominates the others: their own numbers show the sink is not a property of the model alone. Two checkpoints with the same architecture differ by an order of magnitude depending on weight decay, and the sink's address is chosen by the data pipeline — randomise the first token and it moves to the second. For us this is decisive: **any read-out we attribute to a head must be measured on a model whose pre-training pipeline we can describe**, and comparisons across released checkpoints confound architecture with a dozen undocumented pre-training decisions. A **method** for causal attribution we can reuse without re-training: the bias ladder manufactures the mechanism as an explicit parameter (key bias alone, key plus value, value alone) and then varies its magnitude continuously, so each component's role is read off a knob rather than inferred from a correlation — and the magnitude sweep at twenty times the unit vector is the cleanest demonstration in this folder that a value-side write *pulls the sink back*, i.e. a head's attention address is chosen by whichever candidate is hardest to write to the residual stream. A **shortcut** for our own models: if sinks are an obstacle to a read-out, they can be removed at pre-training time by de-normalising attention at a loss cost of about one percent, rather than patched at inference.
