## What they wanted, and why

Grokking had been reported as an exotic phenomenon confined to controlled settings - large-norm initialisation or transformers on algorithmic tasks. The authors suspected it was in fact ordinary, and that the odd thing was not grokking itself but the fact that we only look for it on the test set. Their question is deliberately framed as: how subjective is the onset of grokking? If the test set happens not to show it, is there some other sample set on which the same network would visibly start generalising at the same moment? Once they have shown that such a set exists (adversarial examples), they want a training-time quantity that is causally tied to the onset, and they want it to be computed from the network's partition rather than from loss or accuracy. The larger goal is a progress measure - a scalar read off the trained geometry that tells you what stage of training you are in.

## What they studied

A wide sweep of models, deliberately chosen to be unremarkable: a 4-layer width-200 ReLU MLP on 1K MNIST samples (the main workhorse); MLPs of varying depth (2-6) and width (20-2000) on MNIST; a 5-convolution + 2-linear CNN on CIFAR10 and CIFAR100; a pre-activation ResNet18 width 16 on CIFAR10 and the standard torchvision ResNet18 on Imagenette; a scaled-initialisation (×8) MLP on MNIST to induce classical grokking; a transformer on modular addition; and a 12-layer, 12-head GPT trained on Shakespeare next-character prediction with adversarial examples built in the token-embedding space. The unit of analysis is the **input-space partition**: the input space is divided into regions, each of which the whole network maps affinely, and the object of study is the local density of region boundaries near a given input. The paper also reads the partition as a circuit count: every region corresponds to one active-subgraph configuration, and neighbouring regions differ by one neuron.

## How they did it

The measure is a layerwise approximation of the number of region boundaries crossing a small neighbourhood. Around each probe point x they sample P orthonormal directions and build a cross-polytope from x ± r·v, so the neighbourhood is a P-dimensional framed shape of radius r; they then push that neighbourhood forward layer by layer, and at each layer they count how many of that layer's neuron hyperplanes have vertices on both sides (i.e. produce both pre-activation signs across the polytope). That count, summed layerwise, is the local complexity (LC) at x. The expensive exact alternative, SplineCam, computes each neuron's zero-level set analytically on a 2-D slice; the paper validates the cheap version against it on a trained depth-3 width-200 MLP at 20 checkpoints over 500 training samples at r = 0.001 and P = 25, and against a zero-bias width-400 depth-50 network whose hyperplanes all pass through the origin. Defaults: P = 25, r = 0.005, 1,024 train/test/random probe points except MNIST (1,000 train points, 10,000 test/random), r = 10⁻⁴ for Imagenette ResNet18. They report train, test and uniformly-random probe curves separately, with 99% intervals, and compute LC on 2-D slices through three training samples to get the pictures. In parallel, after every update they generate ℓ∞-PGD adversarial examples from the test set (ε ∈ {0.03, 0.06, 0.10, 0.13, 0.16, 0.20}, step 0.0156, 10 steps for most settings and 100 for MNIST) and chart adversarial accuracy against training step, with Auto-Attack run as a check. Training uses Adam, no weight decay except 0.01 on the MNIST MLPs, no batch norm except where it is the variable under test.

## What counts as an explanation here

An **explanation is a geometric mechanism with a training-time signature**. The mechanism is region migration: non-linearities (region boundaries) drift away from the data points during the second descent phase and pile up on the decision boundary, so the mapping becomes flat where the training and test samples live and sharp where the classes meet. The explanation's evidentiary form is the annotated partition picture plus the LC curve: LC is not a summary of the model but a claim about what the model is doing, and the claim predicts that adversarial robustness should improve at the same steps that LC around data drops. It is explicitly partial - the authors state that they do not understand *why* accumulation turns into repulsion, and offer no theory of the double descent they report.

## How they judged success

The primary criterion is co-timing: a change in LC should precede and track the onset of generalisation or robustness, including on a sample set (adversarial) where generalisation happens on its own schedule. Supporting controls: the exact SplineCam comparison for the approximation; the zero-bias toy for the hyperplane count; a **deformation** diagnostic that measures graph eccentricity and diameter of the polytope after passing it through successive layers, used to justify the small radii (deformation is low and flat for r ≤ 0.014 on CIFAR10 and for ResNet18); random-probe LC as a third curve so that the network's movement away from data cannot be confused with a global drop; radius sweeps (r ∈ {0.05, 0.1, 0.5, 1} on the toy) and P sweeps (P = 2, 10, 20, 25, 50, 100, 200) for saturation; and one ablation per design variable - depth, width, weight decay, batch norm, activation, dataset composition (random-label memorisation), dataset size. The comparison standard is band-consistency rather than a numeric fit: no correlation coefficient, no prediction error, no held-out scoring is reported.

## What they found

Delayed robustness is widespread: on ResNet18/CIFAR10 without controlled init, PGD robustness begins improving around 10⁴ steps and nearly matches clean accuracy by 2×10⁵ steps, long after interpolation; the authors also report it for CNNs on CIFAR10/CIFAR100, ResNet18 on Imagenette, the GPT on Shakespeare, and the scaled MLP on MNIST. LC over training shows three phases - an initial descent, a sustained **ascent** up to interpolation, and a **second descent** which they call region migration; the second descent consistently precedes generalisation or robustness onset, and random-probe LC also falls during it, which is why the migration must be *toward* something rather than a uniform decay. In the direct partition evidence (Figure 26, same-area domains, step 95,381), regions near the decision boundary number 123,156 and 88,362 against 33,273 and 32,018 away from it. Batch norm removes grokking, and the argument is quantitative: with BN, the mean squared distance from layer l's data to neuron k's hyperplane works out to σ²/‖w‖², i.e. BN rescales each neuron so its boundary sits as close to the data as the data's spread allows - so the boundary piling that region migration requires is pre-empted, and the first descent disappears. Width/depth/data-size increases mostly expedite grokking (depth 2→6, width 20→2000, 1K→60K MNIST samples, batch 64→512), except that width 2000 *reduces* peak ascent LC relative to width 1000, and random-label data size delays it. Weight decay does not behave monotonically - it delays or removes the second descent and also shortens ascent. Layerwise partitions show deeper layers forming the large robust regions, and GeLU backbones (MLP and transformer) show the same dynamics as ReLU.

## Limits and open problems they name

No theory for the double descent in LC, and no account of why migration happens so late rather than immediately as approximation theory would suggest a free-form spline prefers. Region migration is shown to co-occur with, not to cause, robustness - the causal experiment (move boundaries artificially at step t and check robustness) is not attempted. The LC measure is an un-normalised density over a P-dimensional cross-polytope, so it is a shape statistic whose absolute value depends on r and P, and the paper concedes approximation error grows with depth and radius. No numbers are given for how much earlier the LC signal is than accuracy onset in any single setting; the temporal claim is made from plot alignment. Optimiser coverage is essentially Adam; SGD and SAM are named as future work.

## What we take from it

**A method, and it is the one this folder has been asking for since the partition entries:** local complexity as a boundary count over a sampled cross-polytope pushed forward layer by layer, with train / test / **uniform-random** probe curves reported side by side. The random-probe curve is the control that makes the claim falsifiable, and it is cheap - the paper reports 1,000 ResNet18 probes costing about 28 s on an RTX 8000. **A diagnostic we should copy wholesale:** deformation, measured as eccentricity/diameter growth of the probe neighbourhood through layers, which is what justifies a radius choice instead of asserting one; it is a direct test of whether the neighbourhood the statistic claims to describe survived the forward pass. **A framing with teeth:** LC is a *progress measure* in the Barak et al. sense - a scalar causally linked to where training is - which is the right target for a white-box model, since a probe that reproduces a checkpoint's state but cannot order checkpoints in time is describing a snapshot rather than a trajectory. **A warning:** the paper's central causal claim is an association between two curves, and the mechanism (boundaries migrating to the decision boundary) is verified by pictures and by four region counts on selected 2-D slices - so the mechanism is demonstrated, not measured, and the count near the boundary on one slice is not a statistic over the input distribution. **A shortcut for our own design note:** the migrated partition is exactly a *data-weighted* boundary-density object (run 725's local complexity) observed over training, so the predictive test our folder keeps naming - does a partition statistic on held-out data predict a behavioural quantity - can be run against this paper's curves as the ground truth to reproduce, with the improvement being to state the correspondence numerically instead of visually.
