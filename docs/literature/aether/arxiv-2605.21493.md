# Don't Collapse Your Features: Why CenterLoss Hurts OOD Detection and Multi-Scale Mahalanobis Wins

- **Key:** arxiv-2605.21493
- **Authors / organisation:** Rahul D Ray; Department of Electronics and Electrical Engineering, BITS Pilani, Hyderabad Campus
- **Date:** 2026-04-10
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21493
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
The paper starts from a split the field usually blurs: a representation good enough to classify well is not automatically good enough to say when an input is unlike anything seen in training. The authors argue that most detectors either reuse features trained purely for accuracy or bolt on an expensive uncertainty apparatus (ensembles, Bayesian approximations, evidential heads), and that neither route asks what the *shape* of the feature cloud does to a distance-based score. Their target is therefore a cheap, post-hoc pipeline whose geometry is chosen deliberately rather than inherited, plus an answer to the concrete design question a practitioner faces — should the features be pushed tighter together? The larger motivation is deployment safety, and the framing they reach for is the distinction between classification geometry and epistemic geometry.

## What they studied
A ResNet-18 adapted to 32x32 inputs, trained on CIFAR-10 (45,000 train / 5,000 validation / 10,000 test from the official split), with features taken from two taps: the 128-dimensional output of stage two and the 512-dimensional output of stage four, each global-average-pooled, projected to 512 dimensions together through batch norm and ReLU. Three out-of-distribution sets span the shift spectrum: SVHN as a domain shift (26,032 test images, split into a 5,000-image calibration half and a 21,032-image evaluation half), fifty semantically disjoint CIFAR-100 classes as a semantic shift (~5,000 images), and 5,000 images of clipped Gaussian noise as an extreme shift. The unit of analysis is the per-sample feature vector and the class-conditional geometry fitted to it; no neurons, heads or circuits are inspected.

## How they did it
Three stages, each frozen before the next. Stage one trains the backbone with cross-entropy plus label smoothing only — deliberately no CenterLoss — for up to 40 epochs with SGD, momentum 0.9, weight decay 5e-4, cosine-annealed learning rate from 0.1, early stopped on validation. Stage two is a one-shot, gradient-free fit: features are L2-normalised onto the unit sphere, class means are computed on the sphere, a single covariance is tied across all ten classes, regularised with 1e-5 times the identity, and inverted. Stage three trains a two-hidden-layer perceptron (64 and 32 units, sigmoid output) on three scalars — the log of one plus the minimum class Mahalanobis distance, the maximum cosine similarity to any class mean, and the softmax entropy — with binary cross-entropy against soft targets of 0.05 for in-distribution and 0.95 for out-of-distribution inputs, mixing in-distribution validation images with a 50/50 batch of the 5,000 hard SVHN calibration images and 2,000 noise images, for up to 20 epochs with Adam. Four configurations are ablated on a fixed seed: the full default with CenterLoss at weight 0.01, the default minus CenterLoss, a single-scale variant using stage four only, and a variant whose calibration mix omits SVHN. The default configuration is additionally retrained on five fixed seeds. Baselines include a softmax network, temperature scaling, MC dropout with 20 passes, a five-member deep ensemble, evidential deep learning, EpiNet, a five-expert mixture, and four post-hoc detectors (energy, ODIN, Mahalanobis, k-nearest-neighbour distance) evaluated on the same penultimate features.

## What counts as an explanation here
A *pipeline*, not a mechanism: an ordering of three named stages plus a fitted scalar score, with a set of propositions in an appendix asserting that each stage is the right choice under stated assumptions. The explanatory content is at the level of "why this geometry separates better" — inter-class margin preserved, covariance well conditioned, two levels of abstraction supplying complementary likelihood-ratio factors. Nothing here explains a prediction of the network in the sense the rest of this folder means; the object being explained is the behaviour of the detector's own score distribution. The paper is explicit that the calibration head is a posterior estimator over three hand-picked cues rather than a model of the representation.

## How they judged success
In-distribution: accuracy, expected calibration error (15 bins), negative log-likelihood, Brier score. Out-of-distribution: AUROC per set and averaged, and AUPR / FPR at 95% true-positive rate / thresholded detection accuracy are defined in the protocol even though only AUROC is tabulated. Controls: a five-seed stability study on the default configuration; a disjointness argument for SVHN (calibration half never evaluated); a note that CIFAR-100 was used in no form of calibration; an intra-class to inter-class feature-ratio measurement (reported as rising from 2.5 to 7.5 when CenterLoss is added) offered as evidence that the manipulation worked; a principal-component share of 54.9% reported as the motivation for L2 normalisation; and four theorems in an appendix — multi-scale sufficiency, Neyman-Pearson optimality of the minimum Mahalanobis score under a Gaussian mixture with uniform alternative, Bayes optimality of the calibration head under binary cross-entropy, and a "degradation" statement about tight clusters.

## What they found
Removing CenterLoss raises average out-of-distribution AUROC from 0.9366 to 0.9483 on seed 42 while costing 0.0029 in-distribution accuracy (0.9340 to 0.9311); the same seed-42 default gains from 0.9340 accuracy over the five-seed mean of 0.9344, so the accuracy cost sits inside seed noise. Per set, the improvement is concentrated in the two hard shifts — SVHN from 0.9163 to 0.9372, CIFAR-100 from 0.8955 to 0.9079 — while the noise set moves from 0.9980 to 1.0000, i.e. the headline gain is carried by a set on which the score is already saturated. Dropping the early-layer tap costs 0.0096 average (to 0.9270), and the loss is largest on SVHN (0.9163 to 0.8988), which is the paper's cleanest result and the one its own theory predicts. Dropping SVHN from the calibration mix costs only 0.0011 overall but 0.0249 on SVHN itself. Against baselines, the final model reaches 0.9483 average against nearest-neighbour distance at 0.8967, ODIN at 0.8870, the deep ensemble at 0.8827, energy at 0.8800 and plain minimum-Mahalanobis at 0.6790. The five-seed study of the *default* (with CenterLoss) gives 0.9337 plus or minus 0.0085 average, with per-seed values from 0.9209 to 0.9448 — a spread that brackets the entire 0.0117 CenterLoss effect.

## Limits and open problems they name
The calibration head needs real out-of-distribution examples, so performance on domain shifts should degrade where none are available. Everything is CIFAR-10 plus three sets; ImageNet and adversarial shifts are untested. Seed variability is acknowledged, with the worst seed at 0.9209. Future work is listed as larger datasets, continual addition of new out-of-distribution types, alternative density estimators such as normalising flows, and other backbones. Two limits the paper does not name, and which matter more: the winner (no CenterLoss) is trained on exactly one seed while the variant it beats is trained on one seed and the stability study is run on the *loser*, so no seed-matched comparison of the headline exists; and the calibration head is fitted on a distribution that contains the evaluation set's sibling (SVHN's calibration half), which the authors defend by disjointness but which still means the hard shift being reported is the one whose shape the head was tuned on.

## What we take from it
A **warning** and a **method**. The warning is the useful half: the folder's k=1 result (run 732: five of six classifiers preferred a single nearest neighbour, so what was installed was proximity, not density) gets its mechanistic counterweight here — a *richer* geometry is not monotonically better for a reliability score, because the covariance estimate that a Mahalanobis score depends on degrades exactly when the feature cloud is tightened. Tight clusters shrink inter-class margins, the tied covariance becomes nearly singular in the orthogonal directions, and the resulting score compresses. So both extremes fail for the same underlying reason: the score's diagnostic content comes from the *ratio* of within-class spread to between-class separation, and every one of our statistics either ignores that ratio or fixes it. The method worth stealing is the ablation discipline around a single hyperparameter (the intra/inter ratio reported beside the accuracy and the AUROC, so the manipulation is shown to have happened) — which is precisely what run 732's k-sweep lacked when it reported its optimum without the curve. Against run 731, the contrast is sharp: that paper made a bound finite by adding a density term and thereby kept every sample in the ranking; this paper improves a score by making the density *less* concentrated, and neither paper reports what happens to the samples in the region the score still gets wrong. Mechanically, for our own work: **a reliability score fitted to class-conditional geometry reports the intra/inter spread ratio it was fitted on, and any claim that "tighter" or "wider" features help is reported as a two-point comparison at matched seeds, because the effect size here (0.0117) is smaller than the seed spread of the configuration being compared (0.0085 standard deviation, ~0.024 range) and the paper's own ablation cannot distinguish them.** Cross-reference run 732 (density score tied with energy at chance near shift), run 725 (a bound repaired by naming its failing constant), run 730 (a composite score factored into rate-of-application times rate-of-correctness).
