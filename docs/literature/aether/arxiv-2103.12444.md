# Exploiting Sparsity in Complex Polynomial Optimization

- **Key:** arxiv-2103.12444
- **Authors / organisation:** Jie Wang, Victor Magron; LAAS-CNRS (Toulouse), funded through the Tremplin ERC StG T-COPS, PGMO/AMIES-RTE, POEMA and ANITI programmes
- **Date:** 2021-03-23
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.12444 (read as https://arxiv.org/html/2103.12444v1)
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 1 (background)

## What they wanted, and why
A polynomial programme stated directly over complex variables can be relaxed either by splitting each variable into real and imaginary parts and running the ordinary real moment-SOS hierarchy, or by keeping the complex structure and using a moment hierarchy whose certificates are Hermitian sums of squares. The complex route is cheaper but, above first order, provably weaker. Meanwhile both routes produce semidefinite programs too large to solve past toy sizes unless the problem's sparsity is exploited — and the sparsity machinery had only been developed for the real case. Their goal is to port the two sparsity techniques, variable-clique decomposition and monomial-block decomposition, into the complex hierarchy and then measure the trade-off they actually buy: how much cheaper the complex route is at a given bound quality, and how far its bounds fall short. This is one more step in the effort to make certificate computations scale, which is the same wall the earlier certification work in this survey keeps hitting.

## What they studied
Complex polynomial optimisation in the abstract, plus two concrete families: randomly generated quartic objectives on multi-ball constraints with up to 505 complex variables, and AC optimal power flow instances from a standard power-grid library, the largest with 2869 complex variables (5738 real ones), under three operating conditions. The unit of analysis is the block — one maximum clique of a chordal extension of either the variable-correlation graph or the monomial-term graph. A hierarchical or network object appears nowhere in the paper; the power-grid instances are the only structured system studied, and their graph is the transmission network, not a trained model.

## How they did it
They first restate the dense complex hierarchy, whose certificates are sums of products of a polynomial with its conjugate, and note the structural difference from the real case: the complex moment matrix has no Hankel property, because an entry indexes a pair of multi-indices rather than their sum — a point they say has to be handled carefully when adapting the real machinery. They then define the term-sparsity graph at a given relaxation order by joining two monomials whenever the pair appears in the support of the objective or of a constraint, and build the graph iteratively: starting from the constraint-local graphs, each round adds an edge when some constraint shifted by that pair lands inside the previous round's support, until the chain stabilises. Restricting the moment matrix to a chordal extension of that graph, and requiring only that each clique-sized submatrix admit a positive-semidefinite completion, gives a two-parameter family — relaxation order and sparse order. They prove the sparse order is monotone and bounded above by the dense optimum at fixed relaxation order, prove finite-step convergence to the dense optimum when the maximal extension is chosen, and prove the resulting block structure always refines the partition forced by the problem's sign symmetries — with a two-variable worked example showing the refinement can be strict, unlike the real case where the stable partition equals the symmetry partition. They then combine the variable-clique and monomial-block routes, and add a "1.5th order" relaxation that assigns each variable block its own minimum order instead of one uniform order, aimed at the AC power-flow problem where the objective and constraints have different degrees. Everything is implemented in the authors' own Julia sparsity library, solved with a commercial interior-point solver, and compared against the real hierarchy on the same instances.

## What counts as an explanation here
A hierarchy: a sequence of convex relaxations of a non-convex problem, each indexed by two integers, whose optimal values are lower bounds for the true optimum and which — under named conditions — converge to it. What is explained is the *cost* of the certificate, not the behaviour of any system: the claim is that a given bound can be obtained while never forming a positive-semidefinite block larger than a stated size. It is complete in the limit (finite-step convergence is proved for the maximal extension) and partial at any finite step, and the paper is explicit that it predicts nothing about the problem's solution beyond a bracketing.

## How they judged success
Bound quality and wall-clock time on the same personal machine, with the maximum positive-semidefinite block size (mb) reported for every arm; the number of instances whose local solution is certified globally optimal under a 1% gap tolerance; and relative gap to the real hierarchy's bound. Both chordal-extension strategies — approximately smallest and maximal — are run, and the choice is disclosed per row. Instances whose real-hierarchy arm ran out of memory are marked rather than dropped silently.

## What they found
On the random multi-ball family, the complex hierarchy at second relaxation order and first sparse order is over nine times faster than the real hierarchy with the same block decomposition while producing a bound roughly 20% below it — for example, at 55 complex variables, a bound of about −24.70 in under a second against −21.22 in about nine seconds. That gap shrinks with the sparse order: at 55 variables, raising the sparse order from one to two moves the complex bound from −24.6965 to −21.4174, cutting the relative gap from about 16% to under 1%, at the cost of the block size jumping from 6 to 38 and the time from 0.95s to 13.3s. On AC power flow the same pattern holds with less drama. At first order the two hierarchies return the same bound to the stated precision while the complex one is slightly faster; first-order (Shor-type) relaxation certifies global optimality on 19 of 36 instances. On the 17 that remain, the 1.5th-order complex relaxation certifies 6 and the 1.5th-order real relaxation certifies 11. The single most telling pair is the largest instance under typical operating conditions: 2869 buses, where the 1.5th-order complex arm reaches a 0.17% gap in 1666s and the real arm does not finish at all. Under congested conditions the same instance is 1918s complex versus unavailable real.

## Limits and open problems they name
They state plainly that they do not claim the complex hierarchy is better than the real one; it offers a trade-off between efficiency and accuracy. The complex hierarchy is generally weaker at equal relaxation order. Two of the theoretical differences from the real case are flagged rather than resolved: the refinement-versus-equality of the symmetry partition, and the fact that the complex "smallest extension" row frequently buys almost nothing in bound quality — on the random family the maximal extension is slightly *better* at more cost, and on several power-flow cases the smallest-extension complex arm is markedly looser than its own maximal-extension counterpart while the block size barely drops. Several second-order arms are simply out of memory. The largest instances still need nearly half an hour for a first-order solve.

## What we take from it
A warning, and a reporting rule. (1) The table's most useful columns are the block size and the chosen extension strategy, not the bound: the same method, same solver and same instance moves its bound by tens of percent depending on which chordal extension was used, and one extension is barely cheaper in block size while much worse in the bound. Any certificate we report has to carry its block profile and its decomposition strategy, because a bound without them cannot be compared to any other bound. (2) This paper is the cheapest illustration yet of the survey's recurring issue that an approximation's looseness is a property of the representation choice, not of the object being bounded: two hierarchies for the *same* optimisation problem differ by about 20% purely because one keeps complex structure and the other splits into reals. That is the same lesson as the sparse-certificate papers — agreement between two arms on one instance is a fact about that instance. (3) For our own purposes the relevance is low: no network, no trained object, no reading of a checkpoint. What transfers is the discipline of printing the block profile and the decomposition, and the observation that a "1.5th order" cheap relaxation is a legitimate intermediate point on the cost/quality curve rather than a broken hierarchy.
