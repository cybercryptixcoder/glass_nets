## What they wanted, and why

Sparse autoencoders are the standard tool for reading features out of network activations, but the authors argue the tool is being used outside the regime where it was derived: a single learned projection maps each input to a code in one pass, and the dictionary is learned by gradient descent on the same objective with no account of how the *geometry* of the code distribution distorts both steps. Their claim is that two failures are not separate bugs but one modelling choice — inference and dictionary learning are coupled, because the dictionary decides which explanations exist and the inferred codes decide the learning signal — and that fixing either one alone cannot close the gap. They set out to treat both as the same dynamical process, natural-gradient flow on one variational free energy, and then to show that the resulting model gets more out of every training sample and out of every dictionary slot than the standard amortised encoders. The larger goal is a sparse-coding account of representation that is justified from first principles (a generative model and a variational objective) rather than tuned as an architecture, so that claims about what a dictionary recovers have a stated model behind them.

## What they studied

The unit of analysis is the **dictionary atom** (a column of the decoder) and, downstream, the single feature that a probe or an intervention reads. Two settings: a synthetic benchmark with ground-truth atoms (SynthSAEBench, 768- or 1,536-dimensional inputs, 16,384 true features, Zipf-distributed activation probabilities), and layer-12 residual-stream activations of **Gemma-2-2B** (width 2,304) from Pile Uncopyrighted, at dictionary widths 16,384 / 65,536 / 131,072 / 524,288 latents. Baselines are **BatchTopK**, **Matryoshka BatchTopK**, **JumpReLU** (including released Gemma Scope checkpoints trained on billions of tokens), a **mean-field variational + Adam** iterative baseline, and — inside the synthetic sweep — ReLU-L1 and Matching Pursuit.

## How they did it

They define a generative model with a factorised Bernoulli prior over binary latents, a linear Gaussian decoder, and a factorised Bernoulli *variational posterior*, then write inference as natural-gradient descent on variational free energy with respect to the posterior logits and learning as natural-gradient descent on the same free energy with respect to the decoder, covariance and prior. For this model both dynamics have closed forms: the inference update is a feedforward drive minus a recurrent term formed from the decoder Gram matrix acting on the current posterior means, minus a per-unit variance correction, minus a leak toward the prior; the decoder update is the usual residual-outer-product rule preconditioned on the right by the inverse prior second-moment matrix. Inference is run for a budget of recurrent steps per input with no encoder and no backpropagation through that trajectory, using exponential integration rather than Euler steps, and the decoder update is applied as a matrix function on the residual with matrix-free solves. To test the pieces separately they ablate *(a)* the recurrent lateral term (removed, or made excitatory only) and *(b)* the geometry, by training with all four combinations of natural/Euclidean inference and natural/Euclidean learning, each with its own tuning. Finally they swap the Euclidean preconditioner for Adam in the conditioning analysis, and compare whole recipes against baselines whose dead-feature remedies were upgraded (MinFire gating, resampling, learning-rate decay, retuned sparsity) so that the comparison is not against a neglected baseline.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **generative model plus its inference dynamics**: a sparse-coding account in which a feature is a latent with a prior probability, a decoder direction, and a posterior computed by a stated algorithm. The explanation is not a caption and not a feature dictionary read off a trained encoder; it is the claim that the network's activation is a noisy linear superposition of a few of these latents, and that the posterior can be *computed* rather than predicted. It is meant to be complete with respect to this toy generative family (everything is closed-form under the assumptions), partial with respect to the real network (a linear-Gaussian approximation to whatever the layer actually computes), and it is predictive in a limited but real sense: it predicts the posterior, and the paper uses that to show an amortised encoder cannot match it in principle.

## How they judged success

Synthetic side: **MCC** (mean correlation coefficient between recovered and true atoms), Macro F1 and **Rare F1** (F1 on the least-frequent quarter of true features), dead-latent counts under a stated activity rule, all over 3 seeds with 95% confidence intervals and 10^6 held-out examples. Superposition is manipulated by shrinking the input dimension at fixed feature count and measured as the mean maximum absolute cosine between true atoms. Language-model side: **SAEBench** — sparse probing over 113 tasks with k = 1 and k = 5 selected features (accuracy, ROC-AUC, F1) and **RAVEL** disentanglement (cause, isolation, and their mean) — against trained baselines at matched width and against Gemma Scope checkpoints; the sample-efficiency claim is a token budget comparison. Cost is measured as wall-clock training time per fixed token prefix on eight H200s.

## What they found

Both theoretical results are clean statements of impossibility and of a rate. The impossibility: for a Bernoulli-Gaussian model, whenever an atom is neither orthogonal nor parallel to another, *no* fixed input projection followed by any scalar nonlinearity can compute a latent's exact posterior, because the posterior log-odds gradient changes direction with the input; this covers ReLU, JumpReLU (including Gemma Scope), gated and fixed-threshold BatchTopK/Matryoshka SAEs, and excludes TopK only because a TopK gate depends on every other pre-activation. The rate: averaging over inputs scales a rare feature's decoder gradient by its activation probability, so Euclidean descent needs the frequency range's condition number in extra time (a K^α factor under a Zipf law), while matched Fisher preconditioning removes the frequency factor entirely and gives every direction the same rate; with Adam and frozen known codes the local condition number is still at least K^(α/2).

Empirically, on the two-atom rain/sprinkler illustration the converged model's posterior KL to the exact posterior averages 0.0085 nats against 0.0216 for a numerically fitted best single-projection encoder, and the encoder's two units collapse toward one explanation where the model's do not. On full-width synthetic data the three-seed mean MCC is 0.898 with Rare F1 0.952 and essentially zero dead latents (0.33 on average), against Matching Pursuit 0.813 / 0.824, mean-field 0.784 / 0.726, Matryoshka 0.583 / 0.405, BatchTopK 0.536 / 0.298 and JumpReLU 0.483 / 0.187; upgrading the baselines' recipes (MinFire, resampling, retuned sparsity, L0 ≈ 35–45 versus the model's 35.7) lifts them substantially — BatchTopK to 0.812 / 0.585, JumpReLU to 0.861 / 0.729, Matryoshka to 0.841 / 0.846 — and the model still leads at MCC 0.897 and Rare F1 0.948. Removing lateral suppression at d = 256 drops MCC from 0.686 to 0.281 while an excitatory-only variant fires almost the entire 4,096-atom dictionary and reaches 0.283. On Gemma the model leads RAVEL disentanglement at every width, reaching 0.827 at 524k latents against 0.748 for BatchTopK and 0.685 for mean-field, with cause and isolation both improving (0.800 / 0.854 against 0.719 / 0.776); single-feature probing is ahead at large widths (0.789 against 0.774 for Matryoshka at 524k) but its AUC does not clear the best baseline everywhere (0.805 at 524k against BatchTopK's 0.803, and 0.794 at 16k against BatchTopK's 0.798); it matches Gemma Scope's single-feature score after roughly 6M tokens where those checkpoints used 8B, and beats them (0.789 vs 0.7735 probing, 0.827 vs 0.7555 RAVEL) at 50M tokens. Cost: 12–14× BatchTopK's wall-clock time over the same token prefix.

## Limits and open problems they name

Cost is stated first — repeated inference steps and numerical decoder updates make training 12–14× slower, and the two-readout cost comparison is done on a 999,424-token prefix, not the full run. Their rarity theorem is proved under **frozen, matched** posterior and prior second moments; the general case is bounded rather than exactly compensated. The variational family is factorised, so dependencies *between* latents are not represented (they name pairwise interactions as future work), and the local conditioning result for Adam assumes known codes and a frozen moment estimate. Reading depth is a genuine free parameter and the two read-outs disagree about it: sparse probing peaks within a few inference steps whereas RAVEL keeps improving with depth, so "the features" are not one fixed object across evaluations. Finally the model is evaluated on one language model and one layer.

## What we take from it

- **A method (the important one):** the reason a probe or a dictionary read-out can be systematically wrong is that the read-out is a fixed projection of a signal whose best explanation is *input-dependent*. Before we treat a linear read of our network as evidence about a feature, we should check whether the quantity we want is one that a fixed projection can express at all — the paper gives a worked criterion for the sparse-coding case, and the same argument is the honest null for any "our read-out recovers the mechanism" claim.
- **A measure:** Rare F1 (F1 restricted to the least-frequent quartile of true features) with an explicit frequency reference and held-out sample size, printed beside Macro F1 and MCC. Our account currently reports averages; a tail metric with a stated quartile boundary is the missing row.
- **A warning:** two evaluation axes over the same representation can rank models differently and the paper prints it — the model wins sparse-probing *accuracy* at 524k but loses *AUC* to the same baseline. This is run 585's direction-versus-behaviour rule again in a different costume, and it argues for reading any single "beats baseline" cell as conditional on the metric.
- **A warning on cost, with numbers:** the honest way to report a method that buys quality with compute is seconds per token prefix at each width on named hardware, which the appendix does (33.1 s vs 2.7 s at 16k; 989 s vs 70 s at 524k).
- **A shortcut:** "more dictionary width keeps helping" is a *capacity utilisation* claim, and it is only credible when the training recipe is not itself the binding constraint — so any width-scaling curve in our account should be shown for at least one baseline with an upgraded dead-feature remedy.
