## What they wanted, and why

SAE width scales well up to an asymptote: reconstruction error falls as a power law in width, but with a constant term underneath it, so past some size more latents stop buying much. That residual is what this paper calls the dark matter of SAEs — the part of the activation the dictionary never explains. Most work treats that residue as the failure side of a benchmark; the authors instead make it the object of study, on the argument that whatever resists a sparse linear dictionary is a direct statement about how activations are structured. Their larger goal is the ambitious mechanistic agenda: a complete bottom-up account of a network as features and circuits. If a fixed part of activation variance is permanently out of reach of any sparse linear account, then that agenda cannot be completed by scaling dictionaries, and the residue itself has to be characterised. So the question is not just how big the error is but what is inside it, and whether the inside is one thing or several.

## What they studied

Gemma 2 2B and Gemma 2 9B, plus Llama 3.1 8B, using public SAE suites trained on their residual-stream activations (Gemma Scope and Llama Scope) across widths and sparsity levels, with two middle layers used for the width-and-L0 sweeps because those layers carry the most SAEs in the suite. The unit of analysis is the SAE reconstruction error vector at a token — the difference between the activation and what the dictionary reproduces — treated as its own data object and modelled as three named parts: dense structure, error the dictionary introduces by approximating a feature badly, and features the dictionary simply has not learned. Activations came from the uncopywrited Pile over contexts of fixed length, restricted to later token positions, giving roughly a quarter of a million activation vectors, with regressions fitted on a large random training split and scored on the holdout.

## How they did it

Three instruments, applied in series. First, predictability: fit an ordinary linear probe from the activation to the squared norm of the error, and separately a full linear map from the activation to the error vector, then read the holdout R². A second probe predicts a larger SAE's per-token error norm directly from a smaller SAE's error norm, which needs only a single scalar and no new dictionary training. Second, decomposition: subtract the linear map's output from the error to define the linearly predictable and nonlinearly predictable halves, then run the same norm probe on each half plus on the activation, the reconstruction and the raw error, across hundreds of SAEs. Third, causal and behavioural tests of whether the split means anything — train a fresh SAE to convergence on each half and compare reconstruction loss and auto-interpretability scores from a judge model over sampled features with confidence intervals, then intervene in the forward pass by constructing the activation as reconstruction plus one half and measure cross-entropy recovered against the plain-reconstruction baseline. A synthetic appendix round-trip checks the estimator: pass reconstructions back through the SAE, inject controlled Gaussian noise at the input and at the reconstruction to stand in for the two error sources, and correlate the estimated components against the injected amounts. Model-based reasoning in an appendix argues that the norm of a sparse sum of almost-orthogonal features is itself linearly predictable, with the learned probe vector approximating the activation-weighted sum of the feature directions.

## What counts as an explanation here

A decomposition of the error, plus a small generative model of activations meant to produce it: the activation is a sparse sum of linear features with a dense component added, and the measured error is that dense part plus a term for dictionary approximation error plus the tail of unlearned features. The explanation is explicitly partial — it does not claim the dictionary has been completed, and it says outright that one component may never be representable by any sparse linear dictionary. It does predict: the model implies that error norm should be predictable while error vector is only partly so, that predictability should worsen as density rises, that the nonlinearly predictable component should resist a second dictionary, and that this component should persist as width grows. Two of those predictions are tested directly (norm versus vector predictability across width and sparsity; the constant nonlinear fraction under width scaling), one in the synthetic round-trip, and the rest are left as the authors' stated conjecture.

## How they judged success

R² on held-out activations for both probes; fraction of variance unexplained for the combination of reconstruction plus predicted error; reconstruction loss and auto-interpretability accuracy of SAEs trained on each error half, with confidence intervals and a fixed sampling scheme for features and contexts; percent of cross-entropy recovered under a forward-pass substitution relative to a reconstruction-only baseline, compared against two a-priori predictions (the component's share of reconstruction norm, and its share of recovered variance); and a synthetic control in which the true amounts of the two error sources are known by construction. Baselines for the norm probe include token identity, SAE sparsity, activation norm and model loss, plus a cosine check against the feature-shrinkage alternative and a random-vector synthetic case that is reported as a negative result.

## What they found

Error norms are highly linearly predictable from the activation, with R² running about 0.70 to 0.95 across widths, sparsity levels, layers and models; predictability is worse for sparser and wider dictionaries and for some very dense ones, and across depth it is low in the first layers, high through the middle, and collapses in the last few. Error vectors are much less predictable, R² from roughly 0.15 to 0.72, and here the trend reverses — predictability falls as width and density rise. The combination of reconstruction plus the predicted linear map cuts the unexplained fraction substantially; at a fixed sparsity in middle layers that leftover nonlinear fraction is roughly flat across multiple orders of magnitude of width, which is what licenses the paper's claim that there is a linear-error floor a bigger dictionary cannot remove. Per token, a larger SAE's error norm is well predicted from a smaller SAE's, degrading as the two are further apart in scale — larger dictionaries struggle on largely the same contexts. In the component tests, the nonlinear half's norm is markedly less linearly predictable than the linear half's, a second SAE trained on it converges to a higher unexplained fraction (about 0.59 against 0.54), and it scores about five points lower on auto-interpretability; both halves raise cross-entropy roughly in proportion to their size, with the linear half possibly slightly worse than its share. A stronger encoder at inference time (gradient pursuit on a frozen decoder) lowers total unexplained variance by a few percent but leaves the nonlinear fraction nearly unchanged, whereas earlier-layer SAE outputs explain up to about a tenth of the total error and some tens of percent of the nonlinear part — though the paper concedes the reconstruction itself explains half of that nonlinear error partly by construction, since the nonlinear error is defined as a difference involving the reconstruction.

## Limits and open problems they name

The flat-nonlinear-fraction claim is a hypothesis fitted across observed scales and extrapolated, drawn with dotted lines; the width/error-density grids are sparse and interpolated between trained dictionaries; mid-layer results are the cleanest and peripheral layers disagree; the model of activations is one assumption among several, with the authors noting the alternative that some structure is multidimensional rather than a sparse sum of directions; the component split depends on assuming the approximation error is not linearly recoverable, which biases the split toward the dense part; the synthetic check works on realistic data but fails on purely random vectors; and reducing the nonlinear component has no good answer yet — the encoder-improvement route barely touches it and the earlier-layer route is only a partial explanation, with low-rank dense side channels left as a suggestion rather than a result. The paper also declines to say what the nonlinear component *is*; it establishes that it behaves differently, not that it has been identified.

## What we take from it

Three reusable things. (1) **An instrument: probe predictability as a diagnostic for whether a residual is structured.** Fitting one linear map from the input to the leftover and reading holdout R² is nearly free, requires no new training run, and immediately separates "the model is not representing this" from "our decomposition is failing in a way that has structure" — which is exactly the question we face every time an account of a network leaves a large error term. (2) **A warning about what a completeness claim can consist of.** A residual that is predictable from the input is not an absence of knowledge; it is knowledge our account has not admitted, and a residual that resists a second dictionary of the same kind is a different failure. Printing the deconstruction of our own error — how much is linearly recoverable, how much is learnable by a same-family account, how much is neither — is a stronger completeness statement than any single reconstruction score. (3) **A scaling-flatness test for extrapolation claims.** The paper's central structural claim is that a component of the error is flat in width across orders of magnitude, which turns "our account will get better if we make it bigger" into a falsifiable curve with a floor; any account we build should be checked the same way, on at least two sizes, with the per-item split shown alongside the average. Also useful: the cross-account prediction result, since predicting a larger account's failures from a smaller account's is a cheap and strong test of whether two accounts are measuring the same thing. Warning to carry: the per-token breakdown shows the linear error shrinking while the nonlinear error stays put, so an average-level flatness result can hide two opposite trends underneath it.
