# Auditing Failure Attribution: Label Predictability and Judge Behavior

- **Key:** arxiv-2610.17747
- **Authors / organisation:** Tianyang Xu, Jing Gao; Purdue University
- **Date:** 2026-10-13
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17747
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Failure-attribution benchmarks pay a model, acting as a judge, to name the step or the agent that caused a failed run. The authors point out that a high score on such a benchmark is supposed to mean the judge *checked* the blamed step against the rest of the trajectory. If the annotation can instead be guessed from a surface cue — one step's wording, where the reasoning text happens to stop, which speaker talked most — then the leaderboard is measuring cue detection and not debugging, and judges trained on those labels will blame the wrong step whenever the cue does not hold. Their goal is therefore an audit of the benchmarks themselves rather than another method, and they want the audit to be cheap enough that benchmark builders report it next to the main score. The framing they import is the classic partial-input / shortcut baseline from NLI and reading comprehension, extended with the step that those baselines never took: showing a cue exists is not the same as showing the judge uses it.

## What they studied
Three released failure-attribution benchmarks, chosen because they differ in how failures arise and how answers are scored. Who&When Pro injects one error into a step of a multi-agent run and asks for the faulty step; the authors use its 6,257 text trajectories across eight agent systems for the rule-based predictors, and concentrate the judge experiments on the 311 ALFAgent trajectories (119 task groups, 30 steps each) because it is the only text system where injection makes the reasoning stop at the error; smolagents (3,276 trajectories, 1,411 task groups) is used to check that the finding is not just about that boundary. AFTraj mixes injected errors with naturally occurring agent failures and asks for the first faulty turn, evaluated on its 163-failure first-alarm test (108 injected, 55 natural); a separate experiment uses 468 agentic failures from its corpus (148 injected, 320 natural). Aegis asks for the *set* of faulty speakers in a 600-conversation multi-agent setting (441 query groups). Video trajectories (168 DVD, 748 EVA) get a smaller position audit. The unit of analysis is not a neuron or a feature but a whole attribution decision: the predicted step or speaker set, compared against a released annotation, with models ranging from GPT-4.1, Claude Sonnet 5, o3, DeepSeek V4 Pro, Qwen3-8B and Qwen3-32B down to a logistic regression on TF–IDF and a probe on frozen Qwen3-8B hidden states.

## How they did it
The paper builds four tests and runs them where they apply. *Input restriction* gives a predictor only a fragment of the input — one step's text with field names stripped, step position, the presence or length of reasoning, or the ordered list of speaker names — and asks whether that fragment alone recovers the annotation; the predictor scores each step independently and takes the highest, so by construction it cannot compare steps. For ALFAgent this is a logistic regression on 20,000 unigram and bigram TF–IDF features, trained with five folds that keep whole task groups together. *Cue following* holds the error fixed and moves the cue: they rewrite the empty reasoning fields after the error with either a generic sentence or task-related prose written by DeepSeek V4 Pro, which pushes the last step with reasoning to step 30 while leaving the annotated error where it was, and then counts how many of the judge's previously correct answers migrate to the new position. The generation prompt for the added prose deliberately withholds the label, the error mode, the judges' answers, later steps and the other passages, and every added passage is checked to be 12–55 words and free of error vocabulary. They also paraphrase the reasoning at the error step (and, as a control, at the step before it) and measure the paired accuracy change. *Residual accuracy* scores the judge only on the records the cue-only predictor gets wrong, and because accuracy differences decompose into gains minus losses they report both counts rather than the net. *Aggregation* holds every prediction fixed and recomputes the score under other weights or another metric: for AFTraj they solve for the injected-vs-natural mixing weight at which two methods swap rank and compare it with the released mixture, and for Aegis they pit micro-F1 against exact-set accuracy. All intervals are 95% bootstrap over task groups (query groups for Aegis), 10,000 resamples, both methods scored on the same resample. Finally they fine-tune Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Qwen3-8B with LoRA on the ALFAgent labels, two seeds each, on the same task-disjoint folds, to see what a judge learns from the labels rather than from checking.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not a mechanistic account and not an internal representation — the paper's object of explanation is a *score*. What it produces is a diagnostic battery plus a reporting template: four tests whose joint output is supposed to license or deny the claim "this judge does a cross-step check". The intended epistemic status is deliberately partial and procedural. A cue-only predictor matching the judge's score does not prove the judge is cheating; it proves the score cannot distinguish a checker from a cue-user, which is enough to invalidate the leaderboard's inference. The authors are explicit that the tests complement each other and that on each benchmark they audited, one test alone would have produced a wrong conclusion. The nearest thing to a predictive claim is the failure-mix weight at which two methods exchange ranks: that number is a prediction about what would happen to the leaderboard if the benchmark were rebuilt with a different composition of failures, and it holds the predictions fixed so that it is genuinely about the benchmark's construction.

## How they judged success
A test counts as finding a problem when the restricted-input predictor's exact accuracy (or micro-F1 for speaker sets) matches or beats the judge's, or when the judge's answers move with a moved cue. Precise instruments: exact-step accuracy and exact-set accuracy as the primary scores; micro-F1 and macro-F1 as the Aegis metrics under audit; paired changes with task-group bootstrap intervals; gains `G` and losses `L` reported separately with their difference equal to the accuracy gap; boundary, position and fixed-turn rules chosen only on groups disjoint from the ones they are scored on (halves swapped, or five-fold task-group cross-validation); a paraphrase control that rewrites a different step to confirm a wording effect is specific to the cue-bearing text; and a review of the generated text by humans (71 of 72 Task-continued passages pass, 56 of 60 paraphrase pairs rated meaning-equivalent). Fine-tuning and zero-shot judges are held to the same prompt so that the comparison is not confounded by prompt style.

## What they found
On every one of the three benchmarks, a predictor that performs no cross-step check matches or beats GPT-4.1. On ALFAgent, a logistic regression reading only one step's reasoning picks the annotated step in all 311 trajectories (100.0% on both full step text and reasoning alone), while GPT-4.1 gets 58.8% and Claude Sonnet 5 57.2%; without the reasoning field accuracy falls to 21.2%, and deleting recurring phrases such as "repetitive loop" leaves 98.4%, so it is not one telltale string but a general vocabulary of progress and looping — top positive weights go to *already*, *can*, *keeps*, *so*, *now*. A parameter-free boundary rule (take the last step that has reasoning) is right on 310 of 311 without reading a word, which is an artefact of how the benchmark injects errors. On smolagents, where the reasoning does not stop at the error, the same classifier still reaches 95.2%, and 90.8% on the subset whose error is not final, so the text itself carries the signal. Cue following splits the judges cleanly. Zero-shot judges use the reasoning but do not track the boundary: adding a generic sentence moves 1 of their 536 correct answers at the old boundary to step 30, task-related prose moves 0 of 361, and the worst accuracy cost is 9.0 points — so their answers do not follow the cue, yet they also never approach the 100% the cue allows. Fine-tuned judges behave in the opposite way: all four runs reach 99.7–100%, 41 points above GPT-4.1, but adding task-related reasoning after the error costs them 97.7 and 99.0 points for the 1.5B model and 35.7 and 30.9 for the 8B model, and they too barely follow — at most 8.4% of their old-boundary answers move to step 30. That dissociation is the paper's sharpest finding: a judge can depend on a cue without following it, so a follow-rate test alone would exonerate them while the accuracy collapse convicts them. The controls are tight — the same sentence added *before* the error costs nothing (+0.3 and 0.0 points), paraphrasing the error step costs nothing (0.0 points for both seeds), and the five strongest error-step words are all rarer in the added passages than in the original reasoning, so the loss is not the added text talking about errors. On AFTraj, current-turn TF–IDF ties GPT-4.1 at 82 of 163 (50.3%) but shares only 36 correct failures, and the tie is manufactured by the failure mix: TF–IDF leads by 17.6 points on injected failures and trails by 34.5 on natural ones, which cancel at the released 66.3% injected share. Probe, the best method at 62.0%, overtakes DeepSeek V4 Pro only when injected failures exceed 63.0% of the weight, an interval [38.1, 92.0] that contains both the released share and the corpus share. On Aegis, micro-F1 ties GPT-4.1 with a predictor that labels every speaker faulty (50.0% against 49.2%, interval including zero) and the benchmark's own macro-F1 even ranks the predictor higher, while exact-set accuracy separates them by 19.2 points [15.6, 22.8]. On video, a final-step guess beats GPT-4.1 by 3.5 points averaged over the two systems.

## Limits and open problems they name
Cue following is run only on ALFAgent, the one system where a single step's text recovers every label and every error comes from one injection pipeline, so the fine-tuned-judge result covers one subset, two model sizes and two seeds. The judges in that experiment answer the authors' own controlled prompt rather than the benchmark's (the benchmark's prompt scores *lower*, 50.8% against 58.8% for GPT-4.1). Aegis is evaluated on one data revision, and GPT-4.1's Aegis numbers cannot be compared with the original paper's because the public data has changed. The authors are candid about competing explanations they cannot rule out: the error-step text may simply state the mistake clearly enough for a careful reader to spot from that one step, or it may carry injection traces, and the design does not separate the two; the paraphrase test preserves the claims, so it cannot show whether judges rely on the claims rather than the words; the automatic meaning-preservation check for paraphrases used GPT-4.1, which is also a judge, so only the 60 human-reviewed pairs are independent. On AFTraj, every coding failure is injected, so source and domain are confounded, and inside the agentic domain the injected-minus-natural gaps all have intervals containing zero. Scoring is against the released annotation only, so a judge that blames a different but also valid step counts as wrong. They also note the dual-use risk: documenting the cues makes score inflation easier, which is why the tests are recommended as mandatory reporting.

## What we take from it
Four instruments and one warning, all directly transferable to our white-box model. (1) **The residual-accuracy pair as a mandatory reporting unit**: whenever we claim a readout beats a baseline, report the records gained and the records lost separately, because a near-zero accuracy gap can hide large opposite flows — here GPT-4.1 and TF–IDF both score 82 of 163 while sharing 36, and on DVD the judge's 60.0% residual accuracy overrates it because it misses 39 errors the trivial rule gets. Our probes should carry the same table. (2) **Cue following as the test that distinguishes reliance from following**, which is a distinction our steering literature badly needs: a component can be causal for an answer without the answer moving *toward* the counterfactual the intervention nominally creates — the fine-tuned judges lose up to 99 points while at most 8.4% of them move to the new boundary, and their lost answers land mostly at step 29, just past the error. For us: after any intervention, report both whether answers move to the intended target and where the answers that changed actually land. (3) **The crossing-weight calculation as a compact way to say a ranking is an artefact of composition**: fit the accuracy difference as a linear function of the mixing weight, solve for the zero crossing, print the crossing and the released share side by side. Any of our benchmarks with heterogeneous subsets should report this instead of a single pooled number, and the same algebra applies to a proportion-of-corpus or proportion-of-length weighting. (4) **The leakage-by-construction audit that runs before any probe is trained**: encode the label from a restricted view of each input alone (one step, one turn's words, the position, the speaker list) on task-disjoint folds. If that hits ceiling, the target is predictable without the capability we claim to measure — here the last-step-with-reasoning rule reaches 99.7% because of how the benchmark was built, which is the exact failure mode we must guard against when we build matched-pair and intervention datasets. The warning: positional and formatting artefacts live in the *labeling process*, so an audit of the benchmark is a prerequisite for trusting any method score computed on it, including our own.
