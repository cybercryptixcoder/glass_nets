## What they wanted, and why
Work on emergent communication between neural agents reliably produces protocols that work and cannot be read: a speaker and a listener trained together on the same objective are free to agree on any encoding, so the "message" carries the concept only via a convention the pair invented. The authors argue the same thing happens whenever a teacher network picks examples for a student network trained jointly with it — the two become an encoder and a decoder, and the examples become arbitrary tokens. Their proposal is a single change to the optimisation schedule rather than a constraint on the model, and it is borrowed from cognitive-science work on rational pedagogy: teach the student first on ordinary examples, then fit the teacher to that fixed student. The goal is not a network that reports its own strategy but a teaching policy whose behaviour is legible to a human looking at the examples — a human-readable artefact rather than an internal account. The paper is explicitly a first step, framed as an account of why human protocols differ from machine ones (humans cannot jointly optimise with each other).

## What they studied
No language model. Both parties are deep recurrent networks: a student S that receives a growing sequence of examples and outputs a running estimate of the concept, and a teacher T that receives the true concept plus S's current estimate and emits the next example (directly for continuous example spaces, via a Gumbel-Softmax sample for discrete ones). Four concept families: axis-aligned rectangles in the plane (the "rectangle game", with S regressing min/max coordinates from points); bimodal 1-D Gaussian mixtures (modes to be located from two line lengths); boolean concepts over images generated from four properties — size, colour, shape, border — of which one to three define membership; and ImageNet subtrees (apes, parrots, felines, with 16, 19 and 57 possible concepts) where concepts are internal nodes, examples are leaf images embedded with a pretrained ResNet-50 into 2048 dimensions, and only leaf images may be shown. The unit of analysis is the teaching *policy* — the sequence of two examples T emits — not any internal feature of either network.

## How they did it
Two training procedures are compared throughout. Joint optimisation mirrors an autoencoder: sample a concept, roll out T and S together for the fixed number of teaching steps, and take gradients into both. Best-response (BR) optimisation runs in two stages: train S on the prior distribution of non-pedagogical examples (ten for rectangles, five elsewhere), then freeze S and train T for two teaching steps. The BR loop itself is a single best response, not iterated to equilibrium — the authors say additional best responses bought no more legibility, and that joint fine-tuning after the S pre-training returned results indistinguishable from plain joint training. The rational-pedagogy framing is that a uniform prior over concept-consistent examples plus one round of the teacher/student recursion is enough, so the method is an approximation to a fixed point rather than a search for one. Baselines are a random policy that samples examples from the prior and the jointly-trained teacher, scored against a hand-written intuitive strategy per task. Legibility is measured in two independent ways: similarity of the produced examples to that intuitive strategy, and — for two of the four tasks — a human study on Amazon Mechanical Turk where subjects saw two examples from either the best-response teacher or the random policy and were then tested.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not an account of a network. The object that carries the understanding is the *example sequence*: the claim is that a policy which only works by exploiting the natural mapping between concepts and examples is one whose choices a person can read off, whereas a jointly-trained policy that works equally well may be unreadable because it exploits a convention instead. Two consequences are worth naming. First, the explanation is contrastive by construction — the same learned ability judged legible or not depending on which strategy it rests on, with the joint arm as the matched-but-illegible control. Second, the paper does not claim completeness: it matches a small set of hand-written strategies in four toy domains and says nothing about whether a legible strategy is the correct description of what T computes internally. It predicts the shape of future examples, not the answer to any held-out question about the teacher's state.

## How they judged success
Two criteria, both named explicitly rather than collapsed into one "interpretability" score. (1) **Strategy similarity:** per task, a distance from T's examples to the intuitive strategy — for rectangles, the distance of the two emitted points to the nearest pair of opposite corners of the true rectangle; for Gaussians, an L2 distance between the sorted emitted points and the sorted true modes; for boolean concepts, the fraction of trials in which the two examples share only the properties the concept requires; for hierarchies, the fraction of trials in which the lowest common ancestor of the two shown leaves is the target node. Compared against the random policy and the jointly-trained teacher, averaged over concepts. (2) **Teaching humans:** accuracy of Turkers on held-out test items after seeing teacher-chosen versus random examples, with the difference tested by a likelihood-ratio test on an ordinary logit model (their citation: Jaeger 2008). No measure of what the student network learned internally, no faithfulness metric and no ablation of the reordering itself beyond the joint/BR pair.

## What they found
The BR teacher reproduces the intuitive strategy in every domain and the jointly-trained teacher does not. On rectangles, BR emits points close to opposite corners of the true rectangle while joint training's examples bear no clear relation to it; on Gaussians, BR places one example at each mode, whereas the random policy lands near the same mode about half the time; on boolean concepts, **87% of BR examples share only the properties the concept requires, against 36% for random and 0% for joint**; on the ImageNet hierarchies, **BR follows the lowest-common-ancestor strategy 100% of the time and the jointly-trained teacher never exceeds 20%**. The human results are much weaker than the strategy-similarity results and go in the same direction. On the bimodal task, **60 subjects, 30 per arm, ten concepts: 18% mean accuracy after teacher examples against 8% after random ones**, with a far wider spread in the teacher arm (standard deviation 19% against 6%, maximum 70% against 20%) and a significant group difference. On boolean concepts, **80 subjects, 40 per arm, ten concepts, four test images each: 76% mean against 71%**. The authors' own reading is that neither human count is high — on the Gaussian task a subject has to get the whole distribution right to be counted correct — and they attribute part of the gap to subjects holding a unimodal prior, or over-weighting colour on the boolean task, i.e. the human student's prior not matching the concept space.

## Limits and open problems they name
The human student is much worse than the network student, and the authors' diagnosis is a prior mismatch rather than anything about the examples: on the Gaussian task the humans seem to believe the concept is unimodal, and on the boolean task they over-emphasise colour. Whether that is the whole story is left open, and so is the question of which human limitations are fundamental to the emergence of legible protocols — they investigated exactly one (the inability to jointly optimise) out of many. The domains are all small and the intuitive strategy in each is known in advance, which is what makes "similarity to the intuitive strategy" computable at all; teaching by demonstration or teaching anything more complex than a two-example policy is named as future work. The paper also does not claim that a legible teaching policy implies a legible student — legibility is a property of the examples, and the student network's own representation is never inspected. One measurement artefact of this run: the significance statistic in both human results arrived as a blank in the HTML (rendered math lost by the tag strip), so this entry reports the direction and the authors' word "highly significant" and **asserts no test statistic or p-value**.

## What we take from it
A **method** and a **measure**, plus a warning about what each of them covers. Method: **optimisation order is itself an interpretability intervention** — the legibility of the emitted artefact came from decoupling the party that produces it from the party that consumes it, not from any constraint, penalty or dictionary. That is a cheaper lever than regularising a joint objective, and it is testable in one comparison. Measure: **"similarity to a hand-written intuitive strategy" is a legible account's cheapest score** — it needs no human subjects, only a per-domain rule a person would recognise, and it is the only column in this paper that separates three policies cleanly (87/36/0 and 100/20). The human arm is the priced version of the same question and shows how much of the effect survives contact with a reader: the ordering holds but the magnitudes collapse to 10 and 5 points, which is the honest size of a "the reader learned better" claim at this scale. Warning: **the paper measures legibility of the artefact and never of the model**, so it is exactly the entry that licenses "a readable account need not be a faithful one" against the folder's own optimism — a teacher whose examples match a human rule can still be computing something else, and the only arm here that would catch that is the joint one, which is illegible but equally accurate. For us: when we publish a decoder whose output "reads like" a known strategy, this is the control we are missing — a matched arm optimised only for downstream usefulness, scored on the same hand-written rule. Coreference `arxiv-1810.08575` (the same two-party shape from the supervision side, where the scaffold is discarded rather than read) and `arxiv-2210.10860` (a fluent human-facing artefact worth nothing to the reader — this paper's human arm is the positive case at a much smaller magnitude).
