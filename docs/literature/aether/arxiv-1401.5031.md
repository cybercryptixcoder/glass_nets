## What they wanted, and why

A large class of causal-discovery algorithms works by asking a data set, over and over, whether two variables are independent once a set of others is held fixed — so the cost and the accuracy of that one question sets the ceiling on the whole search. The author's complaint is that the accurate answer to it for nonlinear, non-Gaussian data (the kernel conditional-independence test, KCI) is cubic in sample size, and search makes thousands to tens of thousands of these calls, so the good test is unusable at realistic scale. The aim is a test of the same competence but quadratic, cheap enough that the whole pipeline runs on a workstation in seconds. The larger goal is scaling nonlinear causal discovery to hundreds of variables and thousands of samples, which is exactly the regime our own probe batteries sit in.

## What they studied

No model, no network. The unit of analysis is a *decision*: a single yes/no verdict about whether `X` and `Y` are independent given a set `Z`, drawn from an i.i.d. sample of a continuous data set. Four such decisions are compared head to head — partial correlation (Fisher Z, linear Gaussian), a rank-based partial correlation over the Gaussian copula (Harris & Drton), KCI (kernel), and the proposed CCI — first in isolation and then plugged into the PC / PC-Stable adjacency search, where the compared object becomes the estimated graph. The proposed test is named for the classical notion of conditional correlation.

## How they did it

The construction replaces the kernel Hilbert space with four elementary ideas. (1) Two variables are independent exactly when the covariance of every square-summable transform of one with every transform of the other vanishes — Daudin's association measure, cited from a 1980 Biometrika paper, expresses independence as a sum of squares of such covariances over an orthonormal basis. (2) A complete orthonormal basis in the covariance inner product is all that is needed, and the author takes truncated *powers* of the variable — `x, x², x³, … x⁷` by default, on the argument that up to exact algebraic cancellation the power basis detects what a Hermite basis would. (3) Zero correlation is tested without normality by Hawkins' U-statistic version of the Fisher Z transform, which refers the transformed correlation to a Normal with a variance computed from standardised fourth moments, so finite fourth moments suffice. (4) Conditional independence becomes unconditional independence of nonparametric residuals: regress `X` on `Z` and `Y` on `Z` by a kernel smoother (a uniform kernel, bandwidth from a scaled median-absolute-deviation rule that shrinks with sample size, combined across dimensions by a maximum times the square root of the conditioning-set size), then test the two residual streams. The main routine computes a correlation and a transformed p-value for every pair of basis functions drawn from the list, then applies a Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery-rate step over that whole list of p-values and returns "dependent" if any survives the cutoff. Correctness is argued in the large-sample, small-alpha limit: residuals converge to the true conditional means, the transform converges to truth per Hawkins, and the truncated basis grows to the full one — so any dependence must show up in some pair of transforms, up to the faithfulness-style condition that the aggregate zero is not accidental cancellation. Comparisons run four tests through the same search implementation on a linear Gaussian benchmark and then on fourteen 5-node nonlinear structural-equation models with coefficients and uniform disturbances drawn from a symmetric interval, scoring adjacency precision and recall against the generating model's pattern; a 200-node, 200-edge, 2,000-sample sparse graph then separates the tests on scale.

## What counts as an explanation here

An instrument, not an explanation. What the paper offers is a test procedure with a proof sketch of consistency and a cost class — the claim it licenses is "no transform of the residual of one variable is correlated with any transform of the residual of the other, over this finite basis" — and it labels that claim as *approximate* by construction, since the basis is finite (the author says so plainly, contrasting with kernel tests that are correct in the limit without a truncation). Its predictive content is one bit per query, consumed by a graph search; nothing about the shape or strength of a dependence is produced, and nothing is estimated beyond residuals and correlations.

## How they judged success

Per-decision: adjacency precision (correct adjacencies over correct plus spurious) and recall (correct over correct plus missed) of the estimated pattern against the pattern implied by the generating model, computed via PC on 100 random data sets per sample size in the Gaussian case and on 1,000-sample data sets over fourteen connection-function types in the nonlinear case, at a significance level of 0.01 with CCI's FDR step applied internally. Cost: wall-clock seconds for 1,000 conditional-independence tests with one conditioning variable, on a single core of a 2 GHz laptop, across four implementations in three languages. Scale: the same precision/recall pair plus elapsed time on the 200-node sparse graph on an 8-processor machine, with KCI cross-checked through a different PC implementation and the expensive first step of the rank test parallelised. Baselines are the three named competitors at their authors' settings; there is no ablation of the basis length, the kernel, or the FDR step, only prose comments on each.

## What they found

On linear Gaussian data all four tests are almost indistinguishable: precision is high for every test at every sample size from 100 to 700, recall rises with sample size, and the parametric test is in fact the best of the four, which the author flags as discrepant with the original KCI paper and attributes to a difference in simulation style. The decisive numbers are cost — 1,000 tests at 700 samples taking about a tenth of a second for the two correlation tests, 17.5 seconds for the new test, and 1,478.2 seconds for the kernel test. On the nonlinear, non-Gaussian models, the two nonlinear tests dominate the two correlation tests on precision wherever the connection function is not close to linear (the problematic types include quadratic, reciprocal, absolute value, logarithmic, and the multiplicative-noise form), the correlation-based tests have recall under 0.4 on five to six of the types while the new test does so on only one, and the kernel test wins on recall for the reciprocal function where the new test's precisions are comparable. At scale the gap becomes categorical: on the 200-node graph, precision 0.96 and recall 0.75 in 54 seconds for the new test, against 0.84 and 0.48 in 14.5 hours for the kernel test, with the two correlation tests either fast and poor (0.21/0.18 in 2 seconds) or precise and nearly blind (0.90/0.10 in 43 seconds).

## Limits and open problems they name

The residual step is quadratic in sample size, which the author says limits use above roughly ten thousand samples at high dimension — the escape is parallelism, and PC-Stable is cited precisely because it makes same-depth decisions independent and so parallelisable. The finite basis makes the test approximate where kernel tests are asymptotically exact, and choosing the basis is left as judgement (the author notes `{x, x²}` is enough for most of the benchmark's connection types). Kernel width is conceded to matter more than kernel shape, with only a heuristic rule given. Orientation of edges is explicitly out of scope: precisions for nonlinear models are described as useless in most cases, plausibly because contracting connection functions with small coefficients make transitive influence vanish and produce spurious collider orientations, so a separate treatment is deferred. The multiplicative-noise model is named as one where precision is poor for every test.

## What we take from it

Four things. (1) **The right size for an independence claim about activations is a decision, priced in milliseconds per query, not a statistic priced in hours** — because any search over conditioning sets multiplies the per-query cost by the size of the search, the comparison that matters is cost-at-fixed-accuracy, and this entry gives the template: report precision, recall and elapsed time for the identical search, so the accuracy is never quoted without the bill. (2) **Powers of the variable are a legitimate stand-in for a complete basis up to cancellation**, which is a cheap trick we can reuse: test correlations of `r`, `r²`, `r³` between residual streams instead of reaching for a kernel Gram matrix, since the dependence a network creates on a linear projection of its state is usually either a trend (odd power) or a symmetric spread change (even power, and the second power is the cheapest nonlinearity available). (3) **The FDR step is the honest way to consume a family of one-sided tests**: the test does not pick the largest correlation and compare it to a fixed threshold, it collects all the p-values and controls the expected proportion of false dependences, which is the correct currency when dozens of probes are asked the same question about one pair of streams. (4) **A warning that costs us nothing and saves a wrong inference**: a residual-based conditional-independence test is only as good as its regression, and the paper's own failure cases are exactly the multiplicative ones — the same multiplicative-noise counterexample that later appears in the hardness literature — so a "conditionally independent given Z" verdict on activations whose coupling is multiplicative is the first place to distrust our own screens. Coreference `arxiv-1804.07203` (the impossibility result that makes "usable test" the only available goal, and whose counterexample family this paper already reports as a failure mode) and `arxiv-1711.06642` (a test whose power is stated uniformly over smoothness classes rather than demonstrated on a benchmark — the complementary kind of evidence) and `arxiv-1910.12327`/`arxiv-1909.10140` (rank-based dependence *strengths*, which report a number instead of a bit and so do not need a search to be useful).
