# The Sharp Directions Are Against You: Curvature Analysis of Activation Steering

- **Key:** arxiv-2610.16084
- **Authors / organisation:** Seyedarmin Azizi, Arya Fayyazi, Parsa Razmara, Erfan Baghaei Potraghloo, Souvik Kundu, Massoud Pedram; University of Southern California and Intel AI
- **Date:** 2026-10-12
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.16084
- **Kind:** paper

- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

Activation steering — adding a vector to a hidden state at inference — is the cheapest way to change a model's behaviour, and the standard recipe for building the vector is to average the difference in activations between positive and negative exemplars. That recipe is known to be flaky: on a large minority of inputs it moves behaviour the wrong way, it works only in a narrow band of scaling coefficients, and on some tasks the averaged vector has the wrong sign altogether. The authors want to stop treating these as separate empirical nuisances and give one geometric cause plus one cheap fix. Their claim is that activation space is not uniformly sensitive to being poked: a small set of directions amplifies a poke into a large, input-dependent logit swing, and the mean-difference recipe has no way to avoid them. The larger goal is a criterion for *where it is safe to intervene*, rather than yet another method for choosing what to intervene with.

## What they studied

Three 7-8B instruction-tuned models (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct with hidden width 3584; Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, both 4096) and four behavioural axes: positive sentiment, sycophancy (honest disagreement with a misconception), toxicity, and refusal of harmful requests. The unit of analysis is not a neuron, feature or circuit but a **direction in activation space at one layer, together with the model's second derivative with respect to that direction** — the object being studied is the local curvature of the behavioural loss as a function of the steering vector. Three layers per model (early, mid, late) give 36 layer×task×model conditions at token level, and 25 conditions at generation level.

## How they did it

The intervention is the usual one: at a chosen layer, replace the hidden state with itself plus a scalar times the steering vector, and let the rest of the forward pass run. The new object is the *steering Hessian*: take a differentiable behavioural loss — the negative log-probability, at the first generated position, of a task-specific token (e.g. the token starting "I" for a refusal, "Actually" for a correction) — average it over a set of prompts, and differentiate it twice with respect to the components of the steering vector itself, not the weights. Because the model is frozen, the loss is a smooth function of a d-dimensional vector, and the Hessian is a d×d symmetric matrix whose eigenvectors span "sharp" (large second derivative: a small nudge here swings the logits wildly) and "flat" (small second derivative) subspaces. The full matrix is infeasible at these widths, so they estimate the top and bottom eigenpairs by Lanczos iteration with Hessian-vector products approximated by central finite differences (40 iterations, 12 estimation prompts, step 0.01, about 40 seconds per layer), and take the sharp subspace as the top 15 eigenvectors by absolute eigenvalue. Three derived vectors are then compared at every condition: the sharp projection of the standard contrastive vector, its flat projection, its full original form, and a *cleaned* vector obtained by simply subtracting the sharp component — a one-line post-processing step with no retraining and no extra inference cost. A random-subspace projection serves as a dimensionality control, and generic PCA of 2,000 WikiText-2 forward passes serves as the variance-versus-curvature control. Token-level evaluation sweeps ten steering strengths and measures the probability margin between the target and anti-target token; generation-level evaluation uses 50 greedy completions per condition scored by task-specific classifiers, plus two external checks: an over-refusal test on benign prompts that look unsafe, and a jailbreak test where the same vectors are applied with the sign flipped.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **curvature decomposition of activation space at one layer** — not a description of a concept, and explicitly not a claim that concepts are curved. The authors are careful that the object they explain is a property of the computation graph rather than of the behaviour: their first proposition writes the Hessian as a Gauss-Newton term (the downstream Jacobian sandwiched around the logit-space loss curvature) plus a downstream-curvature term, and argues that the top eigenvectors are directions the *downstream layers* amplify most, so the sharp/flat split is inherited from the model's own Jacobian and should be roughly the same across behavioural objectives. The explanation is partial by construction: it is a local quadratic model around one point (the standard vector at strength 1.0), it says nothing about which concept to steer, and its actionable output is a subspace to avoid plus a diagnostic scalar. It nevertheless predicts things in advance — the sharp projection should reverse behaviour, the flat projection should be robust and should recover the correct sign where the standard vector has the wrong one, and the token-level cost of removal should be small while the generation-level benefit is large.

## How they judged success

Five claims, each with its own measurement. (1) Anisotropy: the condition number of the steering Hessian per condition, claimed to exceed 50 everywhere. (2) Sharp directions are adversarial: the anti-steer rate — fraction of held-out inputs on which the probability margin moves in the wrong direction — of the *sharp* projection, averaged over the strength sweep, required to exceed 0.5 in all 36 conditions and bolded at 0.95. (3) Flat projection as diagnostic and layer selector: lowest anti-steer rate against the random-subspace control, and sign recovery on the conditions where the standard vector is inverted. (4) Cleaned vector in generation: beat the standard vector in every one of the 25 generation conditions, with per-position KL between steered and unsteered next-token distributions as the mechanistic readout. (5) Sharp directions are noise rather than signal: apply the negative of each vector to try to break alignment, and show that the sharp-only vector is the *worst* jailbreak while the cleaned vector is the best. Supporting controls: how much of the vector's squared norm the sharp component carries ("sharp energy fraction"), principal angles against a generic PCA subspace, a sweep over sharp-subspace dimension, a sample-size re-run at four times the evaluation prompts, a judge-validation of the keyword refusal scorer, and a norm-preservation argument bounding what removal can cost.

## What they found

The Hessian spectrum is anisotropic in every one of the 36 conditions, with condition numbers from 50 to 3,727. Steering along the top eigenvectors reverses the intended behaviour in all 36 conditions, at anti-steer rates of 0.53 to 1.00, and on refusal the reversal is total on Llama at every layer, with the largest wrong-direction margin shift reaching 0.48. The sharp component is tiny: it carries 1-5% of the standard vector's squared norm, so removing it changes the vector's direction only slightly (cosine above 0.974) and cuts its norm by under 3%, while reducing its directional curvature by a factor between 1.7 and 12.3 (median 4.0). That tiny component nonetheless accounts for nearly all of the distributional gap the standard vector opens during autoregressive generation — on Llama the sharp projection's KL trajectory is almost identical to the full vector's, around 19 nats — and sharp removal cuts that gap three- to fourfold. The cleaned vector beats the standard one in all 25 generation conditions. The refusal results are the striking ones: on Qwen and Llama the standard vector *degrades* safety (Qwen from 78% to 36-44%, Llama from 62% to 24-52%) and the cleaned vector restores and mostly exceeds the baseline (88-98% on Qwen, 74-76% on Llama). On the flat-projection side, the flat vector has the lowest anti-steer rate in 11 of 12 model-task pairs (a 0% rate on Qwen sentiment at one layer across all 1,000 evaluations), while random directions give 60-86% anti-steer. The flat projection also diagnoses inversion — on sycophancy all three models, on toxicity Llama, on refusal Qwen and Llama the standard vector has the wrong sign, and the flat projection recovers it in every case, e.g. Mistral sycophancy from −0.028 to +0.317 and Llama toxicity from −0.010 to +0.390. Flipping the sign of the whole vector is not a substitute: the cleaned vector beats the negated standard vector in 8 of 10 sycophancy and all 4 inverted refusal conditions, by 23-38 points. The jailbreak test is the strongest evidence for the "noise" reading: the negated sharp vector is the least effective attack (Llama never complies, 100% refusal, safer than baseline; Qwen yields 2% full compliance with 40% incoherent partials that dissolve into repetition loops), while the negated cleaned vector is the most effective. The curvature subspace is not a variance subspace: mean principal-angle cosine between the top-15 Hessian and top-15 PCA subspaces is 0.077, about what two random subspaces give, and projecting the vector away from generic PCA *hurts* the behaviour margin (−0.021) where sharp removal preserves it (+0.006).

## Limits and open problems they name

The whole account is a local quadratic approximation around the standard vector at a single strength; higher-order terms could dominate at large strengths, and the formal bounds are stated only at strength 1.0 while the generation gains at higher strengths are demonstrated empirically and not bounded. Only four behaviours and three 7-8B instruction-tuned models were tested — nothing larger, no base models, and nothing black-box. The Lanczos estimate assumes a fixed prompt distribution, so whether the subspaces survive a distribution shift is left open. Their limitations section does not mention three things I would count as limits from the experiments themselves: the target token is a hand-picked single token and the paper checks only one alternative multi-token objective on one model and layer (where the sharp subspace agrees at cosine 0.61, not more); the cleaned vector is *not* safe in the other polarity, since steering it negatively turns out to be the most effective jailbreak in the paper, which they frame as a controllability result rather than as the safety cost it also is; and the sharp energy fraction is computed with respect to one estimator of the Hessian at one layer, so the scalar they propose as a universal audit metric inherits that estimator's noise.

## What we take from it

A **measure and a framing**, both directly usable. The measure: second-order sensitivity of a behavioural loss with respect to an *intervention vector* — the Hessian in the intervention's own coordinates, not in weight space and not in activation-variance space — and the sharp energy fraction of a candidate direction with respect to it. This is a way to score a proposed white-box edit by how much the model's output will swing when the edit is slightly wrong, which is exactly what our predictive model needs if it is going to say whether a predicted effect is robust or fragile. The framing: the direction that best *separates two populations of activations* and the direction that is *safe to add to an activation* are different vectors, and the paper has an ablation that makes the difference quantitative — projecting away high-variance directions actively damages the behaviour while projecting away high-curvature directions improves it. For us that is a warning with teeth: any unit our model proposes because it scores well as a classifier direction must be reported beside its curvature, and the curvature reported must be in the coordinate system of the edit rather than of the readout. A method we can lift cheaply: the cleaned vector is built from quantities we could compute from the same forward passes we already run — a Jacobian-vector product and finite differences at one layer, tens of seconds, one direction, no training — so a "robustness" column can be added to any intervention table without a new experiment pipeline. The sharp-versus-flat pair is also a ready-made *diagnostic* for the folder's recurring question of why a direction that decodes perfectly fails to steer: inversion is diagnosed by asking which subspace the vector's sign lives in.
