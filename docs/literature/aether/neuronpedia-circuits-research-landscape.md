## What they wanted, and why
Five organisations that had all been building attribution-graph tooling sat down to report what replicated, what did not, and what a replacement-model description of a network can and cannot carry. The framing is deliberately biological: like an organism, a network was never designed, so understanding it means building models at several levels of abstraction — some to predict behaviour, some to design interventions — rather than deriving one true schematic. Their motivating complaints are that the field has spent its effort building microscopes and not enough using them, that single-prompt graphs are execution traces rather than algorithms, and that the replacement models underneath the graphs may silently destroy the structure they are meant to reveal. This is the central coordination document for the method our own white-box model would most likely be built on, so its list of failures is effectively a specification of what we must measure.

## What they studied
Attribution graphs built from transcoders: cross-layer transcoders (CLTs) that write to every later layer, per-layer transcoders (PLTs), and skip variants with an unpenalised linear bypass. Models: GPT-2 small and Llama-3.2/3.1 1B for the architecture sweeps, Gemma-2-2B and Qwen3-4B as the replication substrates, with the original Claude 3.5 Haiku case studies as the reference point. The unit of analysis is a feature in a replacement model plus the edges between features on one prompt; transcoders are trained for 100M tokens on roughly parameter-matched configurations (2^17 width for transcoders, 2^15 for CLTs, three times 2^15 for incremental CLTs).

## How they did it
Three strands. (1) Architecture sweep: train each variant across activation functions (JumpReLU, ReLU, TopK, BatchTopK, global-TopK) and skip/no-skip, then score each trained transcoder by *replacement score* — the fraction of a pruned attribution graph's influence that comes from input nodes rather than error nodes — as a function of mean per-token L0, on 260 curated prompts with an EOS token prepended to blunt GPT-2's large first-token norm. (2) Sparsity ablation: hold architecture fixed, vary the training-time sparsity penalty, independently vary the graph-pruning threshold after training, and read the replacement score on the resulting grid. (3) Method commentary: replications of the original Haiku findings on open models, a KL-finetuning arm that adds a divergence loss between the patched-in replacement model and the original, and engine-level benchmarks (sparse matmul kernels, distributed sharding, activation caching).

## What counts as an explanation here
A per-prompt causal graph: features as nodes, directed edges with estimated influence, error nodes standing for unexplained computation, and a pruned subgraph read as the mechanism behind one output. Every contributor is explicit that this is partial and local — one prompt, one execution trace, not the algorithm — and the report's own framing is that useful understanding will be a patchwork of such descriptions plus interventions plus behavioural observation, not a single complete account. It does predict, in a limited sense: the replacement model is a surrogate whose divergence from the original can be measured, and graphs are offered as hypotheses to be validated by patching rather than as findings.

## How they judged success
Replacement score against L0 sparsity (the headline instrument throughout), normalised reconstruction MSE against replacement score (to see whether local fidelity implies global fidelity), KL divergence between the fully patched replacement model and the original, fraction of unexplained variance, counts of dead features, and engineering throughput (FLOPs utilisation, wall-clock sparse-matmul benchmarks). Baselines are the other architecture variants at matched parameter count and matched sparsity; the notable absence is any held-out or cross-prompt fidelity measure for the circuits themselves.

## What they found
CLTs beat PLTs at matched parameters across the sparsity regimes of interest (hundreds of active features per token or fewer); JumpReLU is best for CLTs but needs the most tuning, and TopK/BatchTopK are close behind and far easier to set. Skip PLTs lower reconstruction error for a given L0 and yet *score worse* as replacement models — lower replacement score at a given MSE — which the authors read as skip connections propagating earlier layers' errors downstream. Incremental (weight-tied) CLTs match parameter-matched PLTs and fall short of CLTs. The sparsity grid yields the report's most actionable number: to hit a target post-pruning graph sparsity, train at an L0 that overshoots by roughly five to ten times and let pruning remove the rest; using already-sparse training or over-pruning are both worse. KL finetuning cut divergence from 0.33 to 0.20 and lifted the unexplained-variance fraction from 0.226 to 0.23, yet replacement score *fell* from 0.723 to 0.705 — a surrogate made better at mimicry and worse at attribution, which killed that line of work for them. The replications recovered known circuits (two-hop geography in Gemma-2-2B, language-agnostic then language-specific features, clause-boundary features in Llama-3.2-1B) but the Goodfire greater-than graph differed from the published one in feature selectivity and included an apparently inappropriate parity detector. Two documented limitations matter more than the wins: transcoders shatter geometric structure, so a helix-and-trigonometry account of number representation shows up as lookup-table features instead; and graphs are per-prompt execution traces, so many-prompt merging or geometry-preserving replacement models are named as future work. On the engineering side, torch.sparse in CSR format and the Triton embedding-bag kernels land within noise of each other for forward passes (0.87s vs 0.84s on the stated shape), and distributed TopK training reached 47.8% FLOPs utilisation, 53.8% with one published kernel family and 65.3% with a different activation.

## Limits and open problems they name
Transcoders preserve information but not geometry, so global representation structure is not recoverable from them; graphs describe one prompt and not the general algorithm; error nodes are often too prevalent to trace through; the sparsity results are two small models and should not be assumed at scale; KL finetuning was tried with one weighting and abandoned early; and the entire section of future work — automating hypothesis generation and validation, merging graphs, targeting probe readouts rather than logits, building downstream benchmarks that measure understanding rather than accuracy — is stated as unsolved. They also concede that whether narrow feature selectivity is a real mechanism or a transcoder artefact has not been decided.

## What we take from it
A method, three measures, and one warning. The method is the replacement-score instrument: train a surrogate, plug it in fully, and score how much of the pruned graph's influence survives from inputs rather than error nodes — that is a fidelity-with-attribution measure, and it is the only one in the folder that is computed *after* the description is simplified. The measures are the L0-to-replacement-score frontier (any architecture claim needs its curve, not a point), the reconstruction-versus-replacement table (local fidelity does not imply global fidelity, and the skip-transcoder result is the proof), and the surrogate-divergence column (KL down, replacement score down — divergence alone is not a proxy for explanatory quality). The warning is the one to design against: any decomposition trained to preserve reconstruction will happily destroy the geometry that carries the computation, so our model must report a structure-preservation test distinct from a fidelity number, and any claim of the form "the model computes X by these features" must be checked against a many-prompt aggregate rather than a single trace.
