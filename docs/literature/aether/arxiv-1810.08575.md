## What they wanted, and why
Most useful tasks have no algorithmic training signal (no win condition, no labels), and for the hardest ones a human cannot supply one either — a single person cannot demonstrate or judge behaviour in an observation space that is beyond human scale. The practical consequence is that engineers fall back on a cheap proxy correlated with the real goal, and aggressively optimising a proxy produces the pathologies the paper lists under Goodhart's law: a satisfaction metric that the agent learns to fake rather than improve. The authors want a way to construct a training signal for tasks where neither an external reward nor a human judgement exists, without first having to model human preferences exactly — which is what they argue inverse reinforcement learning would require and cannot identify. Their larger goal is to widen the set of tasks on which gradient-based learning can be applied at all, and to do it by making the specification implicit in how a human decomposes work rather than explicit in a reward function. The paper is a first step: the framework is stated generally and tested on small algorithmic domains, with the realistic case left to future work.

## What they studied
No language model. The agent is a small Transformer encoder–decoder (6 encoder layers, 3 question-attending layers, 8 heads, model width 512, a pointer/copy decoder, batch norm added after each MLP, no positional encoding because contexts are sets of facts)., trained on five synthetic combinatorial tasks with contexts of 8 to 128 short fact sentences: permutation powering (compute σ^k(x) for k up to 63), sequential variable assignment (evaluate a variable defined by a chain of function applications), union-find style component labelling, shortest path in a directed graph, and wildcard-restricted summation over a 6-bit function. A second model, the "human predictor" H′, is a Transformer decoder that imitates the human's choices in a transcript. The unit of analysis is a whole training process — the moving target Amplify^H(X) and the gap between it and X — not any internal feature of the network.

## How they did it
The human expert H is given several copies of the current agent X and uses them to answer a question: H proposes a subquestion, X answers it, H repeats this a fixed number k of times, and then H answers the top-level question having seen the subanswers. X is trained by supervised learning to predict that composite's output. Because consulting a human at every step is expensive, the composite is computed against a learned imitator H′ of the human's subquestion and aggregation behaviour, and H′ is retrained continuously because the distribution of subanswers shifts as X improves (its inputs stop being noise). Training runs four loops in parallel: collect transcripts against H, fit H′, generate (Q, A) pairs against H′, train X on them. A Polyak average of X over the last 1000 batches (a target network, in effect) is used inside the composite rather than the live weights, which the authors say stabilised training without materially slowing it. Two engineering choices carry the method: a curriculum that raises context size from 8 to 64 whenever X reaches 85% accuracy on its own dataset, and a split of X into a context-encoding phase and a question-answering phase so the encoding is reused across many questions per context and across all subquestions of one composite call — reported as roughly a tenfold training speedup. In these experiments the human is replaced by a hand-coded decomposition algorithm, and primitive questions (what is the definition of x; is there an edge from x to y) are answered with ground truth rather than by X, so the oracle's calls are the expensive resource being minimised.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not an account of a network. The object that carries the understanding is the *decomposition* — a tree of subquestions that a human (or here, a hard-coded algorithm) can pose and then recombine — and the claim is that the global objective is *implicit* in that recombination, never written down as a reward. Two points matter for our purposes. First, the decomposition is explicitly a discardable artefact of training: the authors say the hierarchical structure is thrown away, and the trained agent's own procedure will generally not resemble the tree it was trained on, and may learn holistic heuristics instead. Second, the composite is meant to be only *modestly* better than the agent alone at each point in training, and that margin is the signal; efficiency of the collaboration is not required, only that it helps at all.

## How they judged success
Comparison against ordinary supervised learning from ground-truth labels on the same five tasks: accuracy on a held-out test set (a third of examples at the maximum size, a third at the smallest, a third spread uniformly between), plus the number of training steps and the amount of computation per question. The reference point is deliberately permissive — the authors state they do not expect amplification to match supervised learning, only to be "at worst a modest slowdown", because the supervised arm has ground truth the amplification arm by construction does not. Training dynamics are broken out by recursion depth (a separate accuracy curve per depth, for both X and Amplify^H′(X)) and the sample cost is reported as the count of calls to the decomposition oracle per task. There is no faithfulness metric, no human study, and no comparison against debate or against a proxy-reward baseline.

## What they found
The five tasks were learned with at worst a modest increase in training steps, and at roughly twice the computation per question, because the targets have to be generated by actually running the composite (which calls X and H′ between three and ten times per question). The sample-efficiency result is the paper's sharpest number in kind rather than degree: supervised learning needed tens of millions of examples to learn these algorithms, while amplification needed "tens of thousands" of oracle calls, with per-task counts of **7,000 (permutation powering), 6,000 (sequential assignments), 20,000 (union find), 24,000 (shortest path) and 10,000 (wildcard search)**. X always trails the composite it imitates, as expected for an imitation target the model is chasing; and the composite at depth d+1 scores slightly below X at depth d, since answering one depth-(d+1) question correctly requires several correct depth-d answers. Training accuracy sawtooths at each curriculum increment. Agreement with the composite's chosen decomposition is not reported as a number anywhere.

## Limits and open problems they name
The authors are unusually explicit that this establishes almost nothing about the realistic case. In the experiments the questions admit algorithmic decomposition and the human is replaced by a hand-coded algorithm, so nothing is learned about whether people can decompose real tasks or whether messy real decompositions are learnable. The domains are ones where an algorithmic signal exists, which is precisely the case the method was not proposed for. Training is supervised imitation of the composite rather than RL against a reward the composite defines, which they suspect is what real applications would need. The question distribution must cover not only the questions of interest but every subquestion that arises inside a composite call, and the designer is responsible for constructing it. And the central assumption — that a human can coordinate several copies into a team better than one copy alone — is argued by analogy to human organisations being easier to coordinate than AI copies, not measured.

## What we take from it
A framing and a warning, in that order. **Framing:** the decomposition-generating party can be a weaker model than the one being trained, and the training signal is the *margin* between the composite and the agent alone at each step — a moving target whose job is to be slightly better, not correct. That is the same structure as bootstrapped read-out: a small predictor proposes a decomposition of a behaviour, a stronger system checks or completes it, and what transfers is whatever the pairing buys over the agent unaided. **Warning:** the decomposition is discarded, and the agent "will generally not mirror the structure used in training" — so nothing requires the trained model to be legible in the terms of the tree that trained it. Any account we publish from a decomposition-shaped method is therefore an account of the *training scaffold*, not of the learned computation, unless we separately show that intervening on the named parts moves the behaviour. **Measure worth stealing:** the per-depth accuracy split (agent versus composite at each recursion depth) as a cheap diagnostic of whether the gain is coming from depth or from breadth. **Shortcut:** target-network style averaging of the model used inside the composite, which is a stability trick we will need whenever a read-out is generated by the model it is describing.
