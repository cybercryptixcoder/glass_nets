## What they wanted, and why
The author argues that post-hoc interpretability is structurally the wrong target now that language models act rather than chat: a tool that reverses an inference after it has finished can describe a bad output but cannot stop it being emitted, and since such tools live outside the deployed model they are both approximate and discarded at deployment. His stated evidence for the stakes is a reported 56.4% rise in documented AI safety incidents from 2023 to 2024 and the inclusion of interpretability among the priorities of a 2026 DARPA-NSF-CAISI programme on auditable autonomy. The proposal is a paradigm shift: instead of explaining a trained network, require architectures whose forward pass exposes states a human can read and an auditor can edit *while* the computation runs. He names this property **generative interpretability** and proposes neuro-symbolic models as the worked example. The larger goal is audit and attribution of responsibility — an undesired outcome traced to the specific step that bears it, rather than to the system as a whole.

## What they studied
No models are trained and no data is used; the unit of analysis is the *interpretability paradigm* and the *inference path*, not neurons or features. Four paradigms are compared: white-box models in Rudin's sense (readable before inference), post-hoc interpretability split into an **anatomical** branch (attention heads and FFNs studied for stable, input-independent function, e.g. MLP layers as factual stores that can be edited) and a **physiological** branch (the end-to-end information flow of one pass, grounded in causal mediation analysis and represented as a DAG over nodes, with circuits and attribution graphs as the current frontier), concept-bottleneck models and their language-model extension as the single-interior-checkpoint case, and vanilla LLMs with chain-of-thought as the input-output-boundary case. Neuro-symbolic models, categorised by an existing survey's Type-3 (neural and symbolic components in a feedback loop), are the proposed instance; NLProlog is named as a representative, with proof steps as checkpoints and embedding-based similarity scoring as the opaque part inside each step.

## How they did it
The method is definitional, comparative and prescriptive, and it is worth sketching because the shape of the argument is what we could borrow. (1) Define the property: a **checkpoint** must be both natively meaningful (it maps to a human-readable concept without an external tool recovering the meaning) and causally intervenable (the pass functionally depends on it, so a do-intervention has a well-defined counterfactual by construction rather than a contingent one). (2) Place the paradigms on two axes — *temporal*, when readable states exist relative to inference (before / during / after), and *spatial*, where along the path they sit (input-output boundary / single checkpoint / multiple interior checkpoints / throughout) — and read the axes as **partial orders of commitment strength**. (3) Argue the proposed point is both *sufficient* and *minimal* by ruling out weaker and stronger commitments. Weaker: after-inference cannot audit before generation; boundary-only readability, including chain-of-thought, audits tokens that inference already produced and those tokens are not faithful anyway; a single checkpoint audits one cross-section, which the author says has been shown empirically insufficient. The theoretical prop for "multiple" is a cited result that any efficiently computable function is provably compositionally sparse with DAG structure, so a DAG-shaped computation needs more than one cross-section. Stronger: throughout-readability at the level of monosemantic neurons or directions would collapse the near-orthogonal packing that superposition buys, since a d-dimensional space holds exponentially many such directions and forcing each to one meaning reduces capacity from exponential to linear in d. (4) Illustrate with a constructed example: a customer-service refund agent whose pass alternates neural steps with symbolic states (a claim of a refund, a duplicate check, a limit check), where an auditor at the limit checkpoint rewrites the state and the downstream step changes from refund to escalation. (5) State the three scaling obstacles.

## What counts as an explanation here
An explanation is a **trajectory of named, human-readable states at fixed points of the computation** — the schema of each checkpoint pre-defined by a symbolic vocabulary, the values filled by an opaque sub-computation. The claim is deliberately partial: most of the network stays unconstrained and uninterpretable, and the author argues that partiality is what makes it scale. It is not a dictionary, a circuit graph or a probe; it is closer to a causal abstraction or a program with observable intermediates, and the intended product is a *predictable counterfactual* — after editing a checkpoint, the downstream states should follow by rule. The distinguishing claim against post-hoc work is that these explanations are meant to be used at inference time, not after it.

## How they judged success
**No quantitative evaluation, benchmark, metric, ablation or experiment is presented anywhere in the paper.** Success is argued conceptually through: the two-axis partial order and the minimality/sufficiency argument above; one citation to an applied healthcare study reporting a gap between what internal representations contain and what steering interventions could actually fix; a citation that chain-of-thought in the wild is not reliably faithful; a citation for single-checkpoint insufficiency; a complexity-theoretic result on compositional sparsity; and the Johnson-Lindenstrauss bound on near-orthogonal directions. The refund-agent figure is explicitly a designed illustration, not a result. The only concrete commitments for the future are the three challenges, each with a suggested direction rather than a measurement.

## What they found
The deliverable is the taxonomy plus one firm claim, and the taxonomy is the part with content. First, the axes cleanly separate white-box (before, throughout), concept bottlenecks (during, single), post-hoc (after, external), and the proposed neuro-symbolic point (during, multiple), and each cell of the resulting grid states whether audit can occur *before* the undesired content is generated. Second, the argument that the "during / multiple" cell is the weakest position that still permits pre-generation audit, and the strongest that does not pay the superposition capacity cost. Third, the honest concession that this is a **prescription about models we should build**, not a description of models we have: existing neuro-symbolic systems are small-neural, single-domain, and largely fixed workflow templates, and scaling them to general-purpose models is unsolved.

## Limits and open problems they name
Three, stated as challenges. (1) **No universal symbolic system exists and hand-building one fails at LLM scope** — the Cyc project is cited as the cautionary case; the proposed route is to align, merge and extend the many existing domain symbolic systems with data-driven methods, which the author says is being studied in concurrent work. (2) **Neural-symbolic integration degenerates into task-specific rule-based templates**, and no general workflow pattern is characterised; the compromise offered is to abstract a shared template per task category. (3) **The neural/symbolic boundary is unprincipled** — some knowledge is graded similarity ("family resemblance", which interpolation handles) and some is exact rule (hashing, formal syntax, where approximate is simply wrong), and where to cut is not specified. Beyond these the paper concedes the cost side openly: semantic throughout-readability would destroy capacity, so any such architecture is interpretable only in patches.

## What we take from it
A **framing** and a **design criterion**, not a method. The framing worth carrying: interpretability claims differ in *when* and *where* they are available, and an instrument that only produces readable states after a forward pass has finished cannot, even in principle, support a claim about what the model would have done had a state been different — which is exactly the claim our prediction-under-intervention goal needs. The criterion worth carrying is the checkpoint test itself, and it is sharper than anything in the probing or dictionary stack: for each read-out site we name, ask (a) is the meaning *native*, i.e. does it come from a pre-declared vocabulary rather than from a tool fitted afterwards, and (b) does the computation *functionally depend* on it, i.e. does altering it propagate with a counterfactual we can predict before running the experiment. Our instruments currently satisfy (b) on a good day and (a) almost never, since every direction, probe and dictionary label in this folder is assigned post-hoc; saying so explicitly is the honest column. The **warning** is the other half and it cuts against the paper's own enthusiasm: the author's own minimality argument concedes that a readable-everywhere network loses the near-orthogonal capacity that makes models work, so a white-box model of a *trained* network should be expected to be readable in patches and should not be judged a failure for that — which is the same conclusion `arxiv-2410.16443` reached from the empirical side with its layer-variance column. A **shortcut** worth noting too: the partial-order-by-commitment device is a cheap way to position our contribution against everything else without benchmarking against any of it, and if we use that device in our own paper we should say plainly that it is an argument and not an evaluation, because this paper does exactly that.
