# Feature emergence via margin maximization: case studies in algebraic tasks

- **Key:** arxiv-2311.07568
- **Authors / organisation:** Depen Morwani, Benjamin L. Edelman, Costin-Andrei Oncescu, Rosie Zhao, Sham Kakade — Harvard University (all five; Morwani, Edelman and Oncescu carry an equal-contribution footnote)
- **Date:** 2023-11-13 (v1); last revised 2024-02-19 (v2)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.07568
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (v2 HTML, including Appendices A–I)
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Mechanistic interpretability had succeeded at describing *how* particular networks compute particular functions — the Fourier algorithm for modular addition, the group-representation algorithm for group composition — but not at saying *why* training lands on those algorithms rather than on any other solution that fits the same data. The authors take the generalisation-theory literature's answer, margin maximisation, and ask whether that single inductive-bias principle is strong enough to specify not just the decision boundary but the internal features. Their larger goal is the one our programme needs: a theory that *predicts* what a trained network will contain, from the task's symmetry and the loss alone, rather than one that reverse-engineers a finished network by hand. The claim is deliberately narrow — stylised one-hidden-layer networks on three algebraic tasks — and the bet is that narrowness buys exactness.

## What they studied
One-hidden-layer networks with homogeneous polynomial activations: quadratic activations on modular addition and on finite-group composition for cyclic and symmetric groups (S3, S4, S5), and x^k activations on (n, k) sparse parity. No biases; one-hot inputs for the group tasks, ±1 for parity. The unit of analysis is the individual neuron, represented as the triple of its input-side and output-side weight vectors, and the object of interest is that vector's support in a structured basis — Fourier modes for the cyclic group, irreducible-representation basis vectors for a general group, input coordinates for parity. Every result is about the *set* of maximum-margin networks, not about one trained run, so the unit is really the solution set of an optimisation problem.

## How they did it
Step one is a transfer of a known theorem: for a weakly-regularised cross-entropy loss on a homogeneous network, the normalised margin of a global optimum tends to the maximum achievable margin as the regulariser vanishes, so the maximum-margin solution set is a defensible proxy for what a barely-regularised run reaches. Step two is the technical contribution: a certificate method. They exhibit a *pair* — a network and a distribution over the training points — that is simultaneously the best response to each other, and the max-min inequality then certifies that the network is a global maximum-margin solution; more usefully, one such pair characterises *every* maximum-margin network, because all of them must be supported on the neurons that maximise the expected margin under that distribution and must be tight on its support. Step three handles the multi-class case, where the max over wrong labels prevents the expected margin from decomposing per neuron, by replacing the max with a fixed weighting over wrong labels, maximising that easier class-weighted margin instead, and then checking a condition that the weighting only ever puts mass on labels that are already the argmax — so the relaxed and true margins coincide. In each case study they guess the uniform data distribution (the tasks are symmetric), find the neuron shapes that maximise the expected class-weighted margin, scale them so the network is tight everywhere, and read off the resulting structure. Step four is an empirical check: train the stylised networks under the matching L(2,ν) regulariser (ν the homogeneity constant) and verify that the trained weights approach the predicted maximum margin and exhibit the predicted support, plotting Fourier power per neuron, representation-alignment power per neuron, and the training curve of the normalised margin.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **theorem about a solution set** — the strongest form of account in this folder. For modular addition: any maximum-margin network of sufficient width has every neuron supported on a single frequency, with all three weight vectors (both inputs, output) being sinusoids of that frequency at phases that satisfy a sum relation, and at least one neuron per non-zero frequency. For sparse parity: every active neuron's input weight is supported exactly on the relevant bit set, with all relevant coordinates equal in magnitude, and the output weight aligned with the class direction under a sign condition. For group composition: every neuron is spanned by a single non-trivial irreducible representation, and each representation appears. Each is a complete characterisation within its assumptions, and each comes with the exact maximum-margin value. It is not just a description: it predicts the geometry of every weight vector in the network, so it is falsifiable by a single trained run. What it does not predict is behaviour beyond the training distribution, or anything about deeper, non-homogeneous, attention-bearing models, which the authors say only in the discussion.

## How they judged success
Three instruments. (1) **Certification**: existence of the pair satisfying both halves of the saddle condition, plus a check that the class-weighting condition holds, which together prove membership in the maximum-margin set by max-min duality — no numerical optimisation is involved, so the proof is exact within its assumptions. (2) **Margin tracking during training**: the normalised L(2,ν) margin of the trained network plotted over training and compared against the predicted closed-form value — for modular addition the paper gives it explicitly in p, for parity in k, for groups in the representation dimensions. (3) **Concentration diagnostics on trained weights**: per-neuron maximum normalised Fourier power for the cyclic case (read once at initialisation, once after training, so the shift from a scattered distribution to a concentrated one is visible), and per-neuron maximum normalised power in the representation basis for the group case; plus the spread of neurons across frequencies or representations, compared against the construction's prediction that the frequencies / representations should be evenly populated. The activations are contrasted throughout: a ReLU network at the same task is reported as nearly but not exactly single-frequency, while the quadratic network is reported as exact.

## What they found
For modular addition with quadratic activations and enough width, margin maximisation forces pure Fourier neurons: each neuron carries one frequency, the three weight vectors are cosines of that frequency with phases satisfying θ_u + θ_v = θ_w, and every non-zero frequency is represented at least once in the network — so the algorithm Nanda et al. reverse-engineered is not a quirk of SGD but the unique shape of the maximum-margin solution. Crucially, the authors also exhibit an alternative construction that fits the data perfectly with no Fourier sparsity at all (pairs of neurons per input pair, built from one-hot vectors), which they use to argue that correct classification is heavily overdetermined and that the sparse solution wins only because its margin is larger. For sparse parity, the same machinery gives: support confined to the relevant bits, all relevant weights equal in magnitude, a sign condition tying the product of those weights to the output direction, and a closed-form margin in k; trained networks show these properties and approach the predicted margin. For group composition the analysis needs representation theory and yields the analogue: every neuron lives in one irreducible representation, every non-trivial representation appears, and the construction needs a number of neurons proportional to a sum of cubes of representation dimensions. The empirical panels for S3, S4 and S5 show the normalised margin rising to the predicted value and every neuron concentrating on a single representation by the end of training, which is the parallel of the Fourier-concentration observation. They also note that the frequency / representation populations come out close to uniform, matching the construction's own proportions. Toward the end they sketch a generalisation: for groups where the clean theorem's character condition fails, one can choose a subset of representations and a subset of conjugacy classes, solve a small linear system for the class weights and the per-representation scales, and still certify a maximum-margin network supported on that subset — i.e. for general finite groups the prediction is that *some* subset of representations appears, not all of them.

## Limits and open problems they name
Everything is one hidden layer, homogeneous polynomial activations, no biases, one-hot or ±1 inputs, and full-batch-style gradient descent with an L(2,ν) regulariser chosen to match the homogeneity constant — a setting chosen so the algebra closes. The transfer from "global optimum of the regularised objective" to "what SGD reaches" is by theorem for global optima, and the experiments only show the trained networks approaching the predicted margin, not that they attain it; the authors also point to the wider literature showing homogeneous networks reach KKT points of the margin problem rather than necessarily the global maximum. The group theorem needs a character-sum condition that holds for S_k only up to k = 5, and the appendix's generalised version is a recipe rather than a theorem for arbitrary groups. Deep networks, attention, non-homogeneous activations (ReLU is covered only by a matching empirical recipe, and it lands near but not at the exact prediction) and general data distributions are all outside the analysis; the discussion says only that the results may transfer and that this is future work. There is no claim about generalisation, only about which features are learned.

## What we take from it
- **Framing (the big one).** A white-box model that *predicts* a network's contents has to name the selection principle, and this paper shows one is sometimes enough: the loss plus the task's symmetry pins the representation down to a set that can be written in closed form. That is the target our programme should aim at — not "here is what we found in this checkpoint" but "here is the set of solutions this task and this loss admit, and here is which of them training selects". When our account is descriptive, this is the honesty test: which selection principle are we implicitly assuming?
- **Method (certification by duality).** The certificate-pair trick converts an infinite-dimensional "characterise all maximisers" problem into exhibiting one witness plus checking a support-tightness condition. For us the transferable part is the shape of the argument: show one solution, then show that a single distribution over inputs forces every solution to be supported on the same atoms. Any account of ours that claims a feature is *forced* should come with such a witness rather than with a sample of trained runs.
- **Measure.** Two diagnostics are reusable on any trained model, cheap and unsupervised: per-neuron concentration in the predicted basis (max normalised power, measured at initialisation and after training, so the shift is visible) and population spread across the predicted atoms (compared against the construction's predicted proportions). Both can be applied to our own checkpoints wherever we have a candidate basis.
- **Warning (the overdetermination argument).** A network that fits the data perfectly need not resemble the trained one — the paper's own alternative construction is dense in the same basis in which the trained network is sparse. So an account's success in *describing* a trained network is not evidence it explains why that network exists; the discrimination comes from the margin, not from fit. This is the cleanest statement in the folder of why "our account reconstructs the behaviour" is a weak claim.
- **Warning (scale of the claim).** Exact specification is available only for stylised homogeneous networks with a regulariser tuned to the architecture. Reading a 7B residual stream is not this problem, and the paper is scrupulous about saying so; our entries should carry the same discipline, and where we borrow a margin-style argument we should state which homogeneity or symmetry assumption is doing the work.
- **Link to what we already hold.** This supplies the "why" behind two entries: `arxiv-2410.14670`'s premise that a low-dimensional structure is what the loss forces into the weights, and `arxiv-2405.14860`'s finding that features can be irreducibly multi-dimensional — here the multi-dimensionality is exactly what an irreducible representation of the symmetry group supplies, so the two are consistent rather than competing.
