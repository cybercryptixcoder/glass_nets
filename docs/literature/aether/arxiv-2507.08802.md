# The Non-Linear Representation Dilemma: Is Causal Abstraction Enough for Mechanistic Interpretability?

- **Key:** arxiv-2507.08802
- **Authors / organisation:** Denis Sutter, Julian Minder, Thomas Hofmann, Tiago Pimentel; ETH Zürich (Data Analytics Lab) and EPFL
- **Date:** 2025-07-11 (v2 2025-11-12)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.08802
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

Causal abstraction is the strongest available story for what a mechanistic explanation *is*: an algorithm is implemented by a network when there is a map from hidden states to algorithm nodes under which intervening in the network behaves like intervening in the algorithm. Almost every practical instantiation of that idea — distributed alignment search and its descendants — restricts that map to be linear, and inherits the linear representation hypothesis without ever defending it. The authors ask what happens if you honour the definition and let the map be as expressive as you like. The answer, proved and then demonstrated, is that the notion collapses: under weak and plausible assumptions any algorithm can be shown to be implemented by any network, including networks that cannot do the task and networks that have not been trained at all. This is aimed at the interpretive question of whether "the algorithm this model implements" is a fact about the model or a fact about the map you were willing to fit, and it is the sharpest available statement of why an explanation form needs an encoding assumption attached to it.

## What they studied

Two settings. A three-layer MLP with hidden width 16 on the hierarchical equality task (four 4-dimensional vectors, output whether the left and right equalities agree), against three candidate algorithms (both equality relations, left equality relation, identity of first argument). Then Pythia language models from 31M to 410M parameters, at initialisation and across training checkpoints, on the indirect object identification task using the Muhia (2022) dataset, against the ABAB-ABBA algorithm (detect whether the sentence repeats name A or name B in subject position and predict the other name's first token). The unit of analysis is the *alignment map* — a bijection applied to hidden states before a per-node block-wise read-out — not a neuron, feature or circuit; the thing being varied is the expressiveness of that map.

## How they did it

They first re-derive the causal-abstraction definitions from Beckers–Halpern and the Geiger line, in the distributed form where node information is read off a partition of latent variables produced by a layered bijection ϕ, and interventions are performed by mapping into ϕ-space, replacing the target block, and mapping back. They then prove that with ϕ unconstrained and five assumptions in place — countable input space, the network is input-injective at every layer, it is output-surjective onto the class space at every layer, the algorithm's node partial order can be matched by a neuron partition leaving at least one neuron per layer unused, and the network solves the task — every algorithm is an input-restricted distributed abstraction of every network. The proof is constructive per layer: injectivity means the map can identify which input produced a hidden state, surjectivity means it can drive the state to any class, and the unused neuron per layer is the scratch space used to smuggle the algorithm's node value through. They then run the standard DAS training procedure with three map families — identity (privileged bases), orthogonal linear (linear representation hypothesis), and a reversible residual network with tunable depth and width (non-linear representation hypothesis) — scoring held-out interchange-intervention accuracy with intervention size fixed at half the hidden dimension, and compare against the network's own task accuracy. Finally they repeat the whole thing on randomly initialised networks, across Pythia training checkpoints, across five model seeds, and with train and test using disjoint name sets.

## What counts as an explanation here

An alignment map plus a partition, i.e. a claim of the form "these latent directions carry node v and interventions on them behave like interventions on v", graded by interchange-intervention accuracy. The paper's contribution is a claim about the *status* of that object rather than a new one: it argues that this form of explanation is not self-validating, because the map is a free parameter that can absorb essentially any discrepancy, and that what makes it informative is an assumption about encoding that the framework itself does not supply. It is explicitly partial — the authors do not offer a replacement criterion, and their constructive statement of the problem is that a good explanation needs the map's complexity priced into the score.

## How they judged success

Interchange intervention accuracy (IIA) on held-out intervention triples, compared against the DNN's own uninstrumented test accuracy; the DAS cross-entropy objective for fitting ϕ; intervention size as a controlled variable from 4 to 16 latent dimensions; hidden size of the RevNet from small widths up to 16 (MLP) and 64 (language models); number of RevNet layers from 1 to 10; five seeds per configuration with max and mean-with-95%-CI reported; a second MLP task (distributive law); five Pythia seeds replicated from a published suite for robustness; and — the control that matters most — the same procedure run on randomly initialised networks, which is the arm that separates "the algorithm is implemented" from "a map exists". They also report a separability check on the trained MLP: minimum pairwise Euclidean distance between hidden states for 1.28M samples against a random 10k subset, split into pairs sharing an output, pairs sharing both equality-variable values, and pairs sharing neither, over ten seeds.

## What they found

The theorem holds under the stated assumptions, and the empirical arm reproduces the predicted degeneracy. In the hierarchical equality task the identity map is worst and the linear map reproduces the published layer-dependence — IIA for the two equality algorithms drops substantially in the third layer, which is exactly the kind of layer localisation the linear framing is usually read as licensing — while the non-linear map removes that degradation entirely and reaches near-optimal IIA in all layers, so the localisation result does not survive the change of map class. The identity-of-first-argument algorithm sits near 50% for all three map families, which the authors attribute to RevNet capacity rather than to the model's representation. On the trained MLP, raising RevNet hidden size and intervention size monotonically improves IIA, and **with a randomly initialised network the most complex map already exceeds 80% IIA**; as training proceeds, progressively simpler maps suffice. In the IOI task, Pythia-410M begins to solve the task around step 3000, and an eight-layer non-linear map yields near-perfect IIA at every training step including step zero; linear maps track the model's own accuracy curve much more closely and only approach perfect alignment in the fully trained network. Models that never learn the task (31M, 70M) can still be aligned. Across five Pythia seeds the pattern holds with two exceptions — one seed where the DNN fails to learn the task, and one where alignment failed under the authors' untuned setup. The generalisation control is the sharpest result: when train and test use **completely disjoint name sets**, alignment on the randomly initialised network disappears even with the complex map, while the fully trained network still aligns well under the non-linear map and reasonably well under the linear one. The MLP distance check finds injectivity intact but hidden states closer together when samples share an output or share both equality values, i.e. the map has to work harder exactly where the variables agree.

## Limits and open problems they name

The theorem's construction is a form of extreme overfitting that uses perfect knowledge of both network and algorithm, so it says nothing about whether the map is learnable from finite data or whether it transfers — hence the disjoint-names result, which they read as evidence that generalisation, not existence, is the load-bearing condition. Two of the five assumptions are strong: injectivity fails in principle under ReLU bottlenecks and neural collapse, and surjectivity fails in principle under the softmax bottleneck, though they argue both are approximately satisfied in practice and prove almost-sure injectivity for transformers at initialisation in an appendix. They cannot formalise the intuition that the linear map's faithful tracking of the DNN's accuracy curve supports the linear representation hypothesis, and they note the linear map sometimes scores *above* the network's own accuracy, which weakens that reading. Their practical advice is case-by-case: constrain the map if you have grounds to believe linear encoding, otherwise you are stuck with an unpriced accuracy–complexity trade-off. They explicitly leave the trade-off unsolved and point at the probing literature, which faced the same problem and also never resolved it, as the cautionary precedent.

## What we take from it

A warning, and the most important one in this folder, plus a measure. The warning: an explanation form whose validation is "a map exists under which interventions agree" is not a claim about the network until the map's capacity is bounded, and interchange-intervention accuracy is therefore an upper bound on implementation evidence rather than a measure of it — any IIA we publish without a stated map class and map complexity is reporting a number that a randomly initialised model can also achieve. The measure: IIA on a *randomly initialised* network of the same architecture is the correct null for this family of methods, and on this evidence the null is not near chance, it is above 80% on the toy task and near-perfect on IOI. The protocol: pair every IIA number with (i) the map family and its capacity, (ii) the untrained-model arm, and (iii) the disjoint-entity arm, because the third is what distinguished a genuine alignment from a memorised one here. The framing: this is the same partition discipline the folder's stratified-scoring entries demand, applied to the fitting capacity of the instrument itself — a cross-validated, in-distribution alignment map is a fitted object, and the honest report includes what it does on the data it has never seen. Read against the folder's interchange-intervention decomposition entry (which shows a learned rank-one intervention's effect is mostly the training-free copy effect), the two together say the intervention accuracy of a *learned* alignment is dominated by what the fitting procedure chose, not by what the network does.
