# Automated Synthesis and Adversarial Validation of Executable Causal Research Pipelines

- **Key:** arxiv-2607.21173
- **Authors / organisation:** Irena Girshovitz, Dan Zeltzer, Ran Gilad-Bachrach; Tel Aviv University (Biomedical Engineering) and UC Berkeley (School of Public Health)
- **Date:** 2026-07-23
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21173
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (main body sections 1-5 plus the technical appendix; figures read through captions and the prose that restates them, not as images)
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
An automated research agent can produce a script that runs, prints a coefficient, and is wrong, because the causal assumption behind the estimate was never checked. The authors call this a silent failure and want a system that cannot return a number without first having stated its assumptions and been tested against data where those assumptions are deliberately broken. The larger goal is a validity-first form of AI-assisted empirical science, in which an assistant is judged as much on when it declines to make a causal claim as on how closely it matches a reference estimate. The setting that motivates it is health data that cannot be handed to an external model, so the whole design runs on synthetic stand-ins for the real table.

## What they studied
Not a network — an agentic analysis pipeline called ARA, built from LLM agents in two phases. The unit of analysis is the *analysis script and the protocol it implements*. Phase one is a Principal Investigator agent looped against a Reviewer agent over a natural-language research question and a data dictionary. Phase two is five agents (specification extractor, planner, coder, auditor, fixer) that generate a Python analysis script and grade it against synthetic datasets built from structural causal models. The benchmark is the Automated Causal Reasoning Benchmark, from which they take a stratified round-robin sample of 33 cases and run each in an explicit formulation and a stripped ("vague") formulation.

## How they did it
The PI proposes a study design; the Reviewer attacks it on identification grounds — parallel trends, exclusion restrictions — and demands robustness checks such as placebo tests, sensitivity analyses and negative controls, and no approval is granted before at least two full review rounds. The approved protocol is then converted into a causal graph plus structural equations, and a planner enumerates the ways the design could fail: parallel trends violated, a regression-discontinuity cutoff manipulated, an instrument invalid. A data-generating agent writes the generator, producing one baseline dataset where the assumptions hold and up to three datasets where a chosen assumption is broken, all with known effects and with the column names and types of the real data. The code agent writes the script while being explicitly walled off from the ground-truth parameters, and the script must pass syntax checks, smoke tests, an audit for consistency with the protocol, and finally recovery of the injected effect plus detection of the planted violation; a dedicated fixer repairs failures and the loop runs to 12 iterations. The baseline is the same research question and dictionary handed to a single strong model asked for one script, with no protocol, audit or repair. Outcomes scored: which treatment, outcome, causal quantity and identification strategy were recovered; whether the reported confidence interval falls on the same side of zero as the reference; scaled absolute relative error; interval similarity; and a taxonomy of hard failures, incomplete inference and validity concerns.

## What counts as an explanation here
An explicit, reviewable artifact chain rather than a description of a mechanism: a protocol that names the estimand, design, identification strategy and required diagnostics; a causal graph and structural equations; a generated script; and a set of assertion-style tests — one per identification assumption — that the script has to survive. It is deliberately partial and predictive only in a narrow sense: it does not say what the world is like, it says which analyses are permitted to speak and flags candidates for human review when they are not. The authors are clear that the flags are model-generated propositions, not ground-truth labels, and that the terminal script of a run is not necessarily an approved one.

## How they judged success
Benchmark label recovery per component; direction of the reported interval relative to zero compared with the reference interval, with sign flips counted separately from zero-crossings and from missing intervals; scaled absolute relative error of the point estimate; confidence-interval Jaccard similarity and binary overlap; a failure audit table splitting hard script failures from incomplete inference; and cost. A separate qualitative category counts outputs where the pipeline flagged a violated assumption, failed a required diagnostic, or downgraded the causal claim to a descriptive one. 33 cases per condition, so the authors themselves say small counts carry little weight and no significance testing is reported.

## What they found
Protocol construction and adversarial validation did *not* reliably improve numerical agreement with the benchmark. On explicit questions scaled absolute relative error fell from 39.27% for the single-script baseline to 29.85% at the pipeline's final checkpoint, but on vague questions the ordering reversed. What did change was the error profile: no sign flips in either prompt condition at the final checkpoint, against one sign flip from the baseline in the vague condition; more intervals that include zero when the reference is strictly one-signed (4 of 33 explicit, 5 of 33 vague); and more missing intervals (11 of 33 explicit and 13 of 33 vague, against 7 and 5 for the baseline). Hard failures fell across checkpoints — explicit 14 of 33 before audit to 9 of 33 at the end, vague 19 to 11 — but the simpler baseline still finished cleanly more often (7 of 33 and 5 of 33). Treatment recovery under vague prompts was the clearest specification gain (83% of evaluable cases against 75%), while recovery of the causal quantity was the weakest component and stayed behind the baseline. The audit and validation loop cost roughly two orders of magnitude more per run (about $12.83 explicit and $11.94 vague per run against about $0.15), over roughly 2,000 and 1,744 model calls.

## Limits and open problems they name
Thirty-three cases, with the sign-flip result resting on a single case, and every comparison descriptive. The final checkpoint is whatever the loop stopped at, approved or budget-expired, so it mixes validated analyses with abandoned ones. Validity flags are not ground truth and need expert review. Stricter auditing improved protocol compliance while making numerical convergence to a benchmark-like estimate harder within the fixed budget — an interaction the authors flag as unresolved. Both phases depend on specific model versions and prompts, the pipeline has many more moving parts than a single call, and the synthetic data are built as stress tests rather than as calibrated reproductions of the real variance.

## What we take from it
A framing and a warning. The framing: the authors turn "valid" into a conjunction with a checkable second clause — accurate when the assumptions hold, and explicit about which assumptions were checked — and that is exactly the split our white-box claims need. The warning is the headline reversal: their intervention measurably improved the internal process (failures down at every checkpoint) and did not improve agreement with the reference, so a process metric moving in the right direction is not evidence that the output improved. For us: every named assumption in our model's report gets a rung in a degradation ladder generated by breaking that assumption in the reference system, the do-nothing or refuse-to-answer output is scored as its own category rather than as a failure, and downstream agreement is reported alongside the process metrics rather than instead of them.
