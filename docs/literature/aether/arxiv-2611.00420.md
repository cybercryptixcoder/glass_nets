## What they wanted, and why
An attribution graph — transcoder features as nodes, backward Jacobian direct effects as edges — is supposed to *be* the explanation of a model's computation on one prompt, but the authors point out that a short prompt's graph already contains hundreds of active features and thousands of nonzero edges, and that turning that object into a safety-relevant sentence still costs an expert hours. Their larger goal is to make the reading step itself automatable, so a red-teamer can ask a question of a graph in natural language and get back both an answer and an inspectable subgraph. The framing claim is that graphs are easier for an LLM to read than raw activations because they arrive pre-structured: nodes carry dictionary-learned semantic labels, edges carry quantitative attribution, and direction runs from embeddings to logits. Against that they set the honest counterweight, which they state up front: it is unclear how to evaluate the quality of a circuit analysis at all, because circuit-faithfulness metrics swing with the ablation convention and auto-interp labels regularly miss a feature's in-context role.

## What they studied
The subject models are Gemma-2-2B (Gemma Scope PLTs) for the probe task, Qwen3-8B taboo LoRAs for secret elicitation, and Qwen3-4B (public transcoder dictionary) for refusal suppression. The artifact under analysis is always a **directed weighted graph over sparse transcoder features plus token-embedding and logit nodes**, built in a local replacement model that freezes attention patterns, LayerNorm scales and feature activations at their forward-pass values. The reader is an off-the-shelf frontier LLM (Minimax-M3 orchestrator, GPT-OSS-120B subagents by default) that never sees weights or activations — only tool calls over the adjacency matrix, a Neuronpedia label lookup, and the ability to re-run the subject model under an intervention. The unit of analysis is therefore neither a component nor a feature but an **analysis**: a natural-language verdict, a curated subgraph of supernodes, and the tool-call trace that produced them.

## How they did it
1. **Package the graph as a substrate, not a vector.** Nodes are (layer, feature, position) triples; edge weight is the direct effect of a source feature's decoder write, propagated through the frozen linear model, onto a target feature's encoder read; a residual-error node per layer-position records transcoder reconstruction error and is deliberately hidden from the agent. Features from layers the backward pass never reaches are excluded, so every graph has a known boundary.
2. **Give the reader nine tools and no training.** The inventory is GetTopLogits, GetTopFeatures, InspectFeature (Neuronpedia label, top-activating examples, promoted/suppressed tokens, sparsity), GetUpstream, TracePathSubagent, BuildCircuit, plus two task-swapped diff tools and a vote tally. Subagents get a read-only subset so they cannot recursively spawn or pin circuits.
3. **Make the question a "skill", not a fine-tune.** Each proxy task is a static spec pairing a system prompt with a tool subset and a written workflow. Extending to a new safety question means writing a new skill; the paper's own example is a six-step elicitation workflow with a stated tool-call budget of three to eight calls.
4. **Probe task: 80 graphs from four datasets.** Two linear probes on the mean-pooled layer-22 (or layer-12/17) residual are trained per task, differing only in training split — one on a stereotype-consistent split where the spurious cue perfectly predicts the label, one on a balanced split where it carries nothing. Ten prompts per task and probe are selected where both probes are right, ranked by confidence gap. A judge then categories each report's named features as spurious- or core-aligned and returns a verdict.
5. **Secret task: target-versus-sibling differencing.** For each taboo LoRA they build the LoRA's graph and the base model's graph on the identical prompt, and score each feature by its activation minus the largest sibling activation at the same (layer, position, feature). Upstream path-tracing is switched off entirely on the argument that the LoRA is adversarial toward its own output, so late-layer promoted-token statistics point at the evasion machinery rather than the secret.
6. **Refusal task: closed loop with a commit rule.** The orchestrator scouts the graph, shortlists features, sweeps each at a coarse negative scale, escalates along a monotonic ladder while each step leaks more topic content, forms supernodes from co-active features, measures every candidate against a judge ensemble, and then *commits to one* — the committed pick is the headline, with best-of-five reported separately as an upper bound.
7. **Ablate the agent.** On each task they remove the thing that makes it agentic — edge traversal and the verify loop — and read the graph's top-k features in one pass instead, so the value of the scaffolding is measured rather than assumed.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **natural-language analysis plus a curated subgraph** — description and graph together, and explicitly partial. The report is task-relative by design: the authors' stated lesson is that the right way to interpret a feature depends on the downstream safety question rather than on a static label, so a feature's meaning is re-derived per query instead of being committed to a dictionary. The canonical negative example is their own run: a mid-layer feature whose corpus-level auto-interp label is about code and technical snippets is pinned across refusal prompts as a safety/warning precursor that feeds first-person refusal templates, and intervening on it moves behaviour — the label and the causal role disagree, and only the intervention settles it. Nothing here is predictive in the folder's sense: the output cannot forecast an unseen intervention, and its predictive content lives in the subgraph a human is invited to inspect.

## How they judged success
Three proxy tasks with external ground truth, each scored by a judge or an exact match, plus agent ablations, an orchestrator sweep and a pass-to-pass stability measure. **Probe task:** per-report categorical verdict (spurious / causal / mixed / none) from a judge that also counts named features, so a biased run is correct only if the verdict is "spurious" and an unbiased run only if it is "causal" or "mixed"; the two probes are judged independently to avoid confirmation bias. **Secret task:** closed-menu accuracy over a fixed 20-word list and open-vocabulary top-10 recall with morphological-neighbour grading; the closed protocol is disclosed as candidate enumeration rather than signal injection. **Refusal task:** pooled score over five judge models on two axes, ties at |Δ| ≤ 0.04 against a replicated diff-in-mean refusal-direction baseline built with its own 128-pair train / 32-pair validation protocol and a KL filter. **Controls:** two tool ablations and a three-orchestrator sweep per task, five full passes per graph with SD, and Jaccard agreement between passes on both the pinned feature set and the layer set.

## What they found
- **Probe task: a real but thin margin, and the baselines' failure mode is a YES-detector.** Oracle 78.2% ± 2.4 (single passes 76.2–82.5%) against 72.5% for a transcoder-cosine baseline and 65.0% for an SAE-cosine one. Both baselines are perfect on biased rankings (40/40) and collapse on unbiased ones — 0/10 on the two datasets where the spurious cue is not geometrically aligned with the probe direction — and the oracle keeps that shape of failure at smaller magnitude. Most of its remaining errors are negation cues missed because the hypothesis carries many content words and few negation tokens.
- **On that task the agent is not doing anything.** Dropping Neuronpedia inspection *raises* accuracy to 81%, and a single-pass read of the graph's top-k features with no traversal at all scores 77.5% — indistinguishable from the full pipeline. The authors read this as a property of the task (the biased probe's attribution mass sits on the spurious token itself) rather than as evidence that traversal never matters, and they point at the next task to show it does.
- **Secret elicitation: large margin over projections, still behind a trained reader.** Closed-menu 75.0% overall against 25.8% for the residual-SAE projection and 1.7% for the transcoder projection; the trained activation-oracle baseline reaches 86.7% closed and 74.2% open. The projection baselines fail for two instructive reasons: the transcoder reads at the MLP input, upstream of where the concept is shaped, so no layer choice recovers it (≤5.8% at every layer), and the prefill is dominated by chat-template tokens, so top features are template detectors.
- **The differencing is what carries it.** Open-vocabulary accuracy drops from 75.0% to 43.3% when the menu is removed — the largest drop of any method — and the paper says plainly that for some secrets the concept is simply not present in the transcoder logit-lens decode of any surviving feature at any layer band, so no aggregation or prompt engineering recovers them. This is a representation-level limit of the basis, not an agent failure.
- **Refusal: parity with a hand-fit direction, and only when you let it try five times.** 0.804 committed pick against 0.825 for the diff-in-mean baseline (10 wins / 20 ties / 20 losses), 0.854 best-of-five (16 / 24 / 10), from 0.041 unsteered. Here the ablation bites hard: the one-shot pick moves the score by +0.074 over no intervention against +0.766 for the closed loop, because releasing a refusal gate requires finding which upstream feature actually flips the output, not the feature with the largest direct effect on the refusal opener.
- **Stable verdict, unstable circuit.** Two passes agree on the pinned feature set at Jaccard 0.511 but on the layer set at 0.754 — the analysis keeps landing in the same place and names different components.
- **Two behaviours, two shapes.** On a smaller refusal-versus-censorship case study, refusal routes through one recurring mid-layer precursor feature that can be cut to release the output on every refusal prompt, while censorship prompts share no bottleneck at all (best interventions at five different layers, no feature recurring) and the single diff-in-mean direction outperforms the feature-level method on that family. The authors read this as one behaviour localised and one distributed, and flag that the case study is on a different judging protocol and is not part of the headline.

## Limits and open problems they name
Evaluation is the weak point, and they say so: the proxy tasks are proxies, the feature-class assignment in the probe task is defined by the authors, and they invite future work to build evaluations that measure whether the pruned subgraphs and revised feature readings are useful downstream. Verdict stability is asserted to be enough because many subgraphs can support one answer, but they concede they cannot show that differently-featured circuits behind the same verdict are semantically equivalent. Secret elicitation is bounded by the transcoder basis. The refusal result is a tie with a direction fitted on labelled data, which the oracle does not need — that is the paper's most favourable framing of it.

**Limits I would add, marked as mine:** the judge is the measuring instrument for two of the three tasks and is not validated against human labels anywhere; one judge model had to be discarded because it returned verdicts contradicting its own reported feature counts, which is a warning about the scorer family rather than about that one model. The probe-task scoring rule is asymmetric by construction — a biased run must say "spurious", an unbiased run merely must not — so a detector biased toward "mixed" is rewarded on half the set; the baselines' near-perfect biased scores and the oracle's matching shape suggest the accuracy is largely the sum of an easy and a hard half. The orchestrator contributes 70–78.2% on the probe task, which is a spread as large as the margin over baseline. Blocked judge calls in the refusal task are set to the maximum on both axes rather than dropped or imputed, on the stated argument that a safety classifier firing is corroboration; the call rate is symmetric across arms (6/250 versus 7/250), which is the right control but the rule still rounds a censored measurement up. And a specificity ladder is reported where, at a stronger scale, a softer feature yields a *more specific* politically salient fabrication while both completions score alike — an explicit admission that the aggregate metric cannot see the difference that matters, and a reason to distrust the pooled score as the unit of success.

## What we take from it
- **Method — treat the graph reader as a separate, measurable instrument.** This is the folder's first entry whose object is the *consumer* of a white-box artifact rather than the artifact, and it supplies the missing convention: ablate the scaffolding, not just the reader. Their two ablations are the cheapest possible version and they give opposite answers on two tasks, which is exactly the diagnostic value — traversal is worthless where the answer is one hop from the readout and decisive where the intervention has to be found by measurement. Any agentic layer we add to a predictive model needs the same pair: full pipeline versus one-shot top-k on the same items.
- **Warning — a verdict can be stable while the circuit is not, and only one of them is the explanation.** Jaccard 0.511 on features against 0.754 on layers, five passes, fixed graphs, same model, is a direct measurement of how much of a "recovered circuit" is the answer the reader wanted. It is the strongest available argument for the folder's standing position that overlap metrics and verdict accuracy are different claims, and it adds the concrete tolerance band: an agentic reader's named components are about half stable at pass level.
- **Method — differencing against a sibling graph is the cheap way to subtract what the model already does.** Scoring each feature as its activation minus the strongest sibling activation at the same coordinate removes shared scaffold in one pass — here it deleted three template-detector features that looked exactly like the answer on the single graph — and it costs one extra forward pass on the same prompt with a different checkpoint. This generalises to every fine-tune, LoRA and checkpoint pair in this folder: the artifact that distinguishes the specialisation is the diff, not the top activations.
- **Warning — the failure mode of a feature basis is that the answer is not in it, and the tell is the menu lift.** A closed-menu score of 75.0% against an open-vocabulary 43.3% is not a protocol detail; the 31.7-point gap is the part of the task the option list solved for it, and it is computable for any readout we build. Reporting a constrained score without its open-vocabulary twin overstates what the representation carries.
- **Framing — a label is a hypothesis and the intervention is the evidence.** The pinned feature whose dictionary label said code but whose role was a safety precursor is the cleanest statement in the folder of why static auto-interp description and query-driven reading are different objects, and it argues for our own reporting: whenever a decoded concept is named, print the intervention that moved behaviour, because a corpus-derived label cannot arbitrate its own in-context role.
