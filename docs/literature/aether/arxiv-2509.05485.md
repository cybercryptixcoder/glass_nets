## What they wanted, and why
The paper takes seriously the idea that a classifier's confidence should be inherited from the training distribution rather than from the classifier's own outputs: if a test input sits in a region of representation space that the training set populated thickly, trust the prediction, and if it does not, do not. The practical target is a per-sample accept/reject rule that can be bolted onto an already-trained model without touching its weights and without any outlier data of its own, and the framing is explicitly instance-level reliability rather than a single OOD-vs-in score. The authors also want the rule to work when there is no task head to read at all, which is why the score is computed on a frozen embedding model downstream of the classifier rather than on logits. Read in the folder's terms, this is the continuous-representation-density reliability instrument that the activation-partition bound in `arxiv-2106.08365` names as future work and that no other entry has supplied.

## What they studied
Six ImageNet-1K classifiers spanning two families and a wide capacity range: ResNet-50 and ResNet-101 (25.6M and 44.5M parameters), DeiT-Tiny/Small/Base (5.7M/22.1M/86.4M), and ShuffleNet-V2 (2.3M). The density is computed in six *other* frozen embedding spaces: DINO-v1 ViT-S/16 and ViT-B/16 and ViT-B/8 (384 and 768 dimensions), DINO-v2 ViT-S/14 and ViT-B/14 (384 and 768, pretrained on a 142M-image unlabelled corpus), and MobileNet-V2 (1000 dimensions, supervised). Evaluation uses ImageNet-1K as the in-distribution base, ImageNet-V2 split 75/25 into a tuning set and an internal test set, and ObjectNet restricted to ImageNet-overlapping classes (roughly 10k images) as the external test set. The unit of analysis is the individual test sample, and the quantity is a count of near neighbours in a frozen embedding database.

## How they did it
Embed every ImageNet-1K training image once in each embedding model and store the vectors in a vector database, so the base set is a fixed table of roughly 1.28M rows per embedding. For a query, retrieve its top-K neighbours by cosine distance (K set to 1000 as a generous buffer) and declare the prediction *confident* iff at least N of the top-K neighbours lie within cosine distance L; otherwise the prediction is discarded. Two parameters, N and L, are the whole model of the filter. They are tuned on the ImageNet-V2 tuning split by sweeping 241 distance thresholds and several neighbour counts, computing the resulting accuracy-versus-coverage curve, and maximising a normalised area between that curve and the classifier's own all-data accuracy over the coverage interval 0.1 to 1.0 - a choice they justify by noting the curve is monotone and smooth there and noisy below it. The tuned (N, L) is then frozen and applied to two label-disjoint partitions of each test set, so every number is a mean and standard deviation over three disjoint class subsets. A separate arm derives a continuous score from the same neighbour distances, the mean cosine similarity to the N nearest neighbours, and feeds it to standard OOD metrics; that score is compared against Energy (temperature swept over three values), Mahalanobis, and ODIN (temperature and input-perturbation epsilon swept on the tuning split). The comparison is deliberately *not* representation-controlled: the baselines read the supervised classifier's own features and logits, while the density score reads DINO-v2 alone, and the authors say so plainly in a subsection titled for the applicability of the comparison.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **per-sample confidence verdict with a coverage knob**. The output is a boolean per input plus, if you threshold differently, a continuous density score; the explanatory content is one sentence - this input is far from anything the training set put nearby, in a space the model never saw. There is no decomposition into features, no attribution to units, no claim about why the prediction is wrong when it is wrong. It is partial and deliberately so: it predicts a *rate* (the accuracy of the retained subset) rather than a label or a mechanism, and it is model-agnostic to the point that the same filter is applied unchanged to six different classifiers. The one genuine structural claim is negative and useful: whatever the classifier knows beyond the embedding geometry is not in the geometry, since the density filter's ceiling is set by which images cluster rather than by which classes are hard.

## How they judged success
Two framings, and they are not scored the same way. (1) The filter arm is scored by an area-based normalised gain over the classifier's base accuracy, computed on the tuning split and reported on the held-out partitions as mean ± standard deviation across three label-disjoint subsets - a proper tuned/holdout split, which is more discipline than several entries in this folder show. (2) The OOD arm is scored by AUROC, AUPR-in, AUPR-out and FPR95 against Energy, Mahalanobis and ODIN, with the baselines' hyperparameters tuned by AUROC on the same tuning split. Controls: three label-disjoint partitions so each label appears in exactly one evaluation subset; embedding dimensionality held fixed while the pretraining corpus changes (DINO-v1 B/16 versus DINO-v2 B/14, both 768-dimensional) to separate capacity from data; and a comparison of coverage curves at low coverage to check whether all embeddings converge to the same accuracy. No shuffled-embedding or random-neighbour baseline appears anywhere, and the paper does not report an ablation that removes the neighbour structure while keeping the volume of information constant.

## What they found
The filter works in the weak sense: at every coverage level below 1.0 the retained subset is more accurate than the full set, and the gain is largest for the strongest embedding. Normalised gain at the best N runs 0.308 to 0.487 on the internal test set across model pairs, and the best pair in every row except one is DINO-v2 ViT-B/14 (ResNet-101 0.477, ResNet-50 0.487, DeiT-Base 0.480, DeiT-Small 0.472, DeiT-Tiny 0.442, ShuffleNet-V2 0.417 against 0.447 for DINO-v1 B/8). Base accuracies are 0.696 (DeiT-Base), 0.687 (ResNet-101), 0.676 (ResNet-50), 0.666, 0.582 and 0.538 (ShuffleNet-V2) on the internal set, and collapse to 0.269, 0.304, 0.293, 0.229, 0.152 and 0.117 on ObjectNet - where the same normalised gains fall to 0.19, 0.24, 0.24, 0.19, 0.12 and 0.096. The single most interesting number in the paper is about the neighbour count rather than the gain: for five of six classifiers the best (N, L) has **N = 1**, i.e. the entire filter reduces to "is the single nearest training image close enough", and only the best embedding (DINO-v2 B/14) prefers more neighbours (7 to 60 depending on classifier). MobileNet-V2 never wins despite having the largest embedding (1000 dimensions) and the narrowest pretraining set - its best gains are 0.169 to 0.244, roughly half the DINO-v2 column - and the controlled 768-dimensional comparison credits DINO-v2 with 0.04 to 0.07 more normalised gain than DINO-v1 while adding dimensions within a family buys only 0.01 to 0.03. Combining embedding models greedily is *worse* than the single best model in every case, and the authors read the low-coverage convergence across embeddings as evidence that the data distribution decides more than the embedding does. On the OOD arm the picture is blunter: on the internal set every method, theirs included, is at chance - embedding density 0.5867 AUROC on DeiT-Tiny against Energy 0.5858, Mahalanobis 0.5850, ODIN 0.5878, with FPR95 around 0.90 to 0.93 - so on the near-shift the geometry has essentially nothing to say; on the external set Energy and ODIN reach about 0.90 AUROC while embedding density reaches 0.851 with FPR95 0.4426, competitive but consistently behind. Cost: about 39 hours of CPU encoding to build the base table and roughly 8 GB of storage for 768-dimensional embeddings plus auxiliary statistics.

## Limits and open problems they name
The paper's own most damning result is the internal-set one, where all four methods sit at chance AUROC and the authors concede substantial overlap between in-distribution and internally shifted samples rather than blaming the score. They name the comparison as not representation-controlled, and they accept the performance gap on ObjectNet as evidence of "classifier-specific" signal beyond geometry. They name the extension to text as blocked: language has no natural discretisation, sliding windows become prohibitive at corpus scale, and per-token embedding storage at hundreds of billions of tokens is infeasible without compression. The Discussion adds an assertion they do not test - that a more sophisticated embedding model will produce a better confidence curve, where "sophisticated" bundles dimensions, input resolution and pretraining richness - and the greedy embedding combination is admitted to be a heuristic that underperforms, with per-sample model selection floated as the alternative. Nothing in the paper checks the score against the classifier's own softmax confidence on the same inputs, so the "we do not need logits" advantage is left as an argument rather than a head-to-head.

## What we take from it
(1) **The instrument the partition-bound paper asked for, and a warning about its ceiling.** `arxiv-2106.08365` wanted continuous representation density and said it needed a different bound; this paper supplies the density and no bound at all - the number is a neighbour count, and the reliability claim is empirical. That trade is the whole lesson: dropping the bound makes the score cheap and model-agnostic, and it costs the thing the bound bought, which is a *reasoned* per-sample error estimate. (2) **A cheap and very informative diagnostic worth stealing: report the tuned neighbour count.** N = 1 for five of six classifiers says the local geometry that matters is nearest-image identity, not local density, and a density story that works only at k = 1 is a nearest-neighbour story wearing a density label. Any k-NN or kernel-density reliability score we publish carries its tuned k and the curve over k. (3) **The label-disjoint partition control is the right default for any per-sample score we report**, because it makes the reported standard deviation a real estimate of cross-partition variability instead of a seed artefact. (4) **A framing to reuse and a negative result to cite**: the paper's chance-level internal-set AUROC across *all four* methods is direct evidence that near-shift confidence is a representation-geometry problem no scoring function fixes, which is the kind of number the folder's auditing entries rarely get to quote. (5) **Design constraint for us**: the filter optimises *coverage-conditional accuracy*, an aggregate, and never asks whether the discarded samples are the ones the model gets wrong for a reason we can name - so it cannot be turned into a per-sample prediction of *which* class is right, which is what a white-box model has to deliver.
