# Open- and Closed-Loop Neural Network Verification using Polynomial Zonotopes

- **Key:** arxiv-2207.02715
- **Authors / organisation:** Niklas Kochdumper, Christian Schilling, Matthias Althoff, Stanley Bak; Stony Brook University, Aalborg University, Technical University of Munich
- **Date:** 2022-07-06
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.02715
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
The authors start from a diagnosis rather than a gap: the hard step in both open-loop and closed-loop network checking is enclosing the image of an input set through the network, and because that image is usually non-convex, every convex enclosure (interval, zonotope, polytope, star set) pays too much slack for the property to be decided. Their proposal is to make the *enclosure itself* non-convex by keeping polynomial structure, and they argue this matters twice over in feedback loops, where the set of control inputs and the set of states are correlated by construction. The larger goal is a single set algebra used for both the network abstraction and the plant reachability, so the two never have to be reconciled across incompatible representations.

## What they studied
Feed-forward nets with 2-D inputs and 2-D outputs and one hidden layer of 50 units (randomly generated), plus the ACAS Xu networks (6 layers, 50 ReLU units each, 45 nets and 10 specs in the benchmark), plus six plant benchmark families from two cited suites. The unit of analysis is the **image set as a whole**, represented as a sparse polynomial zonotope — a constant offset, dependent generators whose scalars appear with non-negative integer exponents (so products of scalars are expressible), and independent generators on the ordinary [-1,1] box. A single neuron's activation is the abstraction boundary; the abstraction is a low-order polynomial, not a convex region.

## How they did it
Per layer, per neuron: push the offset row of the weight matrix and its bias through the incoming polynomial zonotope, extract ordinary interval bounds from the *enclosing zonotope* (the cheap convex shadow of the object), fit a quadratic on that interval, push the polynomial zonotope through the quadratic exactly-as-a-polynomial, and finally add a box holding the worst-case difference between the activation and the quadratic on the interval. Three fitting routes are given and compared: least squares on N points (N = 10), a closed-form ReLU fit forcing value and slope to zero at the lower end and to match the upper end, and a second-order Taylor expansion at the interval midpoint. For rectified units the polynomial step is skipped when the interval has one sign, and the exact identity or zero map is used instead. Representation size grows with each polynomial pushforward, so order reduction (or a drop to the linear fit, which adds no generators) is applied between layers. For closed-loop work, the image set and the current reachable set are combined by a Cartesian product that deliberately keeps them sharing dependent scalars — the correlation between a state and the command it produces is what survives — and a conservative polynomialization integrator then advances the extended state-plus-input system for one sampling period.

## What counts as an explanation here
A replacement set, not a description: the account of the network is "this polynomial zonotope contains every output for every input in this set," and it is explicitly an over-approximation, not exact — the polynomial is an abstraction of each neuron's transfer curve and the error box covers the residue. It predicts in a genuine sense (any property expressible as a linear inequality can be decided against the containment, and reachable sets can be pushed forward), but it says nothing about which internal unit caused a given output, and the polynomial fit itself is a crude functional summary of the unit, not a mechanism.

## How they judged success
Tightness by picture, speed by table. Image tightness is a side-by-side plot against the true image for three activation families, comparing zonotope (linear) abstraction, star sets with triangle relaxation, Taylor models, and polynomial zonotopes. Open-loop success is wall-clock time on five ACAS Xu net-spec pairs against thirteen competition tools whose times are taken from the competition's public results repository rather than re-run. Closed-loop success is wall-clock time on eight benchmark families against Sherlock, JuliaReach, Verisig, Verisig 2.0 and ReachNN*, with those competitors' times also lifted from their published tables. Soundness of the two new operations is proved (quadratic map, Cartesian product); no accuracy metric accompanies any runtime, and no ablation separates the contributions of the quadratic fit, the order reduction, and the dependency-preserving product.

## What they found
The image comparison is the qualitative claim: convex enclosures cannot bend and the polynomial zonotope can, and against Taylor models the authors give two reasons for their edge — a zonotopic remainder that grows more slowly than a Taylor remainder, and a quadratic fit that in their observation beats a Taylor expansion for ReLU (closed form) and for sigmoid/tanh (regression beats expansion). Open-loop: times cluster at a fraction of a second, competitive with the fastest competition tool on all five pairs, with the notable outlier that their own method takes 34.1 s and 40.6 s on two of the five where most tools take under a second — the split-and-recurse loop is not free. Closed-loop: faster than every other tool on all but one of the benchmark families, attributed not to arithmetic speed but to tightness — the enclosure is good enough that coarse integrator settings suffice and no splitting of the set is needed.

## Limits and open problems they name
None of the disclosure kind: there is no limitations section. The limits are stated in passing and they are real. The polynomial is truncated at quadratic "for simplicity", with higher order left as an extension. Depth is handled by order reduction or by reverting to the linear abstraction, and the paper does not say how much accuracy that costs. The quadratic rendering of a unit still fits a smooth curve to a piecewise-linear function, and for rectified units the residual is a fixed quadratic-minus-linear discrepancy whose size depends on the interval — never reported. Competitor numbers are inherited from other papers, so the comparison inherits their machine, settings and version choices.

## What we take from it
Three things. **First, a representation that is closed under the operation you care about is worth more than a representation that is tight for the object's shape.** Polynomial zonotopes are closed under polynomial maps, so a quadratic neuron abstraction propagates without any case split, no sign patterns, no unions — the exponential in run 623's pattern enumeration and run 635's polytope splitting simply does not arise, and it is replaced by a growth in generator count that is handled by a single order-reduction call. This is the structural answer to the folder's recurring complaint that exactness costs an exponential: change the algebra until the expensive operation is free, then pay a controlled, reportable cost per layer instead. **Second, the correlation that the closed-loop thread keeps losing is preserved here by a bookkeeping choice, not by a tighter arithmetic** — the product in Alg. 2 is only cheap because the input set was built from the state set, so both have identical dependent scalars, and the paper says so in one line. That is run 632's "keep the chain symbolic and evaluate at the end" arrived at from a different direction: the reason to keep the dependency is that the two sets *are* the same trajectory, and any representation that forgets this must re-derive the coupling with an interval. **Third, a warning about the folder's own reporting habit.** This paper is the first in the thread to beat the whole tool list on time on nearly every closed-loop benchmark, and it reports no width ratio, no generator count, no ablation — the exact gap runs 628-636 kept naming — while *also* being the one paper whose mechanism makes the slack measurable in principle (the error box added per neuron *is* the slack, and is available per layer for free). It is therefore evidence for the sharper claim: **when the slack is structurally available the authors still do not print it, so the gap is about incentives, not instrumentation** — and the honest way to use this result is to credit the speed claim while treating "tight" as an unverified adjective until someone in this thread measures it.
