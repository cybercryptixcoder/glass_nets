## What they wanted, and why
Concept-based explanation methods — concept bottleneck models and concept activation vectors — all rest on one unexamined assumption: that the human-labelled concepts they say a prediction depends on are actually *there* in the input, and are found in the right place inside it. The authors argue that the field has scores for class accuracy and for the fidelity of a bottleneck but no standard instrument for checking this assumption, so a method can look interpretable while naming concepts the image does not contain. They want metrics cheap enough to be run as a routine check, a benchmark that applies them to a well-known method, and a vocabulary for the two ways a concept claim can fail: it is absent from the input, or it is present but not localised where the annotator put it. The stated motive is practical — an explanation that names "white throat" for a bird whose throat is visibly black is worse than no explanation, because it invites acting on it.

## What they studied
One post-hoc concept bottleneck model family (Yuksekgonul et al.'s CAV-plus-bottleneck construction) reproduced on Caltech-UCSD Birds-200-2011: 200 classes, 112 binary concept labels, and centre-pixel coordinates for 12 body parts. The feature extractor is a ResNet-18; CAVs are SVM normals over positive/negative image sets; the final classifier is a single layer on the concept vector. The unit of analysis is the *concept* as the model names it — a row of the classifier's weight matrix as a "global" importance, and that weight times the projected concept value for a single image as "local" importance — not a neuron, direction or circuit inside the backbone. No transformer, no language model, no non-visual domain is touched.

## How they did it
They build three instruments. Concept activation mapping (CoAM) multiplies each pre-pooling feature map channel-wise with a concept's CAV and sums over channels, giving one spatial heatmap per concept per image, upsampled and overlaid on the input — the visual analogue of a class activation map, but weighted by a concept direction instead of a class row. The concept global importance metric (CGIM) compares the model's whole concept-by-class weight matrix, and the average projected concept matrix, with the human-annotated concept-by-class matrix, using cosine similarity per row in three variants (weights alone, averaged projections alone, and their pointwise product), since the two matrices live on different scales and cannot be compared entry-wise. The concept existence metric (CEM) ranks concepts per image by importance and asks what fraction of the top *l* appear in that image's ground-truth concept label. The concept location metric (CLM) asks whether the concept's CoAM heatmap contains the annotated centre pixel of the concept's body part, thresholding the map to a region covering 1/12, 3/12 or 6/12 of the image (α = 1, 3, 6); concepts that are not tied to a body part (overall size, shape, plumage pattern) are dropped, leaving 89 of the 112. They then reproduce the baseline method across SVM regularisation strengths and positive/negative sample counts, pick a configuration by classification accuracy, and run the three metrics on it.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not a new form of explanation — the explanation remains the ordered list of concept names, plus a per-concept heatmap. What the paper contributes is a *grader* for that form: an alignment score between the concept claims a model makes and annotation that was produced independently of it. It is deliberately partial and the authors say so: the metrics are sanity checks that concepts are correctly identified in the inputs, not measures of whether the concepts are useful as an explanation. Notably the grader is external to the network — a human annotation file is the reference, and nothing in it is a prediction about what the network does under intervention.

## How they judged success
Three quantitative scores with natural 0-1 ranges and an ideal value of 1 (CGIM near +1 on cosine; CEM and CLM as accuracies), reported per concept and per class in full appendices and aggregated as histograms. The comparisons that carry the argument are internal: three different ways of ranking concept importance (weights, projected values, their product), three top-*l* cutoffs (1, 3, 5), three heatmap thresholds (α = 1, 3, 6), and two test populations (the whole test set versus only correctly classified images). There is no competing metric, no second method, and no random or shuffled-annotation control; the sanity check on the reproduction is classification accuracy against the published baseline, and against a bottleneck-free ResNet-18 on the same data.

## What they found
The reproduction lands near the published accuracy — 59.1% for the post-hoc bottleneck at N = 100 positive/negative examples and λ = 1, against 75.4% for the same ResNet-18 without the bottleneck. Concept existence is weak for the importance ranking that actually drives the prediction (weight × projected value): the single most important concept is in the image only 49.3% of the time across the test set and 55.4% on correctly classified images, falling to 41.2% and 45.8% at top-5. Ranking by the projected concept value alone is far more optimistic (84.3% at top-1) and the authors explicitly decline to rely on it, because that value does not determine the class without the classifier weight. Localisation is worse than existence: at α = 1 the annotated centre pixel falls inside the concept's activated region 13.3% of the time for the combined ranking, and even when the scanned region is half the image (α = 6) the figure is 59.0% — so the heatmaps spread over whole objects rather than finding parts. The global alignment scores are the most damaging: they are broadly distributed with a substantial negative tail, and one concept's weight row has cosine −0.63 with its annotated row, while two classes sit near zero. Their diagnosis of the cause is concept correlation — "grey leg" and "grey bill" are easy to confuse, and co-occurring attributes let a model score well while naming the wrong part of a bird.

## Limits and open problems they name
CEM needs per-image concept annotations for a test set, which exists for almost no dataset, and a metric tied to one dataset invites overfitting to it; they argue that only the top few concepts per image are needed, so annotation is cheap. CLM needed a "best guess" mapping from each of the 112 concepts to one of the 12 annotated body parts, and the heatmaps come from a construction the baseline model was not designed around, so the localisation score measures the model *and* the visualisation. They concede that collapsing alignment to a couple of numbers loses detail and that the baseline's own goal was cheap concept bottlenecks, meaning a per-image-annotated CBM would beat it on this benchmark by construction. All measurements are on one dataset, one backbone and one method family.

## What we take from it
A method and a warning, both directly transferable. The method: split "does the named thing exist in the input" from "is the named thing in the right place", and score each separately, because the two dissociate sharply here (49% existence versus 13% localisation at the tightest threshold) and a single notion of "concept alignment" would have hidden the second. The warning is the sharper half: an importance score computed from the *unweighted* read-out is systematically optimistic relative to the same score from the *weighted* one (84.3 versus 49.3 at top-1 on identical images), so when we read a value out of a network and claim it explains the decision, the ranking must use the term that actually enters the decision, not the term that is most legible. Third, the sensitivity-to-threshold rows (13.3 → 33.4 → 59.0 as the scanned region grows from a twelfth to half the image) are the format any locality claim of ours should adopt: an accuracy printed at three region sizes, because a single threshold chooses the answer. Finally, the honest framing to borrow is theirs: an alignment score against independent annotation is a sanity check on the *identification* of a concept, not evidence that the concept explains the behaviour — which is the distinction our own white-box accounts, which do claim to predict, must not blur.
