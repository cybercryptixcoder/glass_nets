# Look Before You Steer: Geometry Predicts SAE Feature Steerability

- **Key:** arxiv-2609.22782
- **Authors / organisation:** Muhammad Khan, Shlok Channawar, Akshaj Gurugubelli, Girish Gupta, Aditya Shah — Algoverse AI Research

- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.22782
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (arXiv HTML v1, 52,417 clean chars / 1,412 lines; abstract, §1–§7.2, Broader Impact, Tables 1–5 read cell by cell, Appendices A–G; Figures 1–3 not inspected as images, only the prose and captions that describe them)
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
SAE feature steering is still tuned by hand: pick a feature from its top-activating tokens, then sweep coefficients until the behaviour moves. That procedure is expensive and, worse for a predictive programme, it is retrospective — nothing is known about a feature's steering cost until the sweep has been run. The authors ask a narrow, answerable version of the prediction question: can properties of the SAE itself, read off the decoder weight matrix before any forward pass, rank features by how much force each one will need to produce a fixed behavioural change. The larger goal is a screening step for controllability, aimed at safety teams who would like to know which features are cheap to push and which are recalcitrant before an intervention is attempted. They are explicit that they are predicting intervention *magnitude* only, and that collateral risk, which is the more interesting predictive target, is deferred to future work.

## What they studied
Four instruction-tuned models with published SAEs at a named layer: Gemma-2-2B-it at layers 20, 22 and 24 and Gemma-2-9B-it at layers 20 and 36 with GemmaScope residual-stream dictionaries (16K, plus 65K at 2B layer 20); Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct at layers 15 and 23 with a 131K BatchTopK dictionary; Qwen3-8B at layer 18 with a 32K BatchTopK dictionary. The unit of analysis is an **SAE feature**, treated as a decoder direction in residual space, and the quantity being predicted is that feature's minimum steering coefficient. The behaviour is refusal, measured on 100 prompts drawn once from SALADBench and frozen, scored by a keyword classifier. Per-condition feature sets of 75 to 300 are cut by a two-stage selection: contrast scoring against neutral text, then a causal-relevance filter that keeps only features whose clamped decoder direction moves the output distribution.

## How they did it
Steerability is defined as the smallest coefficient at which the on-target score rises by a pre-registered threshold above its unsteered value, with features that never reach it within the grid treated as right-censored rather than dropped. The coefficient grid is nine points from 0 to 20, applied as an additive scaled copy of the decoder direction to the residual stream after attention, positive direction only. Two selection stages determine which features are even eligible: a composite contrast score ranking task-versus-neutral activation frequency, then an L1 output-distribution shift at a reference coefficient with a floor of 0.01, which is a much tighter filter than it sounds (100 of 300 survive on Gemma, while all 300 survive on Llama). Three predictors are computed before any intervention: maximum cosine similarity to any other decoder direction, mean cosine to the *k* nearest neighbours at *k* = 50, and a co-activation correlation from forward passes on a 99-prompt reference corpus. A coherence filter removes degenerate completions by three rules (under five tokens, a token type repeated past fifteen, or perplexity above e⁵ under the unsteered model) and roughly a quarter of rows go at layer 20. Analysis is Spearman on log coefficient, ridge regression cross-validated, AUROC for the binary easy-versus-hard split at a coefficient of 3, a 1,000-shuffle permutation null, bootstrap intervals, and leave-one-metric-out ablations. Two conditions are pre-declared not well-identified before analysis, on a censoring-rate and a coherence-collapse criterion, and are reported but excluded from primary inference.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not a mechanism and not a dictionary: the deliverable is a small set of scalars computed from the weight matrix, plus a claim about what they predict. The intended reading is a screening rule — a feature sitting in a dense decoder neighbourhood is cheap to steer because pushing it also pushes its neighbours, and an isolated feature has to be pushed alone. The explanation is deliberately partial, and the paper is unusually clear that it is rank-based: geometry orders features but does not estimate the coefficient, so what is on offer is a priority list, not a calibrated predictor.

## How they judged success
Spearman rank correlation between each pre-computed metric and the log of the minimum coefficient, reported separately for the full sample with censored features rank-tied at the boundary and for the uncensored subset. The binary AUROC at an easy/hard cut. A permutation null that shuffles metric-to-feature assignment a thousand times to fix the chance level of the correlation. Cross-validated ridge R² with leave-one-metric-out drops. Bootstrap intervals, and a Fisher-z approximation for the depth curve. Threshold sensitivity across four values of the behavioural cut without new model runs, and a robustness check dropping the two features sitting at the top of the grid. Controls and baselines: a behaviour-based proxy — the refusal shift at the smallest non-zero coefficient, which costs one steered run rather than a whole sweep — plus the base refusal rate, plus a neighbour-alignment probe intended to test the co-recruitment story. Replication is across two Gemma scales, two dictionary widths, a second architecture, and a second SAE training regime.

## What they found
The best cell is Gemma-2-9B at layer 20, where neighbour density gives a Spearman of −0.546 on the uncensored subset (n = 73, p around 6×10⁻⁷) and maximum cosine gives −0.490; the corresponding AUROC is 0.822. At matched layer index the larger model is markedly stronger than the 2B, where the same metrics sit around −0.288 and −0.299 with AUROC 0.610. The 65K dictionary at 2B layer 20 reproduces the signal at −0.435, which the authors read as evidence that what matters is *relative* crowding rather than absolute cosine, since the wider dictionary has much larger absolute values and comparable effect sizes. Cross-architecture the signal survives but weakens: Llama at layer 15 gives −0.266 for maximum cosine and −0.188 for neighbour density over 300 features, both significant, and the deeper Llama layer attenuates rather than vanishing. A mechanism check finds that easy-to-steer features have neighbours about three times more aligned with the mean refusal direction than hard ones, with that alignment itself correlating at −0.450. Co-activation is null in five of six Gemma conditions, and the one apparently significant cell is shown to be zero-inflation — nine in ten of the features never fire on the reference corpus, so their co-activation is exactly zero by construction, and among the sixteen that do fire the correlation is null. The behaviour-only baseline is the most consequential comparison for us: the refusal shift at the smallest coefficient predicts at −0.339, which is weaker than both geometry metrics in the same condition, but not by much and at a fraction of the cost. Qwen is a genuine boundary case: the full sample shows *positive* correlations of about +0.28 that collapse to roughly +0.01 once censored features are removed, so there geometry separates steerable from unsteerable and says nothing about ordering among the ones that respond. Censoring climbs with depth in both Gemma models and collapses the 2B's deepest layer entirely, which the authors attribute to the target layer's much larger residual norm making the same coefficient roughly two and a half times the applied force.

## Limits and open problems they name
The keyword refusal classifier has precision 0.979 but recall 0.575, so soft refusals are missed and the reported coefficients are overestimates — which the authors use to argue the true correlations are stronger than published, a direction-of-bias argument worth taking seriously but not a measurement. One behaviour only, four models, and the Llama arm is a single run at each of two layers while the Qwen boundary case is not explained, only attributed to BatchTopK training and attention-sink filtering. The fixed coefficient grid makes cross-layer comparison invalid in exactly the way the depth analysis reports: the censoring rate is partly a property of the layer's activation scale, not of the behaviour. Co-activation as operationalised here is unusable. Positive steering only, so nothing is known about suppression. The off-target questions the paper sets up in its own numbering are never measured, so the claim that dense neighbourhoods predict collateral risk is an argument from analogy rather than a result. And the Qwen sign flip is described in the text as an absence of continuous signal while the appendix reports the censored features as sitting at the *high*-geometry end — which is a statement about where the unresponsive features live that the main text does not follow up.

## What we take from it
- **A framing.** The decoder weight matrix is an input to prediction, not just a product of decomposition: neighbour statistics computed with no forward pass carry rank information about how the model will respond to a push. That is a cheap, zero-cost predictor family our white-box model should always include as a baseline, because if a metric computable from the weights alone explains a quarter of intervention cost, any predictive model that does not beat it has explained nothing.
- **A warning, and the sharpest thing in the paper.** The behaviour-only proxy — one steered run at the *smallest* non-zero coefficient — reaches −0.339 against geometry's −0.404 to −0.435 in the same condition. That is one run per feature versus a full nine-point sweep, and it recovers most of the ranking. Any claim that a weight-derived predictor is worth its cost has to be stated as a *marginal* gain over this arm, exactly as run 784's rule requires against a budget-matched baseline; the paper here does include the arm, which is to its credit, and still calls its own signal the dominant one on a gap that is real but small at the 2B scale.
- **A measure worth stealing: right-censoring handled as data.** Features that never reach threshold are not deleted and not scored as failures; they are tied at the top of the ranking, excluded from regression where they would create a ceiling, and their count is reported for every condition. That is the honest treatment of an intervention whose budget ran out, and it is better than the folder's usual habit of reporting a null as a null.
- **A warning about depth.** The depth trend here is confounded by activation norm: the same coefficient is a smaller absolute push at a deeper layer with a larger residual, so the "steering costs more at depth" finding is partly about the grid. Any depth profile our model reports on intervention strength has to normalise the dose, not the coefficient, or it will reproduce a scale effect and call it a boundary.
- **A shortcut for our evaluation battery.** The equal-cost ranking comparison is a cheap first-pass test for any predictor of intervention behaviour, but it is only a ranking test — the paper's own ridge R² is near zero, so this geometry predicts order and not magnitude, and any predictive model we build must be scored on magnitude too or it is doing what this paper already does.
