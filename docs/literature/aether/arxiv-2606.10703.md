# From Observation to Intervention: A Causal Audit of Expert Importance in Mixture-of-Experts Models

- **Key:** arxiv-2606.10703
- **Authors / organisation:** Leonard Engmann; Christian Medeiros Adriano; Holger Giese — Hasso Plattner Institute, University of Potsdam (Germany)
- **Date:** 2026-06-16 (v2; v1 2026-06-09)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.10703
- **Kind:** paper (ICML 2026 PhilML workshop, camera-ready, non-archival)

## What they wanted, and why
Interpretability work routinely reads a statistic computed over observed model behaviour (attention weights, saliency, routing frequencies) and treats it as a prediction of what would happen if you *intervened* on the component it describes. The authors want to test that inferential step rather than assume it. Their chosen case is Mixture-of-Experts pruning, where utilisation rates, activation norms and routing-weight averages are used to decide which experts can be deleted at no cost. In Pearl's hierarchy this is evidence from observing promoted to a claim about doing, one rung up. The larger goal is a discipline for interpretability: replace "the statistic predicts behaviour" with "the statistic predicts the effect of the intervention", and price the difference with a properly powered experiment.

## What they studied
Three high-redundancy sparse MoE language models, chosen to span the design space: OLMoE-1B-7B-0924 (16 layers, 64 experts, top-8, no shared expert), Qwen1.5-MoE-A2.7B (24 layers, 60 routed plus one shared, top-4) and DeepSeek-V2-Lite (27 layers with layer 0 dense, 64 routed plus 2 shared, top-6), with activation rates from 6.7% to 12.5% and load-balancing coefficients spanning an order of magnitude. The unit of analysis is the **expert at a single token position**, and four corpus-level observational metrics are audited: utilisation rate, activation norm, mean routing weight when active, and activation standard deviation.

## How they did it
For a token position, the model routes to a top-k expert set; one of those experts is zeroed out and the loss change at that token is recorded, giving a per-token functional importance for that expert. The protocol then asks the question pruning implicitly asks: at each sampled position, rank the active experts by the observational metric, ablate the top-ranked and the bottom-ranked, and compute the paired difference in loss change. Metric validity is defined as that difference being reliably positive — a valid metric would mean removing low-scored experts preferentially removes cheap ones. They sample n = 200 positions per metric-layer cell, across four metrics and five depth-proportional layers per model (60 cells total), on the WikiText-2 test split, and test the paired difference with a paired t-test (Cohen's d with 95% CI) plus a Wilcoxon signed-rank check, counting any cell where the two disagree as noise; Bonferroni correction is applied per model at 0.05/20 = 0.0025. A control swaps the corpus-level metric for the **per-token** routing weight of the expert at that same position (corrected at 0.05/5 = 0.01), run with identical machinery and identical n, to establish that the design has power. A progressive ablation removes the k highest-weight active experts at a layer for k = 1 to 7 (n = 500 positions, six layers) and tracks cumulative loss change, and a redistribution analysis correlates routing weight with residual-stream displacement and displacement with logit-level compensation across depth. Before data collection each model passes a four-test verification suite: per-token cross-entropy against the HuggingFace reference, no stale state after hooks are cleared, position diversity, and a position-specific ablation effect.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not an explanation of a model, but a **verdict on an inferential licence**: for each statistic, a statement of whether it may be used as a predictor of intervention effects at the token level, plus the evidence class that would have to replace it. The paper's form of understanding is a per-cell table of effect sizes and intervals read as estimates rather than accept/reject decisions, with one arm (the per-token routing weight) serving as the positive control that makes the nulls interpretable. It is deliberately partial — it does not build a replacement predictor, and it says so.

## How they judged success
Cohen's d on the paired difference, 95% noncentral-t CIs cross-checked against a 20,000-sample bootstrap (agreeing to within 0.02 in every cell), paired t-test p-values, Wilcoxon signed-rank p-values, and a dual-test agreement rule under per-model Bonferroni correction. The key design choice is that the **control arm is estimated with identical n**, so the contrast between the null cells and the one surviving control cell is a statement about where effects are centred, not about statistical power. Progressive ablation is reported as mean, median and P95 cumulative loss change in nats.

## What they found
No observational metric reaches corrected significance in any of the 60 cells in any of the three models; effect sizes stay below d = 0.23 throughout, and signs are inconsistent across layers. The cells that exclude zero at the uncorrected 95% level are exactly the p<0.05 cells and none survives correction — Qwen activation std at L23 (+0.222) crosses the corrected t-threshold but is rejected by Wilcoxon. Activation norm, which prior benchmarking had identified as the strongest expert-dropping criterion, never exceeds |d| = 0.157 and never survives. The per-token routing-weight control recovers one signal and only one: OLMoE's final MoE layer, d = +0.231, 95% CI [+0.09, +0.37], p = 0.0013, with the effect growing monotonically with depth and the Spearman correlation between weight ratio and loss difference at +0.199 there; Qwen stays within |d| = 0.124 and DeepSeek within 0.098. Progressive ablation explains why the observational null is compatible with pruning's reported successes: on OLMoE, removing seven of eight active experts at layers 0 through 9 raises mean loss by less than 0.083 nats, while Layer 15 breaks at k = 2 (+0.155 nats) and reaches +0.431 at k = 7 — and even there the median at k = 7 is only +0.018 nats while P95 reaches +2.633, so the cliff is an upper tail on a minority of positions rather than a uniform shift. At early and mid layers the metric and a random rule do the same thing. The redistribution analysis dissociates the two links in the chain: routing weight predicts residual-stream displacement at every layer in both OLMoE and Qwen (Qwen's Spearman runs +0.558 to +0.744), but displacement reaches logit-level compensation from Layer 6 onwards in Qwen and only at Layer 15 in OLMoE — and since Qwen shows comparable displacement scaling (about 38× from L0 to L23) without comparable functional concentration, displacement growth alone is not sufficient to explain the late-layer signal.

## Limits and open problems they name
The effect intervals are wide (half-widths near 0.14 at n = 200), so the claim is that no metric yields a reliably positive token-level effect, not that effects are exactly zero. The audit tests one-expert ablation at one position, whereas deployed pruning makes one-shot global decisions and then fine-tunes, so the token-level null does not falsify the deployment pipeline — it removes the per-token mechanism that pipeline assumes. All three models are high-redundancy (6.7–12.5% activation); whether observational metrics regain validity in low-redundancy architectures such as Switch or Mixtral is explicitly left open. The single OLMoE signal is one model, one layer, and requires the forward pass through that layer, so it is an existence proof of a per-token predictor rather than a deployable metric; three architectures cannot attribute it to any training or design factor, and the authors call for within-architecture checkpoint variation instead. They also note that null-hypothesis testing measures evidence against a metric's validity rather than support for a rival, and suggest Bayesian model comparison as the next step.

## What we take from it
**A measure and a warning.** (1) A copyable validity test for any statistic our white-box model proposes as a predictor: rank units by the candidate statistic *within the context where they are active*, intervene on the top and bottom of the ranking, and require the paired difference to be reliably positive — the same statistic can be a perfect description of the model and a worthless predictor of the intervention. (2) The positive control estimated at matched n is what licenses reading a null as "effects are centred at zero" rather than "we were underpowered"; every null our paper publishes should carry one. (3) The redundancy-buffer result is the sharpest warning: a selection criterion applied in a regime where nearly all selections are harmless will look validated, so an evaluation must put the method where the intervention actually has variance. (4) The two-link dissociation (router tracks displacement everywhere; displacement reaches logits in one model only at the last layer) is a template for localising *where a description stops predicting behaviour* — the same object, tracked honestly, is informative about one intermediate quantity and uninformative about the output.
