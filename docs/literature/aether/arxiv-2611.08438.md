## What they wanted, and why

The paper asks one question and holds to it: does understanding how a model works actually make it safer? The authors' complaint is that the field's central premise — that mechanistic insight into internal computation translates into safety — is largely untested, and that the literature has accumulated methods far faster than evidence that the methods deliver. Their answer is deliberately two-sided: they build a survey of the field's foundations, methods and applications, but they also supply the instrument they think the field lacks, a *single evaluation framework* under which incompatible methods can be scored on the same five axes, plus a taxonomy of six research programmes with different goals and different evidence standards. The larger goal is to convert mechanistic interpretability from a bag of fitted tools into a cumulative science, so that a claim about a component, feature or circuit can be compared with any other claim about the same model.

## What they studied

No new models or experiments — this is a survey. The object of study is the *literature and its evidence practices*: transformer-based autoregressive language models as the default site, with sections on architecture families where the field's methods hold less well (CNNs, state-space and recurrent models such as Mamba and RWKV, diffusion models, mixture-of-experts). Unit of analysis is deliberately plural and is treated as a variable: neurons, features, attention heads, circuits/edges, layers, weights, whole-model behaviour, plus training checkpoints and training data as additional axes. Twenty-one authors from sixteen-odd institutions, five senior advisors, a companion website and a repository of code notebooks reproducing key findings.

## How they did it

The survey is organised as four pillars — motivation and concepts, technical methods, research directions, safety and open problems — and each method family is presented with its assumptions, its characteristic claims and its known failure modes rather than as a list. The methodological novelty is in §7.5, where they define a behaviour $B$ as a set of input prompts, a scalar behaviour-relevant metric $M$, and an intervention on a component set $S$ that replaces its activations with reference activations; the intervention effect $s(S,x)$ is the metric difference between the intact and the intervened run, and $s(S,B)$ its expectation over the behaviour's inputs. Every evaluation dimension is then written as a functional of that pair plus whatever attribution score $a(S,x)$ the method emits. They pair the framework with a per-method table of which dimensions matter most (SAEs, circuit discovery, probing, steering vectors), a five-step integrated protocol, and a second table comparing the six research orientations on focus, key metric, tools, time-to-impact, scalability and robustness to deception. They close by naming open problems, including one — proxy fidelity — that they argue is the load-bearing gap for any dictionary-based claim. I read the framework section, the SAE failure-mode section, the probing section, the circuit-verification section, the six-orientations section, the proxy-fidelity section and the conclusion; no figure was inspected as an image.

## What counts as an explanation here

Explanations are the field's usual objects — feature dictionaries, circuits, directions, steering vectors, probes, natural-language descriptions of intermediate states — but the survey insists on a stricter admission test for what counts as *mechanistic*: engagement with model internals and a causal claim, both required. By that test belief-updating descriptions and probes are explicitly demoted: a probe that only decodes is not mechanistic until its direction is used to intervene. The understanding is admitted as partial by construction, with the stated norm that a circuit typically accounts for 60–80% of behavioural variance rather than all of it. The framework adds a second, orthogonal notion of what an explanation must be *for*: an explanation is scored on whether it predicts behaviour on held-out inputs, whether it accounts for the behaviour's coverage, whether it survives resampling, whether the identified parts are necessary, sufficient and specific, and whether humans can actually use it to predict and debug.

## How they judged success

Five dimensions with named metrics, all defined as functionals of $s(S,x)$ and $a(S,x)$:
- **Attribution faithfulness** — intervention-effect agreement (rank or linear correlation between the method's importance score and the measured intervention effect), effect-size error (mean absolute gap between estimated and measured effect), and counterfactual effect recovery (patching the identified set from an input of a reference behaviour into an input of the target behaviour, normalised by the clean behavioural gap). They note explicitly that the recovery ratio is unbounded — its denominator can approach zero and the patched metric can overshoot the donor or undershoot the recipient — and point at a bounded variant.
- **Completeness** — loss recovery (reconstructed loss minus ablated loss, over original minus ablated, with the ablation baseline named as zero, mean or statistics-matched Gaussian noise), behavioural coverage (fraction of inputs on which the identified set's intervention effect clears a threshold), and unexplained variance (reconstruction error over deviation from the behaviour's mean activation).
- **Stability** — cross-seed agreement (mean pairwise alignment over seeds, with Hungarian matching on pairwise cosine similarity given as one concrete alignment), hyperparameter sensitivity (the range of a metric over a configuration grid divided by its mean), and paraphrase consistency (one minus the within-paraphrase-set variance of the intervention effect divided by its mean square, bounded to [0,1]).
- **Causal validity** — necessity (fraction of behaviour lost when the set is ablated), sufficiency (fraction of behaviour retained when everything outside the set is replaced), and specificity (the set's effect on the target behaviour over the sum of absolute effects across a set of named control behaviours). The protocol requires random components of matched size as the comparison arm for both necessity and sufficiency, and adds an interchange test.
- **Human interpretability** — explanation utility (human prediction accuracy with versus without the explanation), debugging utility (error-fix rate with versus without), and inter-rater agreement (Cohen's kappa between human explanations).
Benchmarks are named as the field's existing causal-validity instruments: MIB for circuits, AxBench for steering, RAVEL for interchange-validated localisation, SAEBench for SAEs. The framework's own limitations are listed rather than hidden: metric choice is not uniquely correct, thresholds are arbitrary and need calibration, the full protocol is expensive, the human dimension is a bottleneck, and a method's score depends on the task.

## What they found

The survey's substantive findings are mostly negative and are what make it worth an entry. On SAEs: dead features can reach 10–50% of the dictionary in a poorly tuned run; absorption is presented as the failure mode where a general "starts with S" feature goes silent on a token because a more specific child feature absorbed part of its direction, detectable by comparing the feature against a linear probe for the same concept and checking whether the silent case's active feature aligns with the probe; and on instability, for a fixed architecture and dataset different seeds yield substantially different dictionaries, with **fewer than 50% of features remaining the same across seeds** while broader semantic clusters hold up better — so they recommend reporting seed stability beside reconstruction loss. On identifiability they are blunt: classical dictionary learning needs low coherence, LLM activations are likely to have high coherence, and multiple distinct sparse combinations can produce the same output, so a dictionary should be framed as one valid approximate decomposition, not the true features. On circuits: strict necessity and sufficiency are usually unattainable because behaviours depend on distributed and redundant pathways, and a discovered circuit typically explains 60–80% of behavioural variance. On probes they give the split that this folder keeps rediscovering — decoding is not using, a probe can succeed on spuriously correlated features, a complex probe can fit random structure, and a probe is best treated as a hypothesis generator; they pair this with the one case where it still works, the sleeper-agent detector, whose contrastive mean-difference direction over prompts unrelated to the trigger reaches above 99% AUROC, and they are explicit that this is a detection result (information presence) rather than a mechanistic one. On safety applications they separate localisation from control: knowing where something is represented does not imply reliable control, and the hydra effect is named as the reason. Their most pointed analytical contribution is the **proxy fidelity problem**: a dictionary or transcoder is a surrogate whose agreement is measured on *output* while its claims are about *internal* algorithm, so absence of a deception circuit in the proxy licenses only the sentence that it was not found in the proxy — and they propose interchange interventions against the proxy's features as the way to test whether it is a valid causal abstraction of the target. Finally, their taxonomy of six orientations — pragmatic, ambitious, developmental, interpretability-across-time, introspective, automated — is scored on scalability and robustness to deception in a table, with introspective and automated rated *low* on robustness to deception and developmental and across-time marked unknown.

## Limits and open problems they name

The framework is the paper's own weakest point and they say so: no benchmark suite yet exists for it, comparison across methods with different goals is unsolved, and whether mechanistic explanations admit objective ground truth is called open. They also name the ontology question — several mechanisms can explain one behaviour, the level of abstraction is a subjective choice, and a "borzoi detector" label is a human convenience — and the incompleteness problem, which they tie to a lossy map from distributed low-level computation onto a human-level causal model whose discard rate is unknown and, circularly, hard to estimate because the thing you would measure against is the opaque model itself. Adversarial robustness gets a paragraph: mechanistic tools published and deployed create an incentive for models to spread computation to resist analysis, an interpretability arms race with no endpoint. Transferability beyond transformers is marked as unresolved, with MoE routing flagged as a specific technical obstacle — patching an intermediate MoE layer can change which experts downstream selects, so the patched activation's effect is confounded with redirection of routing. The survey is also, by its own framing, an agenda rather than evidence: its claims that mechanistic understanding is under-tested are asserted from the shape of the literature, not measured.

## What we take from it

A **measure** and a **reporting discipline**, mainly. Four things port directly into our battery. (1) The five dimensions are a checklist we can apply to any claim we make, and two of them we do not currently report consistently: the specificity score (an intervention's effect on named control behaviours, normalised by the sum over that set) and paraphrase consistency of the intervention effect, which is the cheapest available test that our effect is a property of the behaviour rather than of one prompt. (2) The unboundedness warning on our favourite numbers: counterfactual recovery and loss recovery both have degenerate denominators and can leave [0,1], so any headline we print from either needs a bounded variant or an explicit note — this matches what this folder has measured several times and usually attributed to the method rather than to the metric. (3) The proxy fidelity framing is the cleanest statement we have of a problem our own white-box ambition inherits: a white-box model is a proxy, agreement on outputs does not license claims about internals, and interchange interventions on the proxy's own units is the test that would. (4) The seed-instability figure (<50% of features matching across seeds) is the number to quote against any single-dictionary feature claim, including ours. As a **warning**, the paper's demotion of pure probing to non-mechanistic status is the same partition this folder has built empirically across ~40 entries, arriving here as an editorial stance with a citation trail — useful for our write-up, and a reminder that "we decoded X" is a first sentence and never a conclusion.
