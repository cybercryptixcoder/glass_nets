# Deep Linear Probe Generators for Weight Space Learning

- **Key:** arxiv-2410.10811
- **Authors / organisation:** Jonathan Kahana, Eliahu Horwitz, Imri Shuval, Yedid Hoshen — School of Computer Science and Engineering, The Hebrew University of Jerusalem, Israel (correspondence to Kahana)
- **Date:** 2025-10-22 (v2; v1 2024-10-14)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.10811
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Model hubs now host more networks than anyone can document, and a checkpoint's training set, its generalisation error, or the nature of a fine-tune are frequently unrecorded. The authors want to predict such attributes from a network alone. They frame the problem by analogy with binary analysis: reading the artefact directly is the "static" route, executing it on chosen inputs and watching the responses is the "dynamic" route, and in reverse engineering the dynamic route usually wins. Interpreting a network by running probes through it is exactly the dynamic route, so they set out to make probing — largely a side-channel in prior weight-space work — into a first-class method. The larger goal is a cheap, architecture-agnostic read-out of untrusted checkpoints.

## What they studied
Unit of analysis: whole model, represented by its output vector on a small fixed set of learned inputs (k = 16 up to 128 probes; 2,048 only for one point-cloud comparison). Substrates: implicit neural representations, one per MNIST and one per Fashion-MNIST image (predict the source image's class), small CNNs of 3-5 layers trained separately on CIFAR-10 for generalisation-error regression (the CIFAR10-GS and CIFAR10 "Wild Park" zoos), a newly built zoo of more than 6,000 ResNet-18 models each trained on a random one of ten Tiny-ImageNet subsets, and ShapeNet point-cloud INRs. Nothing here is a language model; the models are small and the labels are known by construction.

## How they did it
Probing represents a model by concatenating f(p_1)…f(p_k) over k learned inputs and training a classifier on that vector. They first reproduce vanilla probing over latent optimisation of each probe and compare against a statistics-based baseline (per-layer weight statistics with gradient boosting), two equivariant "deep weight space" architectures, a scale-equivariant graph metanetwork, and Neural Graphs (a transformer over the neuron graph that also ingests probing features). Then the key diagnostic: replace the learned probes with probes that are never trained — random synthetic images from the Dead Leaves generator, or real in-distribution images — and retrain only the classifier. The fix is ProbeGen: factor each probe as p_i = G(z_i) with a per-probe latent code and one shared generator G, and make G a stack of linear layers with no activations between them (a deep linear network, chosen for its known implicit regularisation), with transposed-convolution layers for image inputs to inject locality. Ablations replace the generator's conv layers with fully connected layers at matched feature-map size, re-insert activations, vary depth from one to five layers, compare against L2 weight decay on the generator from 1e-2 down to 1e-8, and sweep probe count. They also print FLOPs for 128 probes at batch size 64.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not a mechanistic account: a *read-out* — a fixed probe set plus a small classifier that maps a model's responses to an attribute. It is predictive by construction (the deliverable is an accuracy or a rank correlation on held-out models) and it is deliberately black-box-compatible: only input/output behaviour of the target is needed, so it can be applied through an API. It says nothing about layers or weights, which the authors concede excludes weight-generation tasks. The probing point of view inverts the usual feature-probe programme: here the probe is a chosen *input to the studied network*, not a direction read from its internals.

## How they judged success
Accuracy for class prediction and Kendall's tau for generalisation-error regression, averaged over five seeds with standard deviations, against five published baselines on four standard zoos; a new 6,000-model ResNet-18 zoo as a scale test where graph methods are too expensive to run; FLOP counts as a cost measure; and the learned-vs-unlearned probe substitution as the core diagnostic. Generalisation gap (train minus test) is the overfitting measure in the generator ablations. No human rating, no intervention on the target network.

## What they found
Vanilla probing already beats the statistics baseline by a wide margin and matches the equivariant and graph baselines with 128 probes (MNIST 0.955 accuracy; CIFAR10-GS 0.936; Wild Park 0.889 tau), so the premise they start from — that probing, done with care, is competitive — is supported before any of their method exists. The diagnostic then undercuts it: probes that were never learned score the same or better — synthetic probes 0.959 / 0.856 / 0.917 / 0.893 at 128 pits against learned 0.955 / 0.808 / 0.936 / 0.889, and in-distribution probes reach 0.941 and 0.930 on CIFAR10-GS and Wild Park. Learned probes, visualised, look like low-level adversarial texture, which the authors read as overfitting. ProbeGen reaches 0.984 MNIST, 0.877 FMNIST, 0.957 CIFAR10-GS and 0.933 Wild Park at 128 probes (best baseline at the same budget: 0.976 / 0.745 / 0.938 / 0.885), reaches 0.910 tau on the ResNet-18 zoo against 0.856 for statistics and 0.843 for vanilla probing, uses 0.02 vs 63.40 and 3.41 vs 94.56 billion FLOPs on MNIST and CIFAR10-GS, and with 32 probes already surpasses 128 vanilla probes on FMNIST (0.844 vs 0.808) and 2,048 vanilla probes on point clouds. Removing activations from the generator improves every task at every depth; the conv generator beats a matched fully connected one; and ProbeGen at 0.984 / 0.877 / 0.957 / 0.932 edges out the best L2 regularisation at 1e-7-1e-8 (0.982 / 0.870 / 0.947 / 0.928) without per-task tuning. A classifier using only the entropy of each probe's output distribution still reaches 0.877 tau on Wild Park, so much of the signal is a confidence statistic.

## Limits and open problems they name
The input and output spaces must mean the same thing across compared models — a zoo with different class sets, or the same classes in different orders, breaks the method outright. Because it only observes input/output behaviour it cannot give layer- or weight-level predictions and cannot be used for weight generation or editing. Inference still requires forwarding the full model with gradients for many models per batch, i.e. compute comparable to training one, so CLIP- or Stable Diffusion-scale targets are out of reach. Other modalities likely need different structural priors, which they only sketch. They propose adaptive, model-specific probe selection as future work.

## What we take from it
A method, a control and a warning. The **control** is the unlearned-probe arm: any probing read-out we build should be compared against a matched-size set of random or in-distribution probes with the same classifier — if learned probes do not beat random ones, the learning is decoration and the signal is in the classifier or in the network's own output statistics. That is the cheapest null in the whole probing line and it is exact for us, because our probe sets are also inputs we chose. The **method** is to make probes structured by construction — shared generator, per-probe code, an inductive bias matched to the input domain — which buys a large accuracy gain at a tenth to a thousandth of the compute of graph metanets, and which argues that probe *set structure*, not probe count, is the resource. The **warning** is the entropy result (0.877 tau from per-probe output entropy alone): a representation that looks like a rich fingerprint can be explained by a scalar confidence summary, so any claim that our probe vector carries specific content must be priced against a one-number-per-probe baseline. This is the dynamic twin of the weight-side entries: same target (predict an undocumented model's properties), opposite access (behaviour vs parameters), and the paper is explicit that the dynamic route is cheap and blind to internals while the static route is informative and expensive.
