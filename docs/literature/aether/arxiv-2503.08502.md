## What they wanted, and why
A companion to the earlier "On Space Folds of ReLU Neural Networks" (TMLR 2025, Lewandowski et al.), which first defined a folding measure and reported that folding rises with depth in well-generalising networks and falls when the error rises. This paper is the theory-and-tooling follow-up: make the measure well-behaved, make it apply to activations other than ReLU, make it cheap enough to compute on real datasets, and sketch a way to regularise with it. The larger goal is to have a geometric quantity computed from a network's own activations that indicates generalisation capacity — ideally one usable as a training signal, not only as a post-hoc diagnostic.

## What they studied
Small ReLU MLPs (the earlier paper's setting: networks totalling 60 and 600 hidden neurons at fixed depth) and, via the generalisation, any feedforward network with a monotone element-wise activation — GELU, Swish, SwiGLU are named. The unit of analysis is a **path**: a straight line between two input points, mapped through the network to a sequence of binarised activation patterns (one bit per hidden neuron, positive vs non-positive). The objects measured are Hamming distances between those patterns, not weights, neurons or directions.

## How they did it
Sample two inputs, walk the straight segment between them in small steps, and at each point binarise the concatenated hidden activations to get a pattern π. Then compute two numbers: r₁, the largest Hamming distance reached from the start pattern π₁, and r₂, the total Hamming distance travelled along the walk. The folding measure is χ = 1 − r₁/r₂, so a flag means the path travelled far in the Hamming cube without ever getting far from where it started — in a straight-line input walk that can only happen if the network is folding the space. They then prove four properties: (i) extra steps inside one region leave χ unchanged, so the measure depends on which regions you visit, not how often; (ii) χ = 0 exactly when distance-from-start never decreases along the path; (iii) χ is direction-sensitive, since reversing a path can lower r₁ without changing r₂; (iv) but flatness (χ = 0) is direction-invariant. They show χ is neither sub- nor super-additive with explicit three-bit counterexamples, yet report observing only sub-additivity in trained networks, and define an interaction term |χ(Γ₁⊕Γ₂) − χ(Γ₁) − χ(Γ₂)| to quantify the deviation. For a dataset they generalise to a global measure Φ: per class pair, take the median of non-zero χ over sample pairs, and average over all ordered class pairs; MNIST-scale pairwise computation is dismissed as factorial, so the global value is estimated on samples. The ReLU-only structure is dropped by observing that the linear regions are the equivalence classes of "same binarised pattern", which is still a well-defined partition for any monotone activation, so the same walk measures a walk through equivalence classes. Their sampling algorithm replaces the earlier equal spacing: step forward, halve the step whenever the Hamming distance jumps by more than one bit, accept the step when it is exactly one bit or when the step floor is reached. Finally they propose adding λ/(Φ+1)² to the loss, with the max inside r₁ replaced by a log-sum-exp so it can be backpropagated.

## What counts as an explanation here
A scalar summary statistic of a network's geometry — one number per path, one number per model — plus a set of proved invariance properties that say what that number does and does not depend on. It is deliberately partial: it says nothing about which neuron or region produces the fold, and nothing about what the folded regions mean. The one prediction the programme claims is directional and relates to training rather than to a single forward pass: more folding goes with better generalisation.

## How they judged success
By proof, not by benchmark. Every success claim is a proposition with a proof (stability under repeated regions, iff-condition for flatness, asymmetry with a worked three-pattern example, flatness invariance), plus concrete counterexamples showing non-additivity in both directions. The only statistical test in the paper is a Mann–Whitney comparison of intra-class against inter-class median folding values on networks with low generalisation error, reported qualitatively as significant, with thresholding referred to Cohen's effect-size conventions. There is **no comparison against a baseline measure, no ablation, no accuracy table, no seed count, and no experiment described anywhere in the paper**.

## What they found
All the results are structural. Repeated traversal of one region cannot inflate the score; flatness has an exact iff-characterisation and is invariant to walking the path backwards; the score itself is not, and the paper's own example makes the asymmetry concrete (a three-pattern path scores differently from its reverse). Additivity fails in both directions, which means the measure cannot be decomposed across sub-paths — the authors note that in trained networks they have only seen the sub-additive case, with the super-additive one occurring in a synthetic sandbox network built for the purpose. The claimed empirical facts — depth-dependence, the low-error/high-folding association, and the near-invariance of folding to network width once training is good — are all reported as observations from the earlier companion paper and were not re-measured here. The extension beyond ReLU is argued, not tested.

## Limits and open problems they name
Three are listed explicitly in §7: the regularisation scheme is **untested** and would in its current form require stopping training every n epochs to recompute Φ; behaviour under non-ReLU architectures "necessitates further investigation"; and the interaction term is defined but never used empirically, with adversarial attacks named as the intended use. A limit they do not list is more important: Φ is defined over medians of pairwise path scores on sampled pairs, and the paper's assumption that this converges to an architecture-level constant rests entirely on the companion paper's observation rather than on any argument or measurement here. The sampling algorithm's floor Δmin is admitted to be the delicate parameter — adjacent regions can differ by more than one bit, in which case the algorithm accepts a multi-bit jump and the walk silently merges regions.

## What we take from it
Three things, in order of usefulness. **First, a measurement discipline we should copy: every explanation they propose is checked for invariance before it is checked for diagnostic value.** They ask what a statistic does not depend on — repeated visits, the direction of the walk, sub-path decomposition — and prove the answer before anyone uses the number. That is the cheapest form of the control this folder has been demanding for every projection and rank statistic since run 722, and it costs pen and paper rather than compute. Mechanical rule for us: **before reporting any statistic in our white-box model, we write down the transformations under which it should be invariant and prove or measure invariance under each — the order of a path, the number of repeated samples inside one region, the decomposition of an input into sub-inputs — because a statistic whose invariances are unknown cannot be compared across two models, and a comparison is the only thing we will ever want it for.** **Second, a warning about direction-dependence with a concrete form.** Their score is asymmetric on the same set of points, so any directional statistic of ours (a walk along a path, an attribution from one input to another, a boundary crossing counted one way) is a different number reversed, and the paper shows this can be strictly larger than a rounding difference. **Third, the honest shape of a workshop-theory paper: the untested regulariser is not a weakness we should imitate.** Adding a geometric term to the loss whose effect on accuracy was never measured is exactly the failure mode that runs 718–726 kept flagging, and the paper at least says so in its own future-work list. Note also, for provenance, that the empirical half of the whole folding programme lives in a paper not in this folder — the TMLR companion is cited without an arXiv id — so nothing here should be quoted as evidence about trained networks.
