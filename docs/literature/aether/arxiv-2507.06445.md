## What they wanted, and why

Interpretability is normally graded by whether it predicts the effect of a *mechanistic intervention* — ablate this head, patch this site, steer along this direction. The authors argue that this is a narrower ambition than scientific understanding usually admits, and propose a different test: can internals observed on in-distribution data predict how the model will behave on **unseen inputs**, i.e. under distribution shift? They motivate this as the classic scientific criterion (a theory earns its keep by predicting behaviour in conditions it was not fitted to), as what practitioners actually need (engineering tolerances — a statement of where the instrument fails), and as a way round a debate they consider stuck, since causal faithfulness claims are contested and frequently fail to transfer. The philosophical commitment is explicitly instrumentalist: internals are read as traces of the algorithm rather than as the causal machinery implementing it, so a description can be predictive and simultaneously un-faithful. A secondary target is the causal standard itself — they want to show that ablation can be actively misleading about predictive value.

## What they studied

Two populations of tiny transformers with perfect in-distribution (ID) accuracy and deliberately varied out-of-distribution (OOD) behaviour. Primary setting: 270 minGPT classifiers (hidden width 64, depths 1–3, widths 1–3 heads per layer; 53K / 103K / 153K parameters) on a Dyck-1 parentheses task whose training set is consistent with two different rules at once. Secondary setting: 79 decoder-only autoregressive transformers (6 layers, 8 heads, model dimension 512, feed-forward 2048, ~54M parameters) on English yes/no question formation. Unit of analysis: **attention heads and their activation patterns at the readout position** (the EOS token for classifiers, the `quest` token for question formation), treated as population-level features.

## How they did it

The data trick is what makes the question askable. ID examples satisfy both candidate rules or neither; an OOD test set satisfies the linear rule but not the hierarchical one, so a model's OOD accuracy is a clean readout of which rule it implements. In Dyck-1 the two rules are an unordered **Equal-Count** (equal opens and closes) and a **Nested** rule (a valid bracketed tree); the OOD set contains equal-count-but-not-nested strings, and accuracy is scored against Nested, so 0% means "pure Equal-Count" and 100% means "Nested". In question formation the same structure appears as "move the first auxiliary" (linear) versus "move the matrix auxiliary" (hierarchical), with ID sentences built so both rules agree. They train a grid over depth, width, weight decay and seeds, t-SNE the models by their OOD output probabilities, and find clusters corresponding to rules rather than a continuum. They then define a head-level feature: a head is **hierarchical on a dataset** if at the readout position it persistently favours either violation or non-violation tokens on at least 80% of the sequences that contain both. Two subtypes are distinguished — violation-detecting heads attend to tokens that break the nesting condition, sign-matching heads attend to violations only when the final token itself is a violation and to non-violations otherwise. This feature is computed **only on ID data** and used to predict the model's OOD rule, first by thresholding and then as a classifier: a five-statistic random forest on EOS attention numbers (centre of mass, mass on the last tenth, largest weight, weight on EOS, mean self-attention; summarised by max/mean/min over heads) and a hyperparameters-only logistic regression serve as comparators. Held-out evaluation is run two ways — 50 random 150/120 model splits, and a harder split that holds out an entire (depth, weight decay) setting so the predictor must extrapolate. Finally, for causality they apply uniform attention ablation (every head forced to attend equally over unmasked positions) to all heads at once, with one-head-at-a-time and mean ablation variants in the appendix, and measure the change in OOD accuracy.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **population-level correlational signature plus an intervention**: the explanation is the inferred algorithm (which of the two rules the model is executing) and the head-level attention pattern that hints at it. Nothing is written down as a circuit, a dictionary, or a program. The claim is deliberately partial and instrumentalist — the authors say the pattern may be a spandrel, a vestigial training artefact, or a malfunctioning module, and that any of these still licenses a prediction about unseen inputs. What it is meant to predict: the model's behaviour on a distribution it was never evaluated on, and, in the held-out-hyperparameter split, the behaviour of *models it has never seen*. Faithfulness is explicitly decoupled from predictive value, and the paper's headline is that the two came apart.

## How they judged success

Four measures, all on populations rather than single models: (i) whether independently trained models cluster categorically by rule (t-SNE visualisation, plus the exact counts of models at each rule); (ii) whether carrying an ID hierarchical head separates OOD accuracy distributions (Mann–Whitney U across depth × weight-decay subgroups, median comparisons, and a precision/recall/F1 framing of "has the head" as a binary classifier); (iii) held-out predictive AUROC for the head rule, the five-statistic forest, and the hyperparameter baseline, on random splits and on the leave-one-setting-out split, with chance marked; (iv) causal ablation, scored as change in OOD accuracy and separately as change in ID accuracy, with mean and uniform ablation and single-head variants as robustness. Two controls do real work: the hyperparameter baseline (which beats internals on random splits and collapses to chance under setting hold-out) and the ID-accuracy column of the ablation (which barely moves while OOD accuracy moves a lot).

## What they found

- Rule choice is categorical, not graded: models land near 0% or high OOD accuracy, with a third cluster — a **First-Symbol** heuristic (label True iff the first symbol is an open paren, which scores 55% OOD) — appearing only in 1-layer unregularised models. 1-layer models learn only First-Symbol or Equal-Count; 2-layer models are the most hierarchical (2% Equal-Count-leaning); 3-layer models are the most variable. 24.4% of the transformer population learns pure Equal-Count, 10.4% reaches at least 90% OOD accuracy, and only three models hit 100%.
- Width is inert (no significant effect across depths); depth and weight decay are the dominant hyperparameters, with weight decay pushing 1-layer models to Equal-Count (60% of unregularised 1-layer models instead pick First-Symbol) and making 2-layer models more reliably Nested.
- Transient heuristics: unregularised 1-layer models pass through phases where their OOD accuracy sits at the First-Symbol level before settling, which they read as a vestigial circuit that no longer affects ID behaviour.
- **ID hierarchical heads predict the OOD rule.** Heads tracked on ID data keep behaving as hierarchical heads OOD 77% of the time. Among models with any ID hierarchical head, 81% have sign-matching heads only, 9% violation-detecting only, 8% both, 2% neither subtype.
- Held-out AUROC (predicting membership in the Nested region from ID information only): random splits give 0.85 for the head rule and 0.88 for the five attention statistics, while hyperparameters alone do better; under leave-one-setting-out, hyperparameters fall to 0.54 (chance) while the head rule and attention statistics retain predictive power. The question-formation transfer gives 0.76 for the Main Auxiliary-Detecting head and 0.72 for the same five statistics, 5-fold cross-validated. Hierarchical heads never appear in a first layer, which the authors offer as a partial explanation for why 1-layer models cannot learn Nested.
- Within fixed hyperparameter settings that produce diverse OOD behaviour, models without hierarchical heads have a median OOD accuracy at or below the bottom quartile of models with them (significant by Mann–Whitney across four of the sub-populations).
- **The causal decoupling.** Uniform attention ablation damages OOD accuracy in models carrying violation-detecting heads but **improves** it in models carrying sign-matching heads — the same correlate, opposite causal sign. ID validation accuracy barely moves under the same ablation (mean drop 0.6%; maximum 77/1000 for any head type, median around 10/1000), and the per-model effects of ablation on ID and OOD accuracy are only weakly correlated. The qualitative pattern survives one-head-at-a-time and mean ablation.
- In the question-formation setting the same ablation leaves OOD behaviour essentially unchanged (mean effect within a percentage point of zero under both ablation types, ID accuracy intact), so the authors call the Dyck decoupling a proof of existence rather than a general law.
- Regularisation *promotes* the sign-matching heads, so they reject the vestigial explanation for them and conjecture spandrel or OOD malfunction instead.
- Random seed and data order matter as much as each other: in a plurality of runs OOD accuracy differs by at least 10 points across seeds, and the maximum gap exceeds 90 points.

## Limits and open problems they name

Synthetic tasks with a known ground truth for the rule and hundreds of models to form a population — conditions that do not hold for real models, where the OOD rule is unknown and where scale dilutes the role of individual heads. The paper is a proof-of-concept for an objective, not a method that transfers yet. They also flag that rule selection depends on hyperparameters in ways that defeat "the simplest rule wins" narratives (the same regulariser favours different rules at different depths), that a mechanism can behave very differently OOD (a quarter of ID sign-matching heads switch behaviour OOD, which they say makes it hard to describe any mechanism in a way that holds across domains), and that causal roles here are data-dependent — so a negative ablation result is weak evidence against a mechanism. Whether representational geometry gives usable advance warning in larger models is left open.

## What we take from it

- **A framing and an eval we should own outright.** Our white-box model's deliverable is a prediction; the natural test is behaviour on inputs the predictor was not fitted to, scored against the rules the model could be implementing. This paper is the folder's cleanest instance of "predict behaviour, not intervention effect" and supplies the scaffold: an ID-ambiguous task, an OOD set that separates the candidate rules, and a population of models rather than one.
- **A baseline we would otherwise skip.** Hyperparameters predict almost as well as internals on random splits and collapse to chance when a setting is held out — so any claim of ours that internals beat a cheap non-internal predictor must be evaluated with the analogous split held out, not on random rows.
- **A warning that inverts a habit of this folder.** A feature can be a strong, stable correlate of the behaviour and still push *against* it under intervention; and an ablation that leaves ID performance intact says nothing about the mechanism's importance on the edge cases that matter. Every intervention result we publish should carry the OOD column beside the ID column, and we should stop treating a null ablation as evidence that a component is not the mechanism.
- **A concrete cheap feature family** (five readout-position attention statistics → random forest) that we can compute on any transformer we probe, to see whether structural signals predict behavioural strata before we build anything heavier.
- **A pattern to compare against later:** predictive-but-not-causal is a real epistemic position, not sloppiness, and the paper's own philosophical defence of it is worth reusing when someone asks why our predictor is allowed to be right for the wrong reason.
