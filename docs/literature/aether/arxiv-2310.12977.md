## What they wanted, and why

Almost everything known about how a deep network changes during training is read off the loss curve, and the loss curve is a function of the data, the labels and the objective. The authors want a statistic that is a function of the network's parameters and partition alone - no labels, no task, no loss - yet still shows the phases that the loss curve shows. The bet is that the input-space partition of a piecewise-affine network is the right object, because it determines what the network can do at every point and is exactly the thing that has been treated as stationary in prior work. So the paper is two questions in one: is there a cheap partition statistic, and does it do anything informative over training? The larger goal, stated in the introduction, is to find parameter-only statistics that carry information about the learned mapping, which they frame as a largely unexplored way to understand training.

## What they studied

Fully connected ReLU networks on 1K MNIST samples (depth 2-5, width 200 for the depth sweep; depth 3, width 20-2000 for the width sweep), a CNN and a ResNet18 on CIFAR10 with varying depth and with and without batch normalisation, and the same MNIST MLPs with weights scaled by 8 at initialisation to induce grokking. The unit of analysis is the **input-space partition** - regions on which the whole network is affine - and the measured quantity is local partition density: how many of each layer's neuron hyperplanes cut a small neighbourhood around a probe point. Probes are drawn from three sets kept separate throughout: training samples, test samples, and points sampled uniformly from the input domain.

## How they did it

For each probe point x they draw P orthonormal vectors and take the convex hull of the two-sided offsets, giving a P-dimensional neighbourhood of controlled diagonal; for the MLP and CNN runs P = 25, for ResNet18 P = 20. They forward-pass the neighbourhood's vertices once and, at each layer, count neuron units whose pre-activation does not share a sign across all vertices - i.e. hyperplanes that cut the hull - and sum that count over layers. Because the vertices are pushed forward with the forwarding, deeper layers' counts are of hyperplanes cutting the *embedded* hull, not the input-space hull, a point the paper states rather than hides. They then train the model while recomputing this statistic at checkpoints and plot it alongside train and test accuracy, using a hypercube neighbourhood swept between fine (small r) and coarse (large r) scales. They validate the approximation against SplineCAM, an exact region-count method, on a trained depth-3 width-200 MLP at several checkpoints over 500 samples, comparing a 2-D slice count at radius r against the cheap method's count on a 25-dimensional neighbourhood of matching diagonal. With that instrument they run one factor at a time: depth, width, batch norm, weight decay, training-set size with clean labels and with randomised labels, and the scaled-initialisation grokking setting. LC is computed on training, 10k test and 10k random locations for MNIST, 2K for the CNN and 1K for the ResNet, with error bands of a quarter of the standard deviation.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **trajectory of a geometric quantity, annotated with pictures of the partition itself**. The claim is that the network passes through three LC phases - an initial descent, an ascent, then a final descent - and that what the network is really doing during the last phase is moving its region boundaries off the data and onto the decision boundary, so the mapping becomes nearly linear wherever the samples are. The explanation is a mechanism (migration) supported by exact slice visualisations of the partition through three training samples, plus the co-timing of LC phases with memorisation and generalisation. It is explicitly partial: the paper offers no theory of why the phases occur in this order or why the boundaries end up where they do, and the Conclusion states that these dynamics remain unexplained.

## How they judged success

Four criteria, all comparative and none quantitative in the statistical sense. **(1) Agreement with an independent exact method:** the LC curve and SplineCAM's exact region count both show the same double-descent shape on the validation MLP, and the paper argues the cheap method is far faster besides. **(2) Co-timing with behaviour:** accuracy peaks during the first descent, memorisation happens in the ascent, and generalisation begins in the second descent, checked across the depth sweep, the grokking runs and the two dataset-size sweeps. **(3) Consistency across independent manipulations:** regularisers, overparameterisation and data composition each change the LC curve in the direction the migration story predicts. **(4) The random-point curve** as a control on the locality claim: LC at uniformly sampled points tracks the test curve rather than the train curve, so the train-side ascent is a data-proximity effect rather than a global change in density. There is no correlation coefficient, no lead-time number, no held-out scoring rule, and no baseline statistic of comparable cost for comparison.

## What they found

The three-phase trajectory holds across all their architectures: an initial descent after initialisation, an ascent to a peak, then a final descent; and the fine and coarse radius views agree on its shape for the deeper networks. In the MLP runs the ascent is dominated by the **training** probe set - test and random LC barely move - and increasing depth reduces both the size and the sharpness of that gap, while the shallowest network keeps a coarse-scale second descent that the finer scale does not show, which the authors read as regions close to the data being retained. Width behaves non-monotonically: from 20 to 1000 increasing width makes the ascent start earlier and peak higher, but going to 2000 lowers the peak, which they interpret as overparameterisation acting as an implicit regulariser by thinning the crowding of boundaries near the training samples. CNNs on CIFAR10 tell a different story from the MLPs: train and test LC are nearly indistinguishable throughout, depth *increases* the ascent peak rather than reducing it, and the added density is spread over the data manifold rather than concentrated at training points. Batch norm removes the final descent while raising overall LC for train, test and random points alike - consistent with the paper's citation of a result that BN explicitly places boundaries close to the training data - and weight decay also delays or removes the final descent but, unlike BN, shortens the ascent and lowers its peak. With grokking induced, memorisation occupies the ascent and generalisation begins at the final descent, and there is a second, smaller ascent right before grokking; increasing depth raises the ascent peak in that setting (the reverse of the ordinary case) and delays generalisation. Dataset size moves the curve in the expected direction in both regimes: more clean-labelled samples remove the ascent peak, more randomly labelled samples lengthen it and raise both train and test peaks. Layerwise, shallow layers carry the sharper ascent peak and the larger train-test gap; in deeper layers that gap is negligible, and in shallow ResNet blocks the second descent alternates between the two ReLU stages within a residual block.

## Limits and open problems they name

No theory: the Conclusion says the phases remain unexplained and that the observation rebuts work that assumed a stationary partition without supplying a replacement model. The measure is an approximation whose absolute value depends on the neighbourhood's dimension and size, it counts hyperplanes rather than regions (so double counting within a layer is possible), and the paper never reports how the LC value scales with r or P - it uses fine and coarse settings as a qualitative contrast instead. The migration claim is established by exact visualisation on selected low-dimensional slices through a few samples, not by a distribution-level boundary-distance statistic. The code is promised on release and is not in the version read. The relation between LC dynamics and membership-inference fragility is raised as a speculation, not tested. Everything is small-scale: MNIST MLPs, one CNN, one ResNet18.

## What we take from it

**A method with a price tag, and the price is where the method is weak:** the single forward pass over the hull's vertices is the cheapest partition statistic in this folder's reach, and it is deliberately label-free - which means it can be computed on any checkpoint of any model without a task. For our white-box model that is exactly the shape of quantity we want: read the weights, compute a geometric summary, predict something about behaviour. **The reusable discipline is the three probe sets** - training, test, and uniformly random - with the random curve used to separate "the partition reorganised near the data" from "the partition changed everywhere"; the paper's central mechanism is only readable because those curves disagree. **A framing to adopt carefully:** LC as a phase indicator across checkpoints, which makes it a trajectory statistic rather than a probe, so our own reporting should keep the per-checkpoint curve rather than a final value. **A warning to carry:** the strongest claims (migration to the decision boundary, the second ascent before grokking) are demonstrated in annotated pictures and a handful of slice counts, and the paper itself reports that the same manipulation - depth - moves the ascent peak in *opposite* directions on MNIST MLPs and on CIFAR10 CNNs, so the three-phase shape is robust across settings while any specific direction of effect is not. **And a gap to aim at:** nothing here predicts a behavioural quantity from the LC value; the statistic is computed, plotted and read, so this is the phenomenon stated in its cleanest form, not the predictive test the folder keeps naming.
