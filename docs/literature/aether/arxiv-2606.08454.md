# Beyond Linear Activation Steering: Invertible Latent Transformations for Controlling LLM Behavior

- **Key:** arxiv-2606.08454
- **Authors / organisation:** Tuc Nguyen; Thai Le — Indiana University Bloomington
- **Date:** 2026-06-07 (v1); v2 2026-11-11
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.08454
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (arXiv HTML v2, licensed CC BY-NC-SA 4.0)
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

Every mainstream steering method — CAA, RepE, ITI — assumes that the move from one behaviour to another is one fixed displacement, identical for every input. The authors argue this is a claim about geometry disguised as a modelling choice: if the representations of two behaviours sit on curved or unevenly stretched manifolds, no single vector can be the right move for all of them, and the intervention will be well-aimed on some inputs and misaligned on others. Their proposal is to stop hunting for a better vector in the original space and instead learn a coordinate system in which a single translation is correct, then pull that translation back. The larger goal is an intervention that is both as expressive as a small trained adapter and as cheap and as input-transparent as a hook.

## What they studied

Five instruction-tuned models — LLaMA-3-3B and 3-8B, Qwen2.5-3B, 7B and 32B — plus one vision-language model (LLaVA-1.5-7B) for the image arm. Behaviours are six persona-dataset traits (conscientiousness, religion-following, self-improvement, self-awareness, alliance-building, impact-maximisation) defined by contrastive question pairs with binary answers, plus refusal and hallucination benchmarks in both multiple-choice and open-ended form, and the POPE object-presence benchmark for images. The unit of analysis is a single hidden state at one chosen decoder layer, with one learned map per (behaviour, layer).

## How they did it

Final-token activations are collected for positive and negative prompts at a chosen layer, split 80/20 at the *pair* level so no pair straddles train and test, and z-scored with training-split statistics only. A RealNVP-style invertible network — affine coupling layers with alternating partitions interleaved with activation normalisation — maps normalised activations into a same-dimensional latent space with an exact analytic inverse. It is trained on three terms: a Gaussian negative log-likelihood (which regularises the latent distribution), a term that pushes the positive and negative class means apart, and a penalty on the mean and variance of the log-Jacobian determinant, added because without it the network is free to stretch some regions enormously and the inverse then produces wild updates there. At inference a forward hook normalises, maps in, adds a scalar multiple of the latent mean-difference vector, maps back, and denormalises. The baselines are eight published steering methods spanning fixed directions, transport maps, Householder rotations, learned controllers and ODE/flow-based interventions; the authors tune strengths for each and report the best configuration found for every baseline. A LoRA adapter on the 3B model supplies the fine-tuning ceiling. A synthetic control of two concentric noisy rings makes the motivating argument concrete: the correct source-to-target move there is radial, so its direction depends on the angle and no single vector can serve.

## What counts as an explanation here

There is no representational account of the behaviour and the authors do not claim one. What is offered instead is a *reparameterisation*, and the checkable objects attached to it are (i) two geometry scores computed after training and not optimised for — a Fisher-style discriminability of the two classes along the mean-difference direction, and a consistency score measuring whether individual paired differences point the same way as the global direction — reported in both the raw and the learned space; and (ii) a theorem stating that the induced update in the original space is the latent vector pulled back through the local inverse Jacobian, so unless that transformed vector is constant across inputs no fixed activation-space vector can reproduce the method's first-order behaviour. The authors are explicit that invertibility is structural, not semantic: it rules out reconstruction error and makes the hook algebraically reversible at strength −α, but guarantees nothing about fluency or factuality.

## How they judged success

Alignment probability — the model's probability mass on the target answer token — is the primary metric, with perplexity on a reasoning prompt as the fluency cost, both reported per (model, task) cell against the base model. Three random seeds give standard deviations for every cell of the main table. Capability preservation is four zero-shot MMLU subjects, with an α = 0 arm that applies the round-trip map without any latent shift as a direct test of whether the identity is really recovered. Layer robustness is a sweep over all 32 layers compared against each baseline's own best layer. Safety behaviours are scored twice, once as multiple-choice probability and once as an open-ended LLM-judge score (GPT-OSS-120B, 0–10 scales for refusal and for hallucination, greedy decoding, 200 tokens). The image arm uses POPE accuracy and macro-F1 across random/popular/adversarial splits, with the "always answer no" degenerate strategy named as the failure mode to avoid. Cost is measured as wall-clock seconds per method on one H100.

## What they found

The gap over the best baseline is large and is largest exactly where the linearity assumption should hurt most — the small models. Averaged over the six traits, the strongest baseline reaches 78.61% alignment against 94.52% for this method, at perplexity 11.57 versus 12.37, and the headline comparison in the abstract is a 15.9% relative improvement over the best steering baseline on LLaMA and 13.42% on Qwen. On LLaMA-3-3B the method reaches 93.41% against a 72.51% base while the best competitor (ODESteer) reaches 78.42; the ceiling is LoRA at 97.05% with perplexity 15.21, and the runtime split is 29 s for this method against 11,400 s for the adapter. Per-seed variability is real and uneven: the tightest cells are near the ceiling (LLaMA-3-8B conscientious 99.60 ± 0.12) while the weakest cells carry the widest bands (Qwen2.5-3B religion 83.37 ± 5.92, LLaMA-3-3B impact 86.93 ± 4.86). The geometry scores move in the predicted direction on all thirty model-task pairs — discriminability rises from 0.39–0.74 in raw space to 0.94–1.00 in the learned space, and consistency from 0.15–0.45 to 0.74–0.94 — with the learned discriminability saturating so high that its variation cannot rank the pairs. Capability is nearly untouched at α = 0 (LLaMA-3-3B MMLU 45.35 base, 44.90 round-trip) and degrades modestly once steering is on (42.37), with the loss shrinking with scale to 1.53 points on Qwen2.5-32B (72.27 to 70.73). Refusal probability on the multiple-choice task goes from 69.06% for the best baseline to 84.48%; hallucination suppression is where the method is *not* uniformly best — it reaches the lowest probabilities on the mid and large models (Qwen2.5-32B 31.49 against a 40.42 base, Qwen2.5-7B 35.96 against 46.30), but loses to ODE and TruthFlow on LLaMA-3-8B (61.10 versus 62.50 and 63.80), so the advantage shrinks or reverses on the smallest models and grows with scale. Under open-ended judging the refusal score is the strongest average while perplexity stays competitive. Layer choice is a genuine advantage: steering on 9 of the 32 layers matches or beats the second-best baseline on its own best layer.

## Limits and open problems they name

A separate network must be trained per behaviour and per layer, so nothing is shared across traits, and the method's quality is bounded by the contrastive data and by how well-conditioned the learned map turns out to be. Invertibility prevents information loss but says nothing about semantic preservation. They flag multi-turn, compositional, open-ended and human-preference evaluation as future work, and — the part we should hold them to — propose that perturbing latent directions orthogonal to the steering vector, while keeping the behaviour fixed, would yield internal alterfactual-style explanations of which latent features are irrelevant. That is named, not done.

## What we take from it

Three things, one of which is a warning. First, a **reparameterisation is a legitimate alternative to a better vector**, and their synthetic two-ring control is the cleanest small demonstration in the folder of why: the target move varies with position, so a global direction is provably wrong and the failure is geometric rather than statistical. Any time our white-box model reports that a direction "mostly works", the two-ring test is the cheapest way to ask whether the residual failures have structure. Second, the **round-trip control at zero strength** is a control we should adopt verbatim: applying the identity path of any learned transformation and measuring the capability change separates "our map is lossless" from "our steering is harmless", and here it isolates a small honest number (45.35 to 44.90) from a larger one caused by the intervention itself. Third, the warning: the geometry scores they report as validation rise to 0.94–1.00 and then cannot distinguish anything, because a discriminability measured along the very direction the loss was trained to lengthen is close to circular. A score that saturates on every unit is not a measurement of the units. And the hallucination table is a useful counterweight to the headline: the paper's best-abstract summary is "consistently improves control", while the per-scale numbers show the competitors winning on the smallest models and the margin narrowing with size — exactly the pattern a pooled average hides.
