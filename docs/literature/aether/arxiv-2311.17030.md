## What they wanted, and why

The paper asks whether the two things a subspace intervention is used for can be separated: *changing* a model's behaviour, and *crediting* that behaviour to the subspace you touched. The authors' answer is that they routinely come apart, and that a great deal of mechanistic-interpretability practice — find a low-dimensional direction by optimising a patch, report the behavioural flip, call the direction the feature — rests on an equivalence that does not hold. The motivation is the linear-representation hypothesis: if features live in subspaces rather than neurons, then the field's unit of analysis is exactly the thing whose causal status this paper disputes. The larger goal is a defensible notion of "this direction carries that variable", which is the same target our white-box model needs, since a model that predicts behaviour from a direction is only useful if the direction is causally in the path rather than adjacent to it.

## What they studied

The unit of analysis is a **linear subspace of one component's activation** — in practice a 1-dimensional direction, with 100-dimensional cases in an appendix. Models: GPT-2 Small for the indirect-object-identification task and for the toy-to-real bridge, GPT-2 XL for the factual-recall and rank-1-editing half. Locations: the post-GELU hidden activation of the layer-8 MLP, the residual stream at the last token after layer 8, and the same residual stream at the last subject token for factual recall. Directions are found three ways: by differentiating the difference in name-mover attention scores with respect to the residual stream (a gradient direction), by DAS (optimising a learned rotation so that patching the subspace flips a behaviour), and by converting ROME's rank-1 weight edits into an equivalent activation subspace.

## How they did it

For IOI they use a restricted distribution — 216 single-token names, 144 objects, 75 places, three templates with two used in training and the third held out, so the test set is unseen in templates, names, objects and places; GPT-2 Small reaches about 91% on it. They compute a gradient direction that maximally shifts name-mover attention between the two names, and separately fit a 1-dimensional DAS subspace in the MLP activation and in the residual stream. They then patch between ABB and BAB examples — pairs that differ only in which name comes first — and measure two things: the fractional logit-difference decrease (how much of the model's log-odds margin for the correct name the patch removes) and interchange accuracy (the share of patches that actually flip the prediction). The diagnostic that matters is a decomposition: split the fitted direction into its orthogonal projection onto the kernel of the MLP's down-projection and the remainder in the row space, then re-run the patch on each part alone. A direction whose effect survives with the kernel part removed is causal; one whose effect collapses without it was carrying its signal in a component the network cannot read. They confirm the mechanism by plotting the MLP's contribution along the gradient direction before and after the patch, by repeating the exercise with the layer's weights replaced by random matrices of matched output statistics, and by sweeping the same tests over 100-dimensional subspaces. For factual recall they use the first thousand CounterFact facts that GPT-2 XL answers correctly, sample pairs within relations, fit DAS directions, and additionally derive ROME-equivalent subspace interventions so the two intervention families can be compared on the same facts with rewrite score, direction cosine, and a variance ratio.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **direction with a measured causal role**, and the paper's contribution to the form is a *negative* one: it specifies what evidence a claimed direction needs before the attribution half of the claim is licensed. Their proposal is that a patched subspace is credible when it is simultaneously discriminative for the feature on the data and aligned with the direction downstream components actually read, with the kernel/row-space decomposition as the screening test — in their words, patching the row-space part alone should retain the effect. The explanation is partial by construction: it is about one subspace at one component and one token position, and they explicitly say they cannot define a ground-truth mechanistic explanation to compare against, so the illusion claim rests on a collection of independent arguments rather than on a definition. There is no predictive model of the network here; the predictive content is narrower — "if this direction is causal, removing its unreadable component will not change the effect".

## How they judged success

Two metrics, printed side by side: **FLDD** (fractional logit-difference decrease, where above 100% means the patch more often than not flips the answer, and a negative value means the patch helped the model) and **interchange accuracy**. Controls and ablations: patching the full component as the baseline for what that component can do at all; patching the kernel projection alone (provably no effect, so a non-zero value is a numerical or implementation fault); patching the row-space projection alone (the diagnostic); both 1- and 100-dimensional subspaces; a random-weight MLP with matched activation norms as a "does the layer's content matter" arm; a two-name overfitting arm to check whether DAS is memorising; held-out templates, names, objects and places; and for factual recall, rewrite score, cosine between the fitted direction and ROME's key vector, and the ratio of introduced variance to ROME's.

## What they found

The MLP direction that DAS fits in layer 8 looks like it mediates name position and does move the log-odds, but its effect is carried by the part of it that the down-projection cannot see: the full direction gives 46.7% FLDD at 4.2% interchange accuracy, the row-space-only version drops to 13.5%, the kernel-only version to zero, while patching the entire MLP activation gives −8% — the whole component is not doing the job the subspace appeared to do. The decomposition is not balanced either: the kernel projection has norm about 0.65 against 0.75 for the row-space part, i.e. roughly equal, as their optimality argument predicts. The residual-stream direction behaves in the opposite way — 140.7% for the direction, 127.5% with the unreadable part removed, 123.6% for the full residual stream, and 74.8% / 63.1% / 54.8% in interchange accuracy — and it agrees with the independently computed gradient direction at cosine 0.78 against a random-vector baseline around 0.03, with 91% and 98% of their norms respectively inside the row space of the name-movers' query matrices. Only the S-inhibition heads write to it, which is what the published circuit says should happen. The illusion is not a matter of the layer's content: a random-weight MLP with matched statistics yields a *stronger* illusory direction. It also survives at 100 dimensions (MLP subspace 62% FLDD, residual 190%), and the row-space component is what removes the MLP effect in both cases. On factual recall the same pattern appears, and the paper's sharpest single result is an approximate equivalence between 1-dimensional activation patches and rank-1 weight edits: in layers 20 through 35 the derived subspace intervention matches a ROME edit on rewrite score, on direction cosine, and on total introduced variance, while removing the kernel component pushes every rewrite score below one in a thousand — which explains a previously unexplained observation that rank-1 fact editing works in layers where the fact is supposedly not stored.

## Limits and open problems they name

They cannot define a ground-truth explanation, so "illusory" is argued from several directions rather than proved; they concede that under a re-parameterisation that crosses component boundaries the toy illusion becomes the faithful direction, and defend component boundaries only by appeal to what heads and MLPs are observed to specialise in. The variance-ratio and rewrite-score evaluation of an edit is thin — they note that an edit should ideally be tested on related facts that ought to change. The residual-stream causally-relevant subspace is only a proxy, defined through the name-movers' query matrices, because no residual-stream subspace is in the kernel of everything downstream. And they accept that they cannot rule out that a given intermediate MLP genuinely holds cancelling subspaces, only that it is implausible for all of them to.

## What we take from it

Concretely for us: (1) a **measurement instrument**, the kernel/row-space split of any direction we fit or steer along, reported as a three-row table — full direction, row-space part, kernel part — with the effect on each; this is the cheapest falsification of our own direction claims and needs only a weight matrix and an intervention. (2) A **design rule**: prefer intervention sites that are bottlenecks (the residual stream) over sites that are one branch among several, because at a bottleneck there is no earlier component whose write can bypass the patch through a skip connection, and their numbers show the difference in kind, not degree. (3) A **warning about metric choice**: FLDD at about 50% with interchange accuracy near zero is a real partial effect, and reading only the flip rate would have discarded the phenomenon they are describing — print both. (4) A **framing** for our prediction step: an effect that appears only when the intervention pushes the activation far off its data manifold is not a prediction about the computation, and one cheap check is to re-fit the same account on a component whose weights are random, which here found a *stronger* effect and so dissolved the attribution. (5) The rank-1 equivalence is a **shortcut** worth carrying: an activation patch at one token and a permanent weight edit can be made to agree at that token, so a success in one currency buys the other for free, and neither proves the fact is stored where the edit lands.
