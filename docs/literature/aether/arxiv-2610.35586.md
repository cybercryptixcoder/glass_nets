## What they wanted, and why
Linear probes are the cheapest readable instrument for a third-party auditor: a few hundred labels, one matrix multiply, and a score for a property the model does not say out loud (truth, deception, refusal, harmful intent). The deployment question the paper asks is not whether a probe works but whether it survives being carried to the next model, because each release a lab follows a base model with an instruction-tuned chat version and a distilled reasoning version, and retraining means re-collecting labels and re-fitting for every successor. The whole practice rests on what the author names the preserved-representation assumption: that a successor keeps the base model's internal representation of harmfulness closely enough for a fixed linear classifier to still separate the classes. The paper tests that assumption directly and, separately, asks whether the probe reads anything beyond the surface words of the prompt, since a keyword detector would be useless regardless of how well it transfers.

## What they studied
Four base models and their two successors each: Qwen2.5-Math-7B, Llama-3.1-8B, Qwen2.5-14B and Qwen2.5-32B (the last in 4-bit NF4, the rest in bfloat16), each paired with its DeepSeek-R1 distillation (800k R1-curated samples, supervised fine-tuning only, no RL stage) and its developer's instruction-tuned chat model. The unit is a linear direction over the residual stream at one layer, read from a prompt-level vector taken as the mean of the last 8 prompt tokens' activations (widths 3,584 / 4,096 / 5,120 / 5,120). Prompts are fed as raw text with no chat template and no start token so that base and successor see identical tokens. Data: XSTest (250 safe prompts over ten types, 200 unsafe prompts over eight matched contrast types whose alarming words are shared with the safe ones — the construction that makes a text-only comparison meaningful), plus an independent 2,474-prompt set (OR-Bench hard 1,319 benign-but-alarming, OR-Bench toxic 655, Alpaca 500 ordinary instructions) never used for training.

## How they did it
For each family the probe is an L2-regularised logistic regression (C = 1, scikit-learn default) on per-coordinate standardised activations at a single layer, scored as the signed distance from its boundary. The 18 XSTest prompt types are merged into 8 groups, each a safe type with its own contrast, and in each of 8 folds one group is held out and the probe trained on the other seven — so a probe is always scored on a prompt type it has never seen along with that type's contrast. Layer choice is itself cross-validated inside the training groups with a mean-difference reader, and the chosen layer varies by fold (math-7b 17-19 with one fold at 27, llama-8b 12-23, qwen-14b 27-45, qwen-32b mostly 43). Transfer means applying the base model's probe *including the base model's standardisation* to the successor's activations at the same layer, unchanged; the ceiling is a probe trained on the successor itself, called its own probe. The "safety direction" is the difference of the two class means (training prompts only, per fold) normalised to unit length, and rotation is the angle to the successor's version of it, computed after dividing both models' activations by the *base* model's per-coordinate standard deviations so the two vectors live in one coordinate system. Controls: 38 directions built identically from labels unrelated to safety (all 35 four-versus-four splits of the 8 prompt groups, "starts with How", "has a capitalised non-first word", "longer than the median"), and a Gaussian reference that says a probe losing separation only to a rotation of θ should keep a share cos θ. Text-only baselines are TF-IDF logistic regressions over word unigrams, unigrams-plus-bigrams, character 3-5-grams, and prompt length alone. Intervals: Student t over 8 folds (which the author states are not independent, so he calls them nominal and rests conclusions on cross-family agreement plus the independent set), and a prompt-level bootstrap with 1,000 resamples on the independent set.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not an explanation in the mechanistic sense — a probe is a read-out plus a direction, and the paper treats both as quantities to be measured rather than interpreted. The two analytic objects are (a) the safety direction and the angle by which a successor rotates it, and (b) a one-line Gaussian prediction (share kept = cos θ) that converts an angle into an expected accuracy loss so the measured loss can be compared against it. The vocabulary projection in App. B.3 is offered as an indirect reading and explicitly carries no weight. The paper's claim is predictive in a narrow, checkable sense: a rotation of a stated size implies a bound on separation loss, and the bound is then tested.

## How they judged success
AUC on held-out prompt types, expressed as the change from the base model's own AUC and compared against (i) zero, (ii) the successor's own probe as a ceiling, (iii) the best text-only classifier on the same folds, and (iv) the cos θ reference. Robustness checks: a fixed layer per family instead of per-fold layer selection, three coordinate conventions for the cosine (differing by at most 0.013 at the chosen layers), a per-layer sweep with no selection at all, and a bootstrap on a prompt set from different authors. The threshold analysis uses the probe's unvalidated default of score zero and, separately, an XSTest threshold set to catch 90% of unsafe prompts.

## What they found
The probe transfers with essentially no loss. On XSTest the change is −0.003, −0.011, +0.003 and +0.010 for the distilled successors (base AUCs 0.878 / 0.908 / 0.899 / 0.931) and −0.006, +0.017, +0.027, +0.006 for the instruction-tuned ones; no distilled interval excludes zero. On the independent OR-Bench set it is −0.010, +0.042, +0.011, −0.015 and +0.016, +0.024, +0.053, +0.011 — gains for all four instruction-tuned successors, and one distilled loss whose bootstrap interval excludes zero. Meanwhile distillation rotates the safety direction by 30.5° to 35.1° and instruction tuning by 17.1° to 33.6°, and those angles predict nothing: the four largest rotations come with changes between −0.011 and +0.010 while the two smallest come with +0.027 and +0.006. The rotation is not special to safety — under distillation it is comparable to what the 38 control directions undergo (slightly more than all 35 splits in three families, slightly less in math-7b, and less than the prompt-length direction in every family). And the probe keeps *more* separation than the angle implies: for distilled successors it keeps 0.98 / 0.88 / 0.92 / 0.95 of the successor's own separation against a cos θ reference of 0.82-0.87, a 6-16 point excess (four families, exploratory p = 0.035, and mixed-sign under instruction tuning). On reading beyond the words the answer is only part of a yes: on XSTest the probes beat the best text-only classifier in all 8 folds of all 4 families (0.878-0.931 against 0.728), but on the independent set the margin shrinks to 0.11 / 0.03 / 0.06 / 0.20 (llama-8b's intervals overlap), and at the probe's default threshold it flags 29% / 35% / 42% / 29% of OR-Bench hard prompts — benign prompts that merely sound harmful — rising to 38-68% once transferred to the distilled models, while flagging 0-3% of ordinary Alpaca instructions. A threshold set for 90% recall on XSTest catches only 19-34% of the independent set's toxic prompts.

## Limits and open problems they name
The probe labels describe what a well-calibrated model *should* refuse — no model behaviour is measured and no causal test is run. XSTest's 8 folds share 6 of 7 training groups, so the intervals are narrower than the truth by an unestimable amount, and the author says conclusions must rest on cross-family agreement and the independent set rather than on any interval. The independent set's labels are OR-Bench's operational moderation labels, not independent ground truth, and the instruction-tuned models were run on raw prompts outside their chat format. The probes are linear and single-layer, so nonlinear or distributed encodings are out of reach; all four families are Qwen2.5 or Llama-3.1 bases with one distillation recipe; the 32B models are 4-bit; the 8-token pooling window was never tuned. The RL-checkpoint arm was dropped because those checkpoints were essentially unchanged (per-prompt cosine 0.9998 or higher), which the author states plainly means that arm cannot speak to RL post-training at all. The cos θ excess is called exploratory and rests on four numbers.

## What we take from it
Several things, and they are mostly about how to report a read-out rather than about probes. **A measure:** the paper's decomposition of transfer into (i) how far the read-out's direction moved (an angle, cheap, computable with no labels on the target) and (ii) how much separation the read-out actually kept, and its finding that (i) does not predict (ii) — so a white-box model that reports direction similarity as evidence of transfer is reporting the wrong quantity. **A framing:** the projection argument that makes the dissociation structural rather than lucky — a linear read-out sees only the component of the activation along its own weight vector, so any rotation lying in the orthogonal complement is invisible to it, and the paper's Gaussian reference turns that into an arithmetic expectation (cos θ) that the measurement beats by 6-16 points. **A warning:** the threshold result, which is the sharpest thing in the entry — a read-out whose *ranking* transfers intact has a *threshold* that does not transfer to a new prompt population, and the failure mode is a 29-42% false-positive rate on the benign-but-alarming prompts that a safety filter exists to let through. **A control:** the 38 label-unrelated directions built by the same recipe, which is the cheapest available defence against reading a rotation as a fact about the concept when it is a fact about the models. **A baseline:** the partial-input text-only classifier on the same folds and on at least one out-of-distribution set — the paper's own honest conclusion is that two thirds of its XSTest margin survives on the independent set for one family and almost none for another.
