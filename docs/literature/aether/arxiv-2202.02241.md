# Sparse Polynomial Optimisation for Neural Network Verification

- **Key:** arxiv-2202.02241
- **Authors / organisation:** Matthew Newton, Antonis Papachristodoulou; Department of Engineering Science, University of Oxford
- **Date:** 2022-02-04
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.02241
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

Most methods that certify a network's output range on a bounded input box work by bounding each activation function from outside, and the tightness of that bound determines both how much compute the resulting optimisation costs and how conservative the answer is. The authors argue two things about this state of affairs. First, no previous method attacked tightness and scalability at the same time — the papers that produce sharp bounds explode on real sizes, and the papers that scale give loose answers. Second, each paper in the area tends to handle one activation and one class of constraint in isolation, so there is no framework in which the ReLU trick, the sigmoid sector trick and the tanh sector trick can be turned on together. Their goal is a single formulation, drawn from a theorem in real algebraic geometry rather than from a bespoke relaxation for each activation, in which a dial trades solve time against tightness and the dial is a numeric degree rather than a different algorithm. The larger aim is ordinary for this head — robustness guarantees for safety-critical use — but the specific contribution is architectural: make the certificate flexible enough that activation-specific knowledge is data supplied to the framework.

## What they studied

Feed-forward fully connected networks with an input set given as a hyper-rectangle and a safe output set given as a polytope. The unit of analysis is the **whole network as a constrained algebraic object** — one variable per node, plus one decision variable per output-polytope face whose optimal value is the bound being computed. There are no neurons or features in the interpretability sense; the reading produced is a scalar extremum over the output set. Every network in the experimental section is randomly generated rather than trained, with weights drawn from a Gaussian, and the architectures are small by modern standards: single-input/single-output chains of two nodes per layer for the depth study, eight nodes per layer for the width study, a 100-layer stack for the node-count study, and the accuracy figures use two-in/two-out networks of eight layers of eight nodes (ReLU), ten layers of fifty nodes (sigmoid), and twelve layers of five nodes (tanh). Three activation families are studied: ReLU, sigmoid and tanh. The authors note in their conclusion that training real networks and re-measuring is future work.

## How they did it

The network is written as a chain of equalities (pre-activation is an affine function of the previous layer) and the reliability of each activation is written as a polynomial inequality or equality: for ReLU, nonnegativity, the line above the origin, and a complementary-slackness product that is exactly zero; for sigmoid, a sector pair pinning the function between two lines through the mid-value, sharpened by using two sectors instead of one to capture the inflection; for tanh, the same two-sector construction positioned differently. Input bounds and each output-polytope face are appended as inequalities, and the whole thing becomes a semi-algebraic set. Verification is then recast as an **emptiness test**: rather than proving an inequality holds, one proves that the set on which the constraint of interest is violated is empty, which the Positivstellensatz makes equivalent to the existence of sum-of-squares multipliers obeying a single identity. Minimising the face's decision variable over that representation gives the tightest bound the chosen multiplier degree can produce, and increasing the degree gives a nested family of tests of non-decreasing accuracy. The scalability move is to observe that every constraint involves at most one node and one adjacent layer, so the joint correlative sparsity graph — nodes connected when some constraint mentions both — is a chain of overlapping consecutive layers, whose maximal cliques are one per layer boundary. A chordal decomposition theorem then lets each large positive-semidefinite block be replaced by many small ones on those cliques, and since the clique structure is known in advance rather than computed, the decomposition is chosen rather than searched for. This is implemented in Julia with the CS-TSSOS hierarchy over MOSEK, and compared against the same authors' dense polynomial-optimisation implementation (SOSTOOLS/MATLAB) and against a quadratic-constraint SDP solver restricted to ReLU. The two multiplier settings compared throughout are "minimum order", which collapses to a constant multiplier, and second order.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **certificate**: a proved scalar bound on each face of the output polytope, valid for every input in the box, together with the nonnegativity witnesses that establish it. It is partial by construction — the number reported is the optimum over a restricted class of multipliers, so it is a bound and not the true extremum, and the paper is explicit that with constant multipliers the answer coincides with the established quadratic-constraint method. The form of understanding is therefore not a description of what the network does but a fence around what it can do, and the only predictive content is the fence. Notably, the objects that carry the explanation — the sum-of-squares multipliers and the small Gram matrices on each clique — are computed in full and never inspected; the paper reports the bound and the wall-clock time and nothing about what the multipliers allocated where. As in the rest of this head, the per-layer and per-node slack is the most-computed and least-read object in the paper.

## How they judged success

Two currencies, measured separately. **Scalability**: wall-clock solve time as the network grows, in two sweeps — depth is increased with two nodes per layer, then with eight nodes per layer, and separately layer count is fixed at 100 while nodes per layer grow — with a 1,000-second solve-time ceiling defining the point at which a method is called intractable, run on a 4-core 3.5 GHz Xeon with 16 GB of RAM. Each sweep is repeated for all three activations, giving nine figure walls plus three headline plots. **Tightness**: the computed output-set approximation is plotted against the true output values for the accuracy networks, with a shallow-multiplier variant and one competing method as the reference; the ReLU comparison is against the quadratic-constraint SDP, and for sigmoid and tanh, where that method does not apply, the comparison is against interval bound propagation. There is no ground-truth extremum computed by an exact method, no seed distribution over the random networks, and no benchmark suite — the baselines are one competing solver family plus a propagation method, and the "near optimal" claim for the ReLU accuracy figure is made by visual comparison against plotted true outputs rather than against a certified optimum. DeepSDP cannot be run at all for the nonlinear-activation accuracy cases, and the dense polynomial method is reported intractable there, so those two accuracy figures have only a loose baseline.

## What they found

On scalability, the sparse formulation with constant multipliers is the fastest arm in every sweep and is the only one that survives the width sweep — the dense polynomial method exceeds the 1,000-second ceiling at two nodes per layer once 100 layers are stacked, while the sparse method keeps scaling, and the competing ReLU-specific SDP is beaten in the eight-node-per-layer depth sweep only after an initial lead. Second-order multipliers cost more than the ReLU-specific SDP but stay tractable where the dense method does not, so the degree dial behaves as advertised: accuracy is purchasable and the exchange rate worsens as per-layer width grows, because the clique size grows with the layer and the decomposition cannot help within a layer. On tightness, the same dial is decisive: in the eight-layer ReLU network with input box [5,15] the second-order formulation's approximation is described as close to the optimal output set, while the constant-multiplier version reproduces exactly the accuracy of the existing quadratic-constraint method — meaning the tightness gain is entirely attributable to the multiplier degree and not to the sparsity machinery, which buys only time. For sigmoid and tanh the second-order formulation is the only SDP-based method that could be run at all at those sizes, and it produces bounds visibly tighter than interval propagation on networks whose output collapses toward a point. The paper's own summary is that the framework improves both solve time and bound quality over other methods, and it names the two-sided nature of the result: a constant-multiplier sparse solve is a speed win only, a higher-degree sparse solve is a tightness win at the price of the speed it just bought.

## Limits and open problems they name

The networks are randomly generated, not trained, so every scalability number is a statement about the solver on a synthetic instance rather than about a network anyone would deploy — the authors list training real networks as the first future direction. The activation set is ReLU, sigmoid and tanh; other activations are untested, which matters because the framework's flexibility is its selling point. The relaxation ladder is truncated at second order for cost, and the paper does not say where the ladder stops being worth climbing. The framework is set up for feed-forward fully connected networks: the sparsity exploited is the layer chain, and there is no treatment of a convolution whose receptive field would connect many variables per constraint, which is precisely the case where the chordal argument would be weakest. They propose pruning as a way to shrink networks before verification — an admission that the method still needs the network to be smaller. Beyond what they name: the accuracy claims rest on plots rather than on exact optima, so "near optimal" is unquantified; there is no ablation separating the effect of the tighter sigmoid/tanh two-sector bounds from the effect of the higher multiplier degree; and the soundness of the whole construction rests on the multipliers being genuine sums of squares on the specified supports, so a solver returning an inaccurate Gram matrix would silently invalidate a certificate with nothing in the paper's protocol to catch it.

## What we take from it

A **method**, a **framing**, and one **warning**.

The framing is the most portable thing here: **a certificate whose accuracy is set by the degree of its witnesses converts an algorithm choice into a numeric parameter, and that is what makes a family of certificates comparable in one table.** Here the dial is the multiplier order, and because both arms were run on the same networks, the paper can state cleanly that sparsity bought time and the multiplier degree bought tightness, with the constant-degree arm landing exactly on the prior method's numbers. That decomposition of a claimed gain into "cheaper" and "sharper" is the discipline we should copy whenever we report a new bound or a new probe: name what the change was supposed to buy and run the arm that isolates it.

The method is the clique structure itself, and it is the second confirmation of the shape the survey keeps finding — the network's own wiring graph, read as a constraint graph rather than as a weight mask, gives the decomposition. Here the graph is deliberately coarse (chain of layers, one clique per boundary) because the constraints connect neighbours only, which is exactly why the decomposition is trivial to compute and why it cannot help within a layer. Anything we build on a support decomposition should say which graph it used and therefore what it is structurally unable to compress.

The warning is the one this head keeps earning: **the certificate's own slack objects are computed and discarded in every paper, and this paper is the clearest case yet because its multipliers are literally per-node-per-constraint.** A run of this method leaves, on every node of every layer, a nonnegative weight saying how much that node's activation relaxation was bent to make the global matrix positive semidefinite, plus a small Gram matrix per clique. That is a free per-node read-out of which units the certificate found hardest to relax, comparable across checkpoints, and it is not printed. Two of our standing open directions — the slack read-out and the product-ratio table — can both be answered here without any new theory, only by printing what the solver already returns.
