# TTT: A Temporal Refinement Heuristic for Tenuously Tractable Discrete Time Reachability Problems

- **Key:** arxiv-2407.14394
- **Authors / organisation:** Chelsea Sidrane, Jana Tumova; Division of Robotics, Perception and Learning, KTH Royal Institute of Technology, Stockholm
- **Date:** 2024-07-19
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.14394
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (arXiv HTML v1)
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Almost all the effort in approximate reachability has gone into the *spatial* side of the problem: how finely to discretise the initial set, how to decompose the dynamics, which template set to circumscribe the true reachable set with. The authors argue that the *temporal* side has been left to hand tuning — the departure point is their own earlier tool, which mixes cheap one-step image computations with expensive multi-step ones whose intermediate sets implicitly constrain the trajectories passing through them, and whose ratio of cheap to expensive steps had to be found offline by trial. The goal of this paper is to make that choice automatic and online: decide, from the remaining wall-clock budget, at which time indices to fire the expensive multi-step queries and how deep each should reach. The larger aim is to give a practitioner one more independent knob, one that composes with the spatial tricks rather than replacing them, so a budget can be spent where it buys the most tightness.

## What they studied
Discrete-time dynamical systems of modest state dimension, nonlinear in the state, driven by a feedback policy that is a rectified feed-forward network — the neural-feedback-loop class. Four benchmark systems borrowed from the earlier tool and the ARCH-COMP report: a pendulum with a two-layer, twenty-five-neuron-per-layer controller, a TORA system with three layers of the same width, and a car system with one layer of one hundred and one layer of two hundred neurons. The unit of analysis is the whole forward reachable-set sequence over a fixed horizon — not a neuron, not a feature — and the quantity being optimised is the approximation error of that sequence relative to the true sets.

## How they did it
The underlying reachability engine is inherited unchanged: smooth nonlinear dynamics are bounded piecewise-linearly, those bounds and the network's rectifiers are encoded into a mixed-integer linear program, and a hyperrectangular template is then fitted to give the output enclosure. Only the *schedule* of queries is new. A query is either symbolic — compute the set at step *k* directly from a much earlier set, with the program implicitly forcing every intermediate step to be hit — or concrete — one step from the previous set. Symbolic queries are tighter because the intermediate sets act as constraints, and they also limit the wrapping effect because each jump starts from a tight ancestor; they are expensive, and their cost can grow faster than linearly in depth. The algorithm has two phases. In the *search* phase it starts at depth one and repeatedly re-solves the query from the initial set with the depth raised by one, until an estimate says going one deeper would spend more than the whole budget. The estimate is deliberately crude: it divides the time just spent by the number of steps just covered, takes the running maximum of that per-step cost, and compares the horizon times that figure against the budget. In the *jump* phase it then walks the rest of the horizon in equal-sized symbolic jumps, choosing the jump size so that the horizon divides evenly rather than leaving a loose short jump at the end. An early-stop watchdog, itself hand-tuned, fires if the solver has not produced a feasible program solution and shrinks the jump by one; the budget is only approximately respected because the per-step estimate is not an upper bound. The reported error is the ratio of the summed volumes of the approximate sets to the summed volumes of the reference sets, with a radius-sum ratio as a second metric.

## What counts as an explanation here
A schedule, not a model. The artefact this paper produces is a decision rule that maps (remaining budget, observed per-step timing, horizon position) to (which time indices get symbolic queries, how deep each reaches). It is explicitly partial and explicitly not guaranteed: the paper states outright that it cannot promise a larger budget yields a tighter set, which is the defining admission of a heuristic. It predicts nothing about the network and says nothing about where in the state space the error comes from; the enclosures themselves come from the inherited solver, and their soundness is inherited with them.

## How they judged success
Two error metrics — the volume-ratio of summed approximate to summed reference sets, and the analogous ratio of summed per-dimension radii — plotted against wall-clock budget in seconds, with the hand-tuned schedule of the earlier tool as a dashed baseline on the same axes and a naively concrete one-step schedule as a second baseline. The headline statistic is the percentage time reduction at matched error. Comparisons are made by choosing one run of the new method whose error is at most the baseline's at lower time, then reporting the speedup; the paper notes that individual sets are not always tighter than the baseline's even when the horizon-total error is. Soundness is asserted by citation to the inherited engine. There is no ablation of the time-estimation assumption, no sweep of the watchdog parameter, and the number of algorithm runs behind each curve is not stated.

## What they found
Error generally falls as budget rises, but not monotonically, which the authors attribute to the heuristic nature of the schedule. Against the hand-tuned baseline at matched error, the automatic schedule is faster on all four problems: on the pendulum 15.3 s versus 19.3 s (reported as 20.9 percent faster) with volume-error 1.94 versus 2.27 and radius-error 1.59 versus 1.61; on TORA 47.8 s versus 63.0 s (24 percent faster) with volume-error 7.78 versus 7.67 — i.e. 1.2 percent worse on that metric — and radius-error 1.38 versus 1.41; on the two car problems 117 s versus 400 s and 131 s versus 378 s (70.8 and 65.2 percent faster, respectively 4.7 and 4.1 minutes saved) with better error on both metrics (volume 3.89 versus 4.90 and 4.43 versus 5.29). Every set produced beats the naive one-step baseline on both metrics by a wide margin (e.g. car volume-error 3.89 versus 37.7). Given a larger budget than the baseline took, the schedule reaches about 40 percent lower error on the pendulum. The abstract's summary of the result is a 20 to 70 percent time reduction at comparable error.

## Limits and open problems they name
No completeness: the sets are not guaranteed to converge to the true ones as budget grows, and the authors say combining the schedule with a provably convergent method is out of scope. No monotonicity: more budget does not imply a tighter set, and they state that no such guarantee exists. Budgeting is unreliable in both directions — a bad initial program solution can overrun, the linear-in-depth cost model is wrong for problems where query time is exponential in depth, and long-horizon queries make both effects worse. The soundness of the whole pipeline rests entirely on the inherited solver and is not re-derived. Code is promised rather than linked.

## What we take from it
(a) A **measure**, and it is the one this thread has been missing: the paper defines over-approximation error as a ratio of summed volumes (and, as a check, summed radii) of approximate to reference sets, and reports it in a table next to every runtime. That is exactly the "how much of the space is slack" number that the closed-loop papers we have read either omit or illustrate with overlaid plots, and it is cheap to compute whenever reference sets exist. (b) A **framing** worth copying: the error of an account is a curve parameterised by the budget it was given, not a scalar, and the honest presentation is the whole curve against a baseline curve — a single "we were faster" number hides the fact that on one benchmark this method is slightly *worse* on one of the two metrics. (c) A **warning** that cuts against our own instinct: the paper's schedule allocates effort using a per-step cost estimate that is knowingly not an upper bound, and its budget promise is therefore approximate. This is run 631's split of heuristic-controller versus certified-guarantee at its most minimal — here the soundness argument is imported wholesale and the entire contribution lives on the heuristic side, and the paper is candid that the guarantee it cannot make is the one a user would most want. Mechanical rule for us: **when our account spends a budget on refinement, we report error as a curve over budget rather than a single point, we print the per-metric table for every baseline so a regression on one metric is visible, and we state plainly whether the budget promise is a promise or an estimate — because a schedule whose cost model is a lower bound on the truth will systematically overshoot the very budget it is supposed to respect.** Cross-reference runs 630 and 632: there the verdict and the slack were the missing report; here both are present, and what is missing is the guarantee that spending more buys more.
