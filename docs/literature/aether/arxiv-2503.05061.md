# No Free Labels: Limitations of LLM-as-a-Judge Without Human Grounding

- **Key:** arxiv-2503.05061
- **Authors / organisation:** Michael Krumdick, Charles Lovering, Varshini Reddy, Seth Ebner, Chris Tanner; Kensho Technologies / MIT
- **Date:** 2025-03-07 (v1); v4 2026-09-28
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.05061
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (arXiv HTML v4: abstract, §1–§7, references, Appendices A–K including annotator guidelines, F1 grids, baselines, the overruling example, temperature/CoT ablations, the jury, domain splits, the expanded frontier-model tables and every prompt template); no figure inspected
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Model-as-judge pipelines have spread because judge scores track human preferences cheaply and at scale, and because validation is usually one correlation coefficient against preference ratings. The authors ask a narrower and harder question: in domains where a response is either right or wrong, does a judge actually identify correctness, and what does its agreement depend on? Finance and business is the testbed because it forces multi-step quantitative answers whose correctness component experts can settle without arguing about style. Their finding is framed as a boundary on the method's legitimacy: a judge is trustworthy on questions it can already answer or where it is given a verified correct answer, and the literature's headline agreements come overwhelmingly from the first case, which is exactly the case where a judge adds nothing to just asking the model. This is a measurement paper about an instrument, and the instrument is the one a large part of this survey's evidence base rests on.

## What they studied
Three artefacts. BFF-Bench: 80 two-turn conversations (160 questions) with expert-written gold answers, designed to be hard, conversational and unambiguous. (C)MT-Bench: the math and reasoning categories of MT-Bench, 20 conversations / 40 questions, whose shipped reference answers were audited and rewritten; 15 of 40 were found incorrect or internally inconsistent before repair. VERDICTS: 1,200 model responses from six models spanning 2B to frontier (Gemma 2 2B, Qwen 2.5 7B, Phi-4 14B, Yi 1.5 34B, Llama 3.3 70B, GPT-4o), each graded three times by domain experts, giving 1,036 single-grading and 604 pairwise-grading items after consensus filtering. Unit of analysis: the (question, response) pair, stratified by whether the judging model *as a candidate* answered that question correctly according to the expert consensus.

## How they did it
Each response is graded independently by three analysts from a pool of nine salaried finance professionals (questions mostly written and graded by the same pool, so a small annotator set with expert overlap). Only items with unanimity or near-unanimity survive; the 14% with no consensus or consensus on "not sure" are excluded. Five of the six models also act as judges (format reliability excluded Gemma 2B), with self-consistency over five samples at T = 0.7 and chain of thought; pairwise judgements are run in both orders and disagreements counted as ties. The core manipulation is the *reference* placed in the judge's prompt: none, self (a reference the judge generated itself), human-written gold, GPT-4o-written but human-verified, deliberately wrong (rewritten to look stylistically similar but reach a false answer), and random (a reference lifted from another question). Agreement is reported as Cohen's κ against the expert consensus, separately for the stratum where the judge could answer the question and the stratum where it could not. Baselines: three lexical metrics, five MTEB embedding models scored by cosine to the reference, and five RewardBench reward models. Robustness: a second independently written prompt set, temperature 0.0/0.2/0.7, with and without chain of thought, a five-judge majority jury, per-dataset splits, and a frontier-model replication using GPT-4o-with-gold as a proxy label.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not an explanation of a model, an explanation of a *metric's* validity boundary. The form of understanding is a two-way contingency: judge agreement conditioned on (a) whether the judge model can solve the item and (b) whether the supplied reference is correct. That table is the paper's whole explanatory machinery, and it is meant to be predictive in a narrow sense — it says in advance where a judge's score can be trusted and where it will look fine while being uninformative. It is explicitly partial: nothing about mechanisms, nothing about why the failure concentrates on the items the judge cannot solve beyond the observation that judging needs the same knowledge as answering.

## How they judged success
Cohen's κ against expert consensus with bootstrap confidence intervals, F1 as an alternative, false-positive and false-negative rates on the single-grading task to characterise the error *profile* per reference type, and Δκ (agreement on answerable items minus agreement on unanswerable ones) with an interval, reported separately for each dataset and reference type. Baselines are graded on the pairwise task, where correctness is unambiguous by construction. Every headline claim is a comparison between reference arms on the same items, so the manipulation is clean; what is not controlled is annotator identity (graders are also question writers) and inter-annotator reliability beyond the consensus filter.

## What they found
With a human-written reference, every judge beats every baseline — including Qwen 2.5 7B with a reference outperforming GPT-4o without one. Without a correct reference, judges agree with experts mainly on the items they can already answer, and degrade sharply on the rest: GPT-4o's pairwise κ falls from 0.86 on its own correct answers to 0.16 on the items it got wrong in the self-reference arm, and recovers to 0.83 with a gold reference; Phi-4 is at −0.08 on the incorrect stratum with either no reference or its own. The pooled consequence is a decay that the headline hides: the best judge on the finance benchmark reaches an overall κ of 0.46 with no reference, 0.61 with a verified model-written reference and 0.69 with gold, and the same pattern holds on (C)MT-Bench with higher absolute numbers. Reference *correctness* matters more than provenance: a human-verified GPT-4o reference nearly closes the gap to a gold one, while a plausible-but-wrong reference is *worse than nothing* (κ 0.21 against 0.50 for a random unrelated reference), and the error profile says why — with a wrong reference the false-negative rate rises and correct answers get marked incorrect. Self-preference bias is visible as a higher false-positive rate when grading one's own responses, largest in the self-reference and no-reference arms, and reduced by a human reference. Judges often fail to notice the wrong reference at all, but GPT-4o sometimes identifies the error and overrules the reference to grade the answer correct — an instructed-trust failure the authors document with an example. A five-judge majority jury does not escape the pattern: on the stratum where the jury majority was wrong, single-grading κ with a gold reference rises to 0.51 but sits at 0.07 without, and 0.01 with self-references. Ablations over temperature, chain of thought, prompt wording, dataset and (in an appendix using a proxy label) frontier models leave the pattern intact; higher reasoning effort narrows the no-reference gap without closing it, which the authors read as consistent with their own account.

## Limits and open problems they name
A single domain, one language, one task family (questions with a settled answer), and a modest judge panel by design. Matching judges to candidates is what lets them stratify by the judge's own competence, but it means the judge panel is not the frontier panel practitioners actually use — the frontier replication uses a proxy label, which the authors flag as corroboration rather than a main result. Pairwise grading keeps only correct-versus-incorrect pairs, so its numbers are relative. They name as open whether verified *synthetic* references can substitute for human ones at scale, and warn that they improve the metric while worsening self-preference. Reader-side limits: the annotator pool of nine is small and overlaps with question authorship; the stratum labels are themselves consensus judgements, so Δκ conditions on a noisy variable and its interval reflects that; several claimed comparisons ("not significant impact" for temperature and CoT) rest on overlapping intervals rather than equivalence tests; and no judge in the panel is asked to abstain, so the missing third option is never measured.

## What we take from it
**A stratification variable, and it generalises past judges.** The single most useful object here is not κ but the split *by whether the evaluating model could itself produce what it is evaluating*. Every scorer we build — a probe, a feature labeller, a verbaliser scored for whether its description is right, a reward model used as a label source — has an implicit conditioning of this kind, and the paper shows the pooled number is dominated by the easy stratum. Report the scorer's accuracy conditioned on its own competence on the same item, the way this folder already demands per-context and per-disagreement-stratum breakdowns. **A warning that the stratum is where the claim lives.** Here 0.86 pooled-versus-incorrect-stratum gaps are enormous and invisible in the headline; a probe validated on items the model handles has not been validated on the items the model fails, and those are the items any explanation of failure is about. **A concrete adversarial control worth copying.** The *wrong-but-plausible reference* arm is the sharpest version of a control this folder has met in several guises (a false but same-region claim that scores like the true one; a manufactured class that carries the shortcut): an input that is stylistically correct, topically on-target and factually inverted, scored against the random and the true versions, separates "responds to the content" from "responds to the surface". Applying it to our own read-outs means perturbing the *content* of a stimulus while preserving its form and checking that our statistic moves in the right direction, not merely that it moves. **A procedural habit:** when our own pipeline uses a model as a grader for explanations or behaviours, the required report is the grader's own accuracy on the same battery, and if that is unavailable, gold references for a sample. **A framing caution:** "agreement with human preference" and "identification of correctness" are different quantities, and most published validation measures the first while most uses need the second.
