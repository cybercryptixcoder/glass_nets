# Predicting Where Steering Vectors Succeed

- **Key:** arxiv-2604.15557
- **Authors / organisation:** Jayadev Billa; unaffiliated researcher (previously ISI@USC, Yahoo, Nuance, BBN)
- **Date:** 2026-04-16
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.15557
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
A steering vector is usually chosen by trying layers and keeping the ones that work. The author's complaint is that the field has no cheap way to say, before running the intervention, whether a given concept on a given model can be steered at all, or which layer to steer at. The proposal is to reuse an instrument the interpretability field already has - the logit lens, which applies the model's own output projection to an intermediate hidden state - as a predictor of steering outcome, on the argument that the two are the same quantity viewed twice. The larger goal is a go/no-go screen: spend one forward pass per prompt instead of a layer sweep, and expect particular kinds of failure for the concepts the screen rejects.

## What they studied
Concept families with single-token answers, so that the logit lens yields an unambiguous classification accuracy. Five heterogeneous "core" families (arithmetic, geography, sequence completion, word transformation, analogy) are used for within-concept layer profiles; 24-25 "controlled" binary families (two answer classes, balanced groups, several prompt templates each, about 176-200 prompts per family) are used for across-concept prediction and released as a benchmark. Primary model Gemma-2-2B (26 layers, d=2304), replicated on Llama-3.1-8B, Mistral-7B-v0.3, Qwen2.5-7B, Qwen2.5-1.5B, Llama-3.2-1B-Instruct, OLMo-2-1B-Instruct, five Pythia sizes, and two non-transformer stacks (Mamba-1.4B, RWKV-1.6B). Unit of analysis: the concept family at a layer, i.e. a population of prompt activations rather than a neuron, feature or head.

## How they did it
Three measurements are taken per layer of a frozen model. First, linear accuracy: run every prompt's hidden state through the final layer norm and the unembedding matrix, take the argmax over the vocabulary, and score whether it equals the correct answer token - no training, one extra matrix multiply per layer. Second, a nonlinear upper bound: train a residual MLP adapter (two layers, width 512, GELU, dropout 0.1) at each layer on 80% of prompts to predict the same token, its accuracy being the concept's "nonlinear accessibility"; the gap between that and the logit-lens accuracy is called the probe gap and is read as information that is present but not output-aligned. Third, a perturbation-sensitivity number: add a random perturbation of 1% of the hidden norm at that layer, push it through the remaining network, and measure how much the output direction moves per unit of input displacement, averaged over ten random directions. The triple is called the Linear Accessibility Profile. Steering itself is always difference-of-means between the mean hidden state of prompts with the target answer and that of the others at the same layer, injected into non-target prompts, with effect measured as the change in the target token's probability. Correlations are then computed across layers within a family, and across families using each family's peak linear accuracy, with a partial correlation controlling for layer index because both quantities rise with depth.

## What counts as an explanation here
A per-layer profile of three scalars, plus a three-regime story built on it: below a threshold on nonlinear accessibility the concept is not represented yet and no method can work; where it is represented nonlinearly but not linearly (the "crystallization gap") difference-of-means must fail and a learned, nonlinear read-out is needed; where it is linearly accessible, difference-of-means works and the layer to use is the one with the highest profile value. The explanatory claim is mechanistic but one step long: steering adds a vector, the read-out is approximately linear in the hidden state, so the intervention's effect is governed by the steering direction's projection through the unembedding, and the logit lens is a measurement of exactly that projection's alignment with the target token. It predicts a rank order of concepts and a layer, not a magnitude.

## How they judged success
Spearman correlation between peak linear accessibility and maximum steering effect, across controlled binary families, per model; the same correlation restricted to families above a floor; Pearson partial correlation controlling for layer index on the pooled layer-by-family grid, with a within-family permutation test that shuffles the steering effects and asks how often the observed correlation is exceeded; comparison of the predicted layer against the oracle-best layer found by exhaustive search; steering efficiency defined as the probability gain divided by collateral KL on 50 unrelated prompts, compared between the recommended and the conventional middle layer; cross-model prediction (score concepts on one model, steer on another); and end-to-end generation demos with before/after completions. A trained logistic-regression probe and a tuned lens are run as comparison instruments, and a simple heuristic sweep of layer targets and thresholds is calibrated on three models and checked on two.

## What they found
Linear accuracy is exactly zero for the first fifteen of Gemma's twenty-six layers on all five core families and jumps sharply in the late teens to early twenties, peaking just before the last layer. The probe gap is very uneven: small for arithmetic and sequence (about 0.22), but 0.72 for geography, whose answers a nonlinear probe recovers perfectly while the logit lens reaches only 0.28, so the concept can sit in the model for many layers in a form the output projection cannot read. On prompts the model answers correctly, linear accuracy runs from about 0.47 to 0.95; on prompts it answers wrongly it is 0 to 0.17, which the author reads as linear accessibility being necessary for correct output. Per-family layer correlations between linear accuracy and steering effect are +0.72 to +0.87, but controlling for depth drops the pooled partial correlation to +0.51 and the weakest family's to +0.37 with p = 0.067 - the depth confound is real and is disclosed. Across the controlled families, peak linear accuracy predicts maximum steering effect at +0.86 to +0.91 on five models, and the correlation survives above the floor only on the larger models (+0.93 on Qwen-7B, +0.84 on Llama-8B, but +0.50 to +0.52 on Pythia-2.8B and Gemma-2-2B). Within Pythia, the cross-family correlation rises from +0.42 at 160M to +0.86 at 6.9B and the count of steerable families from 5 to 9 of 23. The recommended layer matches or near-matches the oracle for four of five families; the exception has the weakest signal and the largest probe gap. The raw logit lens beats a trained tuned lens for layer selection, which the author explains by noting that difference-of-means steering is itself realised through the raw unembedding. Two clean exceptions are given: a parity family with respectable linear accuracy but a near-zero effect, because the steering direction's projection lands on the target token at rank 13,440; and a gender family where the projection points correctly but the answer was already probable enough that there was little room to gain. Efficiency at the recommended layer beats the middle layer on every family, with sequence going from zero effect at L13 to a large one at L24. A logistic-regression probe classifies above 93% at *every* layer from the first onward, yet steering at those layers produces nothing. Entity-steering and refusal demos redirect free-form completions at the recommended layer on two models and leave them unchanged at the middle layer, both at matched injection strength. Non-transformer stacks show the same late emergence.

## Limits and open problems they name
The floor effect on the headline number is acknowledged: concepts with near-zero linear accuracy trivially have near-zero steering effect, and the above-floor correlation is much weaker on the two small models, so the high headline is partly the trivial ordering of dead concepts. Single-token answers only, which rules out most of what practitioners actually steer on; multi-token answers are sketched as an extension, never run. No steering experiments on the non-transformer stacks. Thresholds are calibrated on three models and called likely to need adjustment at larger scale. The parity and gender misses suggest the profile needs a companion baseline-probability check, which is proposed rather than implemented. The prediction for SAE features - that they should pay off most in the middle regime - is stated as a prediction and explicitly left untested. Perturbation sensitivity is reported as a failure-diagnosis feature rather than as a validated predictor.

## What we take from it
A method and a warning. The method is the cheapest pre-hoc screen in the folder: a training-free projection of intermediate states through the output head, scored as classification accuracy over a concept family, and used both as a go/no-go and as a layer picker. The warning is that the two are different claims of very different strength, and the paper's own numbers show it - the concept-level prediction is strong on large models and weak on small ones once the trivial floor is removed, while the layer-level claim is a rank over 26 points of a grid with a strong depth trend, surviving only as a partial correlation around +0.5. There is also a reusable control we should copy: a trained probe with above-93% accuracy at every layer that nevertheless cannot steer, which shows that linear decodability and output alignment are distinct properties of a hidden state, and that any of our read-outs reporting decodability as evidence of steering capacity would be quoting the wrong number. Finally, the two named failure shapes - the projection not landing on the target token's rank, and the answer already being probable - are the natural baselines any effect-size prediction of ours should report.
