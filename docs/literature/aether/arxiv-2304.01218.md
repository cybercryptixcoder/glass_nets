# POLAR-Express: Efficient and Precise Formal Reachability Analysis of Neural-Network Controlled Systems

- **Key:** arxiv-2304.01218
- **Authors / organisation:** Yixuan Wang, Weichao Zhou, Jiameng Fan, Zhilu Wang, Jiajun Li, Xin Chen, Chao Huang, Wenchao Li, Qi Zhu; Boston University ECE, Northwestern University ECE, University of Dayton CS, University of Liverpool CS
- **Date:** 2023-03-31
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.01218
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (arXiv HTML v1)
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
This paper is the engineering follow-up on the original prototype: make the same symbolic machinery faster and tighter, extend it to rectifier activations the original prototype handled poorly, and then settle the question the field had been arguing without data — whether the two competing families of model-checking tools (ones that track dependencies across the loop versus ones that only push output ranges) actually differ in practice, and by how much. The larger goal is to know which modelling commitment buys tightness in closed-loop verification, so a practitioner can choose a tool by what it tracks rather than by name.

## What they studied
Closed-loop systems of the form plant ODE plus a four-stage controller: a preprocessing map, a feed-forward network (ReLU, sigmoid, tanh, or a mixed ReLU-tanh / ReLU-sigmoid output layer), and a postprocessing map that clips the network output into the actuator range. State dimensions run from two to twelve. The unit of analysis is the functional over-approximation of the flowmap — a polynomial in the initial-state variables plus an interval remainder — carried across network layers, across control steps and across the plant's continuous evolution.

## How they did it
The framework is set propagation: at each control step take the over-approximate state set, push it through preprocessing, network and postprocessing as symbolic objects, then integrate the ODE under the resulting input range and carry the end-of-step set forward. Inside the network every layer is one affine map followed by an activation; the activation is replaced by a polynomial plus an error interval, and that interval is then transported through the remaining layers. Three additions distinguish this tool from its prototype. First, polynomial choice for each activation is made after composition rather than before: the authors show a worked sigmoid example in which the candidate with the smaller standalone error interval produces the larger error after being composed with an upstream remainder, so they define the "accuracy preservation" question (does the better object stay better under composition?) and answer it negatively for this arithmetic, then simply evaluate both candidates and keep the tighter result. Second, for rectifier activations they exploit convexity: an order-k Bernstein polynomial of a convex function lies above it, so the upper bound is that polynomial and the lower bound is the same polynomial shifted down by its own value at zero, which they prove is the exact maximum gap; this gives a remainder that is tight to within a factor the paper calls tight for any order. Third, the interval remainders are not multiplied out layer by layer. They are kept symbolically with the accumulated matrix that will transform them, and only collapsed to an interval at the final layer, because the wrapping effect in interval arithmetic comes from evaluating a chain of linear maps one at a time; the cost is two extra arrays and two nested loops, so the layer-wise pass goes from linear to quadratic in network depth. Multithreading parallelises the network pass only. Evaluation: sixteen benchmark configurations (six standard reach-avoid tasks each with four activation variants, mountain car, adaptive cruise control, 2D spacecraft docking, attitude control, quadrotor-MPC, and a twelve-dimensional twenty-agent case) against six other tools on a six-core machine, with per-tool hyperparameters tuned so the resulting sets are comparable and only time compared.

## What counts as an explanation here
A functional over-approximation of the closed-loop flowmap: a polynomial over the initial-state variables plus a certified remainder interval, valid for every initial state in the given set and every time in the control interval. It is explicitly partial — sound but loose, never exact — and it does predict, in the strong sense that any actual trajectory must lie inside the boxes, so a box that misses a forbidden set proves safety for all trajectories at once. The explanation is of the dynamics, not of the network's computation: nothing in it says which neurons or features caused the reachable set to be where it is.

## How they judged success
Four-valued verdict per benchmark (property proved, disproved, unknown because the enclosure was too loose under the best setting found, or did-not-finish from a tool or system error), wall-clock seconds, and tightness read off overlaid plots of the reachable sets against simulated trajectories. Ablations: multithreaded versus single-threaded on the same tool; the new rectifier handling against the prototype on one benchmark; the two families of competitor tools against each other; and, notably, an explicit investigation of competitor setup defaults, in which the authors report that one tool's published timing is a warm second run, that another has a division-by-zero failure mode, and that a third produced sets that do not contain the simulated traces on two benchmarks.

## What they found
On the four activation variants spread over the six standard tasks, the tool proves the property in nearly every case and never returns unknown; the mixed competitors return unknown frequently, and pure range-only tools return unknown on almost the entire range-only comparison. On the hardest hybrid benchmark (quadrotor-MPC, 3 position errors as inputs, thirty steps) only three tools engage it at all: 13.1 s to prove it here versus 961.4 s for the differentiable-activation symbolic baseline and unknown after 10854 s for the range-only stack. On the twelve-dimensional twenty-agent case it finishes in 316.8 + 212.5 s (network plus plant) where every other tool either fails at one step, explodes at twenty-five steps, or is recorded as non-finite. The rectifier example shows 1.8 s versus 7.1 s for the prototype with visibly tighter boxes. Multithreading helps at higher dimension and hurts at low dimension because of overhead. The paper's own diagnosis of the pattern: range-only methods can be individually tight on the network's output but compound error across the loop, and the closed loop makes the compounding slow the later steps down as well, whereas dependency-tracking methods pay more per step but stay tight enough to keep going.

## Limits and open problems they name
Scaling is the stated open problem, and it is attributed to three quantities the method itself cannot shrink: state dimension (the polynomials are parameterised by the state variables, so the expressions grow), control step size, and the number of control steps. The largest configuration still costs minutes. One benchmark's property is reported as disproved and another's variants are disproved, so closure is not universal. They also name no quantitative measure of over-approximation width: the comparison of tightness is by inspection of plotted sets, and the summary of results is a tabulation of verdicts and times. There is no limitations section, no ethics or AI-use statement; the software link is not given in the HTML I read.

## What we take from it
Three concrete things. (a) A usable engineering pattern: evaluate competing symbolic objects after composition rather than before, with a small worked counterexample showing why the cheaper choice is wrong — the same check is worth running on any surrogate we build, since a local error metric need not be monotone under the transformations the account then applies. (b) A proof that a structural property of an operation removes a whole class of estimation: for a convex activation the Bernstein polynomial is one-sided, so the two-sided remainder collapses to half of one evaluated value. Our white-box account should look for the same leverage — an operation whose known shape makes the error term fall out analytically rather than by sampling. (c) The comparison is the real contribution and it is a warning about evaluation norms: two families of tools differ not in per-step precision but in whether error compounds around the loop, and the paper only establishes this by overlaying pictures and timing tables. For us: when we claim one representation is tighter, we report the width ratio on matched queries, and we separate the cost of the representation from the cost of a single evaluation inside it — this paper, like its predecessor, measures the second and only illustrates the first.
