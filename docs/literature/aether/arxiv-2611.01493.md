## What they wanted, and why
Mean-difference steering works or backfires depending on which layer receives the vector, but the field's selectors pick a layer from the *extraction* data alone — before ever steering the model — because evaluating each candidate layer needs held-out runs. The two standard terms are a Fisher-type discriminability (how separable the two activation clouds are along the steering direction) and a directional consistency (how aligned the individual example displacements are with their mean). The authors argue both terms miss the object that mean-difference steering actually consumes: the vector is an average of per-example displacements, so whether one vector can stand for the pairs is a property of the *displacement cloud*, and neither separability of the clouds nor alignment of directions says how *extended* that cloud is. They want a selector computed from the same cached activations, requiring no extra forward passes and no validation sweep, and they want to explain why such a criterion can rank layers differently from the accepted ones. The larger goal is cheap, predictable intervention siting — a step toward knowing, from weights and a handful of contrastive pairs, where an edit will and will not take.

## What they studied
Five instruction-tuned models: Llama-3-3B-Instruct, Llama-3-8B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct. Six binary behavioural-steering tasks from the Anthropic Persona Dataset (conscientiousness, religion following, self-awareness, self-improvement, alliance building, impact maximization), each a question with an aligned and an opposite response. Activations are the residual stream at the final prompt token, at every layer. Unit of analysis: a layer, scored by its cloud of per-example displacement vectors; the intervention is the mean-difference vector at the selected layers.

## How they did it
For each task, the contrastive pairs yield a mean-difference vector per layer from raw activations, and the same cached activations yield a *displacement cloud*: standardize each layer coordinate-wise over all activations, subtract the negative from the positive member of each pair, and collect the resulting vectors. Each layer is then scored by the cloud's total degree-zero persistence, which they use in its computational form — the Euclidean minimum spanning-tree length — computed from the pairwise distance matrix. The top-k layers are steered with the raw (unstandardized) mean-difference vector at all intervention token positions. Three baselines: LayerNavigator (discriminability plus consistency, the closest prior validation-free selector), discriminability alone, and a random draw of distinct layers from the middle third of the stack, so the random arm is a competitive reference rather than a degenerate one. Two regimes: *simultaneous*, all selected layers steered at once from one extraction pass, and *sequential*, where after each injection the extraction is re-run through the already-steered model and the next highest layer is chosen from the re-scored, unseen layers — which costs k extraction passes and is a protocol, not a selector. A unit-sphere variant of the score (each displacement normalized to length one before the tree) is carried as a second arm, and a bridge share — the fraction of the score carried by the single longest edge — is returned with every score as a diagnostic for scores driven by one connection between modes rather than by the bulk. Theory: they prove two invariances of spanning-tree length (translation, and scaling of the whole cloud), then a decoupling theorem on a one-parameter family that holds class means and the steering vector fixed while scaling example-level residuals, and they analyse a second, structural axis imposed by the contrastive format (which of the two answer tokens was designated behaviour-matching) by projecting it out as a diagnostic. An appendix sweep varies the topological descriptor, the filtration distance and the layer budget, and a per-input learned selector (W2S-Multi) is compared at a stated compute cost.

## What counts as an explanation here
A layer-selection rule, not a mechanism: a scalar per layer, computed from the geometry of the displacements a mean-difference vector averages, together with a theorem about which geometric variations it is sensitive to and a diagnostic that flags when a single edge carries the score. It is deliberately partial — the score does not say what the direction means, only whether one vector can represent the pairs at that depth — and it is predictive in a limited sense: it is meant to rank layers without running the steering experiment.

## How they judged success
Aligned-answer probability (%): the average probability assigned to the behaviour-aligned response, averaged over the six tasks, reported per model and per layer budget in {1,2,3}; generation perplexity as a fluency check; MMLU accuracy before and after steering as a capability check; per-task results and selected-layer lists in appendices; an oracle sweep over descriptors and distances as an upper bound; wall-clock comparison against the evaluation-based selector. There is no significance test and no seed-variation study in the reported numbers — the arms differ by selection rule only, on the same prompts and the same vectors.

## What they found
Under simultaneous steering, TopoSteer beats LayerNavigator in 10 of 15 model–budget comparisons, ties in one and is lower in four. The largest gains are on Qwen2.5-3B, where aligned-answer probability goes from 64.30% to 71.77% (budget 1), 64.16% to 77.63% (budget 2) and 66.21% to 79.71% (budget 3); on Llama-3-8B it gains 2.18 and 1.22 points at budgets 1 and 2. On Qwen-7B and Qwen-32B the differences are small and mixed, and increasing the layer budget does not reliably help. Sequential re-selection changes rankings — Llama-3-3B favours LayerNavigator at budget 2 (80.61 vs 80.41) and TopoSteer at budget 3 (78.13 vs 75.17) — so no arm dominates across model, budget and regime. MMLU moves little under any selector. The oracle sweep over descriptors and distances matches or beats LayerNavigator in 29 of 30 model–behaviour pairs, gaining 4.0 points on average, but no single descriptor or distance wins across pairs — the authors present it as an upper bound, not a prescription. Against the learned per-input selector, the simple zero-training rule is a little weaker on alignment (78.90 vs 81.25) at a little lower perplexity (13.78 vs 14.53) and roughly half the total runtime (8.4 vs 15.9 minutes). The structural analysis finds that the score is blind to the common mean displacement and that class separation reaches it only through the shared standardization (it enters as a divisor), so part of any measured anti-correlation with discriminability belongs to the standardization rather than to the displacement geometry. They also isolate an answer-polarity axis produced by the contrastive format: it splits the cloud into two lobes while carrying essentially no class information, it cancels from the mean-difference vector by construction, and removing it moves the two criteria through different mechanisms — a direction change for the Fisher term, a bridge/merging effect for the tree length — and does not reconcile their rankings.

## Limits and open problems they name
The method assumes matched positive/negative pairs and is tied to mean-difference extraction; multi-layer steering can cost perplexity on some models; the decoupling theorem is local to a fixed layer and its across-layer consequences are empirical; the guarantees can miss mode splits concentrated in a few coordinates; the learned per-input selector still wins on alignment; and sequential re-selection can change which method is preferred. Ethical and reproducibility statements are present, and the AI-use statement confines model assistance to language editing and code.

## What we take from it
Three things. **A framing**: for any edit built by averaging contrastive examples, the *object being averaged* has a geometry, and its spread is a third quantity independent of class separability and of directional consistency — so when we claim a direction is "the" feature, we can report the extent of the per-example displacements we averaged, which is cheap and needs no extra forward passes. **A measure with a null built in**: spanning-tree length is invariant to the mean and homogeneous, so a layer score is comparable across depths without the mean-displacement confound; and the bridge-share diagnostic is a two-number report (bulk-driven vs one-edge-driven) that our own clustering-of-activations claims should copy, since a cluster score driven by one long edge is the same failure. **A warning about validation-free selection generally**: here the selection rule is genuinely better than the accepted one in two thirds of the comparisons, but the sequential regime, the cheaper desirability of the simpler rule and the learned selector's higher alignment, and the fact that no single descriptor wins the oracle sweep, all say the selector is a *policy* whose ordering flips with the escalation protocol — which is the folder's recurring lesson that a ranking is only as good as the protocol it was ranked under. For our predictive-model work: report the ranking's stability across the intervention protocol (single-shot vs re-extraction), because a white-box model that predicts "edit here" is claiming an ordering that this paper shows can invert when layers are added one at a time.
