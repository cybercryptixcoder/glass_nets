# Disambiguating Moral Circuits and Moral Features in Language Models: Moral content, normative judgment or cue words? Evidence from 14 models

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.21705
- **Authors / organisation:** Davide Liga (University of Luxembourg); Liuwen Yu (Luxembourg Institute of Science and Technology); Matteo Magnini (University of Luxembourg)
- **Date:** 2026-11-16
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21705
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

Interpretability methods can name the attention heads or dictionary features that move a model's answer to a moral prompt. Naming them is not explaining them: a component that flips "stealing medicine for a dying child is morally ___" could be responding to moral content, to the judgment of conduct by *any* norm, or merely to the word "morally" in the prompt. Each reading implies a different organisation of the model and a different pattern of results on prompts that vary one property at a time, and the authors point out that results from a single family of prompts cannot separate them. This is the folder's central methodological question — is the located component a mechanism for the concept, or for the operative cue — put to a concrete domain with a pre-registered design, and the larger goal is a general recipe for making "this component does X" a falsifiable claim rather than a label attached after selection.

## What they studied

Fourteen open-weight models in base and instruction-tuned pairs: Llama-3.2-1B, Llama-3.2-3B, Llama-3.1-8B, Gemma-2B, and Qwen2.5-3B / 7B / 14B. Units of analysis are attention heads (patched), SAE dictionary features, MLP neurons, a steering direction, and a Jacobian-lens direction over layers. Items are minimal pairs of short phrases: 227 retained new pairs written in five categories (moral; four non-moral standards — prudential, etiquette, aesthetic, epistemic; plus descriptive pairs where nobody's conduct is judged), each shown in three frames — a cue frame ending "… is morally" after two priming sentences, a no-cue frame ending "… is" after the same priming, and the original bare cue frame. Fourteen SAE dictionaries cover seven of the fourteen models (TopK dictionaries trained for the project on Llama-3.2-1B/3B base and IT at several layers and sparsities, the public Llama Scope dictionary at layer 12 of Llama-3.1-8B, and layer-0 dictionaries for both Qwen2.5-7B models).

## How they did it

Components were located on an older set (73 moral minimal pairs ending "is morally" plus 32 descriptive controls, later joined by 189 non-moral norm pairs and 73 descriptive pairs for the feature search), then tested exclusively on the new pairs, none of which had been seen during selection. The head measurement is activation patching: run the restrictive version of a pair with one head's output copied from the permissive version and record the change in a score built from fourteen positive and fourteen negative answer words; positions align exactly because the two versions have the same token count in every frame. Crucially, raw effects are not compared across pairs — each pair's absolute effects are normalised to sum to one, so the measure is the *share* of the pair's total effect that a head carries, and a head set's selectivity is its mean share on moral pairs divided by its share on normative pairs, pooled over models as a geometric mean. Two controls carry the argument: controls in the *same* frame (which removes the frame difference that the earlier controls confounded with content) and a *mirror image* selection (heads chosen on normative halves and given a normative/moral ratio), so that any excess produced by selecting heads where one category puts its effect is priced rather than assumed away. Hypotheses, frames, answer words, the annotator retention rule, the analyses and the reading of each outcome band were written down before any model saw a new pair, and the models were run on all candidate pairs so that retention — decided by five blind annotators — could not depend on outputs. Further tests varied the request: a bare "… is", a plain "good or bad?" question, "socially" as the cue, 400 one-sentence scenarios from the ETHICS benchmark, a two-option choice with no evaluative word in either prompt or answer, and twins differing only in who bears the consequence. Feature work repeated the design on dictionaries, with an explicitly reported capture statistic and interventions that leave the reconstruction error in place; neuron work selected lists on one half of the pairs and mean-replaced them on the other.

## What counts as an explanation here

A decomposition into levels, not a single object. The explanation is a claim about *what kind of machinery* a component belongs to — a general evaluation pathway shared by every standard, or a moral circuit of its own — supported by: a circuit of ten located heads per model, the share of the behavioural gap they carry and that their removal costs, a set of dictionary features, a few hundred neurons, one steering direction, and a lens-derived direction that carries the *category* of a pair. It is deliberately partial and explicitly predictive: the three readings in the design each predict a different pattern of shares across pair kinds and frames, so the experiment can and does discriminate between them, and the paper reports which reading the observed pattern fits and where it does not fit cleanly.

## How they judged success

Selectivity ratios with percentile bootstrap intervals over items (10,000 resamples), per-model permutation tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction across models, and pre-declared interpretation bands: an interval entirely inside 0.8–1.25 counts as no meaningful content specificity, above 1.5 as strong, and a two-sided α = 0.025 per primary hypothesis. Two primary hypotheses (H1 with the cue, H2 without). Necessity is tested by mean-ablation of the located heads against twenty random sets of ten heads, both drawn from all layers and drawn from the matched layers, with the fraction of the gap removed reported per category. Explanations are also scored against out-of-distribution prompts: the ETHICS scenarios, the two-option choice with a control question that needs no evaluation, and a twin design that holds scenario and edit fixed. Dictionary claims are gated by a capture statistic — the R² of the difference between the two reconstructions against the difference between the two hidden states — before any feature result is read. Five annotators blind to the intended labels validated the pairs, with a retention rule fixed in advance (α = 0.977 for which version is better; 0.738 for the class of standard).

## What they found

The located heads are general evaluation machinery. Pooled over fourteen models, their moral/normative share ratio is 1.00 with the cue [97.5% CI 0.92–1.07] and 0.90 without it [0.84–0.96] — both intervals inside the pre-declared no-specificity band, and the second also excluding 1. Per-model ratios run 0.83–1.22 and 0.70–1.08, with no model near the strong-preference line. They carry about a fifth more of the effect for normative than for descriptive pairs about a thing (1.19 [1.13, 1.26] with the cue, 1.21 [1.14, 1.28] without), and the authors' 58 additional descriptive pairs *about a person* show that difference lies wholly (no cue) or by half (with cue) between phrases about a person and phrases about a thing, not between judged conduct and the rest. Mean-ablating the located heads removes a median 23% of the gap on moral pairs with the cue and 18% without, against 28% and 21% on normative pairs, beating all twenty random sets in 10 of 14 models on each frame; on ETHICS scenarios removal costs 37–39% of the acceptable/unacceptable difference against 1–3% for random sets. Their attention concentrates on the cue (28% median on "is morally" at the final position, against 15% for the average head) and on the edited tokens in equal measure on moral and normative pairs. The depth at which half the gap is first recovered is the same for moral and normative pairs in 7 of 14 models and within one layer in six of the remaining seven, and it is the cue that moves that depth (0.53 → 0.67 of depth). The located dictionary features follow the *word*: on descriptive pairs ending "is morally" they fire at 0.70–0.99 of their moral-pair activation, and on moral pairs without the cue at 1–33% of it; they respond to "ethically" at about four fifths, "socially" at about half, "logically" at about a third, and to "Morally speaking" at the sentence start hardly more than to no cue. What is moral-specific is small and elsewhere: every model names the annotators' standard when completing "… is a matter of" 81–92% of the time while its located heads treat all standards alike; the lens-derived direction for moral words, removed or exchanged, cuts the moral gap by 2–11% in seven of the eight small models while barely touching the others; held-out dictionaries in four of ten cut the moral gap 5–10% without the cue; 25 neurons per layer cut the moral gap 5–9% in four Llama models but cut the prudential member of a twin about as much. One steering direction, built from normative pairs alone, moves moral, normative and descriptive judgments alike in all fourteen models (40–240% of the pair's gap), and directions built from moral and from normative pairs alone point within cosine 0.72–0.94 of each other. The models' *answers* nonetheless outrun their components: on 23 twins the moral member is judged more strongly than its prudential twin in 11 of 28 model×frame combinations and less strongly in none, and a classifier trained on the main pairs identifies the moral member of a twin in 90% of cases.

## Limits and open problems they name

The judgment is always read from evaluative next-token words (mitigated by the bare "… is" prompt, the plain question, and ETHICS, but only partly by the two-option choice, where the located heads beat all random sets in 6 and 7 of 14 models). A cue fits some pairs better than others — "is morally" suits moral pairs best — and the design does not fully separate cue fit from moral content wherever a result depends on the cue; the authors state this themselves and note that "socially" set etiquette pairs apart in 5 of 14 dictionaries, so a non-moral cue that suits a kind of norm does for that kind what the moral cue does for moral pairs. Items are short English phrases plus one-sentence ETHICS scenarios, and instruction-tuned models receive raw text without a chat template, so the results describe sentence completion rather than conversational behaviour. The dictionaries cover only seven of fourteen models at one to four layers each, and capture little of the pair difference (mean R² below zero in 13 of 14 with the cue), so feature results are claims about dictionaries. The Jacobian lens was fitted only for models up to 3B for budget reasons. Head patching shows a head's output can move the score; it does not say what the head computes. Pairs about a person and twins were checked by language models that first passed a test against the human annotators, not by the annotators. Human validation rests on five lay annotators, showing people see the intended contrast rather than a theoretically derived categorisation.

## What we take from it

Three reusable methodological results, all of which bear on our probe and circuit work. (1) **Comparator arithmetic**: comparing raw effects across item families overstates specificity because almost every unit shares an offset — 86–100% of heads in each base model have larger raw effects on the moral set, and the ten heads selected by the raw test carry 1.3–2.1× share in 13 of 14 models. Normalising each item's effects into shares removes the item-level scale, and the remaining ratio is the quantity worth reporting. (2) **Same-frame controls**: controls written in a different frame count the frame difference as content; the paper's own pilot shows the offset vanishes (1.03 vs 2.25) once control prompts use the same frame and answer words, so any specificity claim we make with a control set that differs in template, length or answer vocabulary is uninterpretable. (3) **Mirror-image selection**: a set of units selected where one category puts its effect looks selective on fresh items of that category by construction, and the fix is to run the same selection in the opposite direction and require the excess to survive the comparison — here ten heads chosen on moral halves give 1.04/0.99 and ten chosen on normative halves give 1.01/1.12, i.e. no asymmetry beyond the control. The fourth thing worth copying is the *capture* statistic reported before any feature claim (R² of reconstruction-difference against hidden-state-difference, median 0.04, negative in most dictionaries), which is exactly the honesty column our dictionary entries usually lack. As a warning, the paper's central empirical result is that a component's behaviour can be entirely explained by the surface cue it reads over — and that the features most people would report as "moral features" are detectors of the word at the end of the prompt, firing nearly as hard for "The harvest after a season of steady rain is morally".
