## What they wanted, and why

Mechanistic interpretability has spent years describing what a circuit computes during inference, but it has had almost nothing to say about where that circuit came from — which training examples caused a head or a feature to exist at all. The authors call this the static-paradigm problem and argue that a developmental account would pay off twice: scientifically, it would connect a mechanism to the statistics of the corpus that produced it, and practically, it would let someone filter or amplify specific data to steer which mechanisms a model forms. The paper's target is one of the field's canonical units, the induction head, and one of its canonical hypotheses, that induction heads underlie in-context learning. The claim they want to make is causal rather than correlational: this data distribution caused this circuit.

## What they studied

The unit of analysis is an attention head, with a preliminary qualitative extension to MLP neurons and SAE features. Induction heads and previous-token heads are the two head types analysed, localised by their own scoring metrics (a prefix-matching score and an average attention mass on the preceding token). For causal validation they retrain the first four Pythia sizes from scratch — 14M, 31M, 70M, 160M parameters — with the official architecture and hyperparameters but dense checkpointing around the emergence window; they report about 800 A100 GPU-hours in total. For scale they run attribution-only tracing on OLMo-2-1B and OLMo-2-7B. The data is the Pythia pre-training corpus (the Pile), scored within a per-model emergence window rather than over the whole corpus: roughly 0.7-1.2M samples per configuration for the four small models.

## How they did it

They take the standard influence function — the change in a function of the parameters induced by upweighting one training example, computed through the inverse Hessian — and swap two things. First, the target function is no longer validation loss but a probe of the unit's own behaviour, evaluated on a synthetic probe set: for an induction head, its direct logit contribution to the copy target on repeated-token sequences; for a previous-token head, its attention mass on position t−1 averaged over random sequences. Second, the Hessian is approximated (EK-FAC) inside the unit's own parameter subspace only — the concatenated query and key matrices for a previous-token head, and the query-key, value and output blocks for an induction head — so curvature from the rest of the network is excluded. Query and key are concatenated rather than decomposed into an effective product matrix, because EK-FAC's Kronecker factorisation is only valid for linear maps of the input. This gives a three-step recipe (localise the unit, compute the unit-specific influence vector, project every training sample's gradient onto it) and a ranking of training examples. They then run counterfactual retraining in both directions inside the window: mask the gradients of the top-influence samples (deletion, a necessity test) or duplicate and insert them (augmentation, a sufficiency test), each against a random-sample arm of the same size. Finally, the top 2,000 positive samples are handed to an LLM (DeepSeek-V3) to extract JSON structural templates, and the templates are turned into Python generators that synthesise training data at scale — a data-augmentation product built directly on the attribution.

## What counts as an explanation here

An explanation is a ranked list of training examples that accounts for a unit's existence, plus a data-level account of which structural features of those examples do the causing. It is explicitly partial and explicitly localised: the authors say plainly that influence computed inside one head's subspace does not decompose indirect effects arriving through upstream nodes, and they concede that aggregating along the whole computational path "introduces substantial noise" (their phrase) and is left undone. So the object predicts nothing about outputs; it predicts how a training intervention will move a unit's score, and it is validated only in that direction.

## How they judged success

The primary criterion is bidirectional causal intervention during pre-training: does masking the top samples delay or suppress the head relative to random masking, and does duplicating them accelerate emergence relative to random duplication? Head strength is tracked with the prefix-matching score from the induction-head literature; robustness is checked with an independent metric (a head-specific zero-ablation logit drop on a synthetic induction task). Controls include random deletion and insertion, an n-gram-matched augmentation baseline that inserts the same bigrams and trigrams without the influence ranking, a QK-only probe variant, and cross-seed ranking stability. General capability is monitored on WikiText-103 language-modelling loss and PopQA factual retrieval. The ICL link is tested by measuring an ICL score (late-context loss minus early-context loss) on WikiText-2 while the same data interventions are applied.

## What they found

Deleting the high-influence samples suppresses or delays the targeted heads, and duplicating them accelerates emergence; random interventions of the same size do nothing measurable — with the important qualification that both the deletion and the augmentation arms converge to comparable saturation scores eventually. Influence scores are heavy-tailed, with the top tenth of samples carrying up to half the cumulative influence (the appendix gives a power-law exponent near 3). The top-ranked samples are overwhelmingly high-repetition material — XML/Base64 blobs, dense code, LaTeX, serialised database dumps, and outright noise — which the authors call a mechanistic catalyst for induction. High-influence sets identified from one induction head overlap strongly with those of other induction heads and remain distinct from non-induction heads, and cross-head projection scores on the top samples are roughly an order of magnitude larger for induction heads; augmenting with samples selected from one head moves the others. Temporal analysis is the surprise: high-influence samples and their scores are spread uniformly across the window, samples drawn from early, mid and late intervals are all effective, and the head still develops when 95% of training samples in the window are gradient-masked — so the mechanism accumulates steadily and the samples modulate its rate rather than triggering it. Interventions on induction-head formation move ICL scores in the same direction. Synthetic data generated from 14M-derived templates raises induction scores at every scale tested (14M 0.432 to 0.485, 31M 0.472 to 0.523, 70M 0.304 to 0.352, 160M 0.508 to 0.558), and 14M-derived synthetic data beat 160M-derived data on the 160M model (+12.3 points of relative increase at 14M versus +9.84 at 160M from its own proxy; the 160M model augmented with its own 160M-derived templates moves +2.56). General language-modelling and factual-recall curves are described as indistinguishable from baseline over the observation window. Tracing cost rises roughly linearly with model size (11.6 A100-hours at 14M through 384 at 7B).

## Limits and open problems they name

The causal arm stops at 160M — 1B and 7B get attribution but no retraining, so the causal claim is made at the smallest four scales and generalised by analogy. Attribution is a subspace-local approximation and does not resolve cascades (a previous-token head influencing an induction head downstream). The window is chosen post hoc from where the score rises, and the authors defend it on cost grounds rather than as a principled definition; they also note post-peak fluctuations and exclude that regime. The augmentation study finds a crossover: synthetic data wins up to about 50,000 samples and loses at 100,000 because the finite template set exhausts its diversity, and the insertion-schedule results for synthetic data are scale-dependent and left open. The larger-model head dynamics are explicitly preliminary — in Pythia-160M some heads lose induction behaviour under intervention while others strengthen, which the authors call a hand-off and decline to characterise. SAE-feature attribution is qualitative only.

## What we take from it

The transferable method is the substitution, applied twice, not the influence function: replace the loss target with a behavioural probe of a chosen unit and restrict the curvature to that unit's subspace, then validate by intervention rather than by correlation. That is the same "target is the experimental variable" move the probe-attribution entry made for attribution graphs, here arriving in the parameter-gradient rather than the activation-graph setting. The framing we want is the distinction between a catalyst that sets the rate and a trigger that sets the event: this paper measures a rate effect and shows the accumulation threshold does the rest, which is a reading of any intervention result we produce — an effect that persists after the intervention is removed is a rate change, not a mechanism, and should be reported as one. The controls are also worth copying directly: n-gram-matched augmentation is exactly the size-and-content-matched null that a "repetition mattered" claim needs, and the paper's decision to keep the unmatched random arm beside it shows why one control is not enough. Finally, the uniform-in-time influence distribution is a warning about the search we would otherwise run: if we want to find the samples that made a feature exist, sampling around the moment it appeared is the wrong instinct, because the contributing data was there all along.
