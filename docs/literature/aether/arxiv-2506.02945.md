## What they wanted, and why

Automatic evaluation of model output has drifted toward asking one language model to grade another, and the graded numbers inherit the judge's problems: compression toward the middle of the scale, miscalibration to the domain, prompt sensitivity, leniency. The authors' diagnosis is structural rather than empirical: a next-token-prediction model is being asked to perform regression on a numeric scale, and fine-tuning it to do so (as the Prometheus family does, with cross-entropy over generated rationales plus a score) attacks the wrong objective at the cost of gradient updates over billions of parameters. Their proposal is to split the job — let the frozen judge do what it is good at (write a rationale) and hand the *number* to a small classical regressor trained on human scores from the target domain — and to arrange the regressor so that it can never be worse than the raw judge score it is built on.

## What they studied

Two base judges used as black boxes: Prometheus-7B-V2, an evaluator already fine-tuned for judging, and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, a general instruct model, with the second included to test whether the recipe survives outside a purpose-built evaluator. Both absolute judging (one response → rationale + score) and relative judging (two responses → rationale + preference) are covered. Data: Summarize from Feedback (7-point Likert, 8.59k train / 6.31k test), HelpSteer2 (5-point helpfulness, 20.3k / 1.04k), Offset Bias (synthetic good-versus-fluent-but-flawed pairs, 6.8k / 1.7k) and Nectar (7 GPT-4-ranked responses expanded to all 21 pairwise comparisons per prompt, 83.9k / 21k). The unit of analysis is a single (prompt, response, rationale-embedding, score) row; the objects being compared are four post-hoc predictors fitted on top of the frozen judge.

## How they did it

For each judged item the frozen judge emits a rationale and, where the prompt allows, next-token probabilities over the score tokens; the rationale's final-layer embedding is the feature vector, and the judge's own score (or its log-probabilities) is kept as an extra input. Four generalised linear models then predict the human score: a least-squares regressor on the concatenation of embedding and judge score, with a population bias beside the slope so that the zero-feature solution reproduces the judge exactly; a multinomial logistic classifier over discrete Likert values using the embedding plus log judge-probabilities, again with a bias that reduces to the judge's own distribution; a Bradley–Terry–Luce probit-style link for pairwise preference on embedding plus log-odds of the judge's preference; and a two-headed variant that feeds the *difference* of two pointwise rationales' embeddings into the same BTL form, with the judge's two scores recombined into a prior. Regularisation strength is chosen by five-fold cross-validation, training uses SGD, and every result is an average over ten randomly drawn training subsets, each run at 10% of the available human labels to imitate a domain where ratings are scarce. Fine-tuned judges (same base model, same prompts) are the expensive baseline; the plain mean and majority-class score are the naive ones.

## What counts as an explanation here

A calibrated mapping, not a story. Nothing in the paper claims to explain *why* a response is good; the interpretation on offer is that a linear map from the judge's written rationale to the human scale is a sufficient statistic — if a 4,096-parameter read-out of the rationale recovers most of what fine-tuning could, then the judge's qualitative content was already carrying the signal and only the arithmetic was wrong. The claim is deliberately partial: it holds for the four domains tested, depends on the rationale's embedding being informative, and requires human labels in-domain to fit at all.

## How they judged success

Six metrics on rating tasks (MSE, MAE, exact Likert accuracy, Pearson r, Spearman ρ, Kendall τ) and seven on preference tasks (accuracy, precision, recall, F1, and the same three correlations), all computed on held-out test sets; gains are marked significant at p < 0.05 against the base judge, judged across the ten resampled training sets. Baselines are the absolute and relative base judges, the fine-tuned versions of the same base models, and the naive mean/majority predictors. Ablations cover training-set size, regularisation strength, and embedding substitution (Prometheus 4,096-d PCA-reduced to 384 versus all-MiniLM-L6-v2 at 384). Cost is reported as GPU minutes per dataset on a single A100-80GB, split into embedding computation and fitting.

## What they found

Fitting the number beats asking the judge for it, on the metric each model was fitted to. With the Prometheus base judge on Summarize from Feedback, the least-squares judge cuts MSE from 6.346 to 2.626 and MAE from 2.041 to 1.362, while the multinomial judge raises exact Likert accuracy from 0.168 to 0.229; HelpSteer2 shows the same pattern (MSE 2.232 → 1.431 for least squares, accuracy 0.355 → 0.416 for multinomial). Correlation is where the story muddies: on Summarize from Feedback the least-squares judge's Pearson r is flat at 0.323 against 0.317 while Kendall τ *falls* from 0.272 to 0.214, and the fine-tuned judge reaches 0.370/0.330/0.285 — so optimising the level of the score degrades the ranking of it. Preference tasks behave better for the two-headed variant: on Offset Bias with Prometheus, the two-headed judge reaches 0.783 accuracy and r = 0.634 against 0.648 and 0.298 for the absolute base judge and 0.535/0.049 for the relative one, and with Llama the same configuration hits 0.800 accuracy and r = 0.657. The pairwise-base judge is the weakest arm on Offset Bias overall, and the paper's own explanation is that pairwise judgments are the most susceptible to surface cues. Nectar is the exception that keeps the result honest: there the fine-tuned judge wins outright with Llama (0.770 accuracy, r = 0.541) and only ties with the two-headed judge under Prometheus, i.e. with plenty of labels the advantage disappears. Cost is measured, not estimated: 0.320 + 0.455 GPU-minutes (embedding then fitting) for the least-squares judge on Summarize from Feedback against 14.160 for fine-tuning, and 2.247 + 0.538 against 19.300 on Offset Bias — a factor of about 6.9 — with the authors noting the embedding pass dominates their own cost. The cross-validation-tuned regularisation matters at both extremes, and swapping in a 384-d MiniLM embedding is roughly harmless for rating but consistently worse for preference, which is the one place the base judge's own embedding earns its keep.

## Limits and open problems they name

Human labels in the target domain are a hard prerequisite — the whole method is a calibration to a scale it must be shown — so it cannot calibrate a new domain without ratings. The quality ceiling is the rationale embedding: if the judge wrote an uninformative critique, the read-out inherits that, and the paper's embedding ablation is the only probe of this. The sample-efficiency advantage is conditional, and their own ablations say so plainly: fine-tuning wins on accuracy under Prometheus at all training sizes, and wins overall on Nectar, with the read-out's edge limited to small-label regimes (Llama up to 20% of data on accuracy for rating, two-headed up to 10% on Offset Bias) — consistent with a 4,096-parameter model against one with billions. Not named but visible to a reader: the confidence intervals come from resampling *training* sets at fixed test sets, the exact-accuracy metric on a 5- or 7-point scale is a weak instrument, and the rationales are never checked for whether the regressor is using content a human would endorse or a length/tone proxy that happens to correlate with scores.

## What we take from it

Two framing moves and one warning. The framing move is the **frozen-features-plus-linear-read-out as an evaluation instrument**: the authors' justification for the small model is explicitly borrowed from probing literature, and their result is the same shape our own read-outs keep producing — a linear decoder over a representation recovers a quantity that the generating model cannot report accurately itself, and does so cheaply. Applied to our work, this is an argument that a probe's value should be scored against *the model's own verbal report of the same quantity* rather than against a random baseline, because that is the comparison a practitioner will actually make. The second move is the **zero-feature anchor**: every judge here is parametrised so that the empty model reproduces the base judge exactly, which guarantees "never worse than what you started from" by construction and makes the reported improvement a genuine delta rather than a re-basing — a cheap design pattern for any read-out we fit on top of an existing scorer. The warning is the **metric split they measure but do not reconcile**: optimising squared error halved MSE and *hurt* Kendall τ, and accuracy gains and correlation gains do not co-move across their four judges. For our own evaluation numbers this is the same two-denominator lesson the folder keeps meeting — a fit statistic and a ranking statistic on the same data can move in opposite directions, so any claim that our predictor "matches" a target should name which of the two it was scored on and report the other beside it.
