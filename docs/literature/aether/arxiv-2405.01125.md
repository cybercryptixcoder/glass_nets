# Lipschitz constant estimation for general neural network architectures using control tools

- **Key:** arxiv-2405.01125
- **Authors / organisation:** Patricia Pauli, Dennis Gramlich, Frank Allgöwer; University of Stuttgart (Institute for Systems Theory and Automatic Control) and RWTH Aachen (Chair of Intelligent Control Systems).
- **Date:** 2024-05-02
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.01125
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Computing the exact Lipschitz constant of a network is intractable, so the field settles for upper bounds, and the two families in use trade against each other: a product of spectral norms is free but wildly loose on deep nets, while semidefinite-programming (SDP) bounds are the tightest known polynomial-time certificates but blow up in memory and time on anything that looks like a real architecture. The authors want a bound that stays inside the accurate family and scales: tighter than the norm product by orders of magnitude, cheap enough to run on 18-layer residual nets, and defined for the whole layer zoo an actual classifier uses rather than for the chain of dense layers that the classic SDP bound assumes. The larger goal is the same one the control community has pursued for decades - certify a property of a composition from certificates of its parts - and the paper's claim to novelty is that it imports two structural facts the machine-learning side had not exploited together: the network *is* a dynamical system when the layer index is read as time, and a convolution *is* a linear system once it is written in state space.

## What they studied
Fully-connected, fully-convolutional and residual classifiers trained on MNIST and CIFAR-10 - LeNet-5 in two pooling variants, a 2-conv/2-dense net, a 4-conv/3-dense net, a 6-conv/2-dense net, and 18-layer residual networks with either dense or convolutional residual paths - plus a synthetic sweep of fully-convolutional backbones at depths 2, 4, 8, 16 and channel widths 8, 16, 32 on a fixed 14x14 input. The unit of analysis is the **layer as a system component with a quadratic storage function**, and the end-to-end object is a scalar bound on the map. Nothing about individual neurons, features or directions is studied; the model is inspected only as an operator between signal spaces.

## How they did it
They write the network as a recursion in which each layer maps an input to an output and hands it to the next, then pose the bound as an optimisation over the terminal spread of two inputs subject to that recursion. Instead of one giant constraint they split it: define, for each layer boundary, a quadratic function of the *difference* between the two trajectories, and ask for the smallest output-to-input gain for which a chain of one-layer inequalities holds. That is exactly a dynamic-programming backward pass, and each one-layer inequality is then relaxed to a linear matrix inequality (LMI) whose slack variables depend on the layer type. For a dense layer the relaxation is exact - the condition is equivalent to the difference-in, difference-out inequality. For a convolution the layer is first realised as a Roesser-type 2-D state-space system in which the kernel taps occupy the read-out matrices and the shift structure is fixed, so a Lyapunov-style inequality with one positive-definite block per propagation direction certifies the layer; the same block is reused verbatim for a state-space model layer, because the proof never looks at the structure of the dynamics. Activations enter through a slope-restriction quadratic constraint with a diagonal multiplier, and the paper is careful to note that the published ancestor of that constraint used a full matrix multiplier and was wrong, corrected elsewhere; GroupSort enters through its own multiplier class, averaging pooling through a scalar rescale, and max pooling by forcing the slack to be diagonal. A residual layer is handled by letting the storage block of the skip path appear in the same inequality as the residual branch's two linear layers. Zero padding is treated as the full-padding case, which over-approximates same and valid padding, so the bound is valid for those too. The bound comes from a single SDP in which the first and last storage blocks are pinned to a scaled identity and the identity respectively, and the paper keeps the number of variables small by concatenating layers into small subnetworks (a convolution plus its activation) rather than one sparse constraint per network. Code is released, and all runs use one solver on the same laptop.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **certificate attached to a parameterised model** - a matrix-inequality chain that a checkpoint satisfies or does not - plus the scalar it yields. It is not an explanation of what the model computes; no feature, direction, unit or input is named. What it does offer is one unusually sharp piece of semantics: the slack matrices are a *metric* on each layer's input and output differences, so the optimisation is not just bounding a number but discovering a weighted norm under which the composition contracts. The paper proves that fixing those slacks to a symmetric block-diagonal form loses nothing relative to the general dissipativity formulation for dense nets, which makes the certificate exact relative to its parent rather than merely tighter. Predictiveness is one-directional: given weights, the bound holds; given the bound, nothing follows about which part of the net is responsible for its size, and the paper never decomposes that.

## How they judged success
Tightness of the bound against two named competitors (the spectral-norm product, and both the dense-only and convolutional versions of the SDP ancestor, including a split version of each in which large nets are cut at pooling layers and the piece bounds multiplied) and against the best available when the tightest method runs out of memory; wall-clock seconds to solve, swept across depth and width and across the number of layers merged into a subnetwork; and the solver's own failure mode (out-of-memory) recorded as a dash rather than as a timeout. No statistical treatment: single runs, one solver, one machine, no seeds, no error bars. Downstream metrics are not the object of study - clean test accuracy is printed next to each model only so the reader knows what was analysed.

## What they found
The chain-of-small-inequalities formulation produces the same bound as the single large convolutional SDP while being far cheaper, and the gap widens with depth: on the 16-layer backbone the two methods agree exactly and the layer-wise version costs a fraction of the time, and the number of constraints can be varied from one to sixteen with the bound unchanged. Against the dense-only ancestor the win is dramatic where the ancestor can run at all - on the smallest backbone it is more than three orders of magnitude slower - and beyond that it simply cannot be run on these nets. Tightness cuts the other way: on the same backbones the dense-only ancestor, applied to a convolution recast as a vast sparse matrix, gives *smaller* bounds, and the paper attributes the loss to its own 2-D systems treatment and says so explicitly. Across the architecture table, tightness and speed are traded per architecture rather than held simultaneously: the layered SDP wins the bound on several nets (LeNet on MNIST, the 2-conv/2-dense net, the fully-connected residual net) at times from a minute to fifteen minutes, loses to a split version of the dense ancestor on the convolutional residual net (a bound around three times better at roughly eighty thousand seconds against about a hundred), and cannot be run at all on two of the larger nets, where a split of itself supplies the best bound. The realised bounds are sobering everywhere: the state-of-the-art method's best bound on the 2-conv/2-dense net is about seven, on the 4-conv/3-dense net over half a million, and on the convolutional residual net over a hundred million, against the norm product's tenth of a billion - i.e. on the deeper nets the tightest available certificate is within a small factor of the bound that the field describes as useless. The paper's own diagnosis of the loss is that representing a convolution as a 2-D system is conservative and that handling max pooling through quadratic constraints is probably also conservative.

## Limits and open problems they name
The relaxation has three named sources of slack - the quadratic (rather than arbitrary) storage functions, the layer-specific structural restrictions on those functions, and the cut-off error from analysing a finite image as if it were an infinite signal - and the third is noted to be a special case of the second. The bound is not monotone in the amount of splitting: splitting buys feasibility and sometimes costs tightness, and the paper has no criterion for where to cut beyond "before memory runs out", so the split configurations are hand-chosen per architecture and are published as tables rather than derived. Convolution-as-2-D-system is conservative in a way the paper measures but does not fix, and max pooling is flagged as probably mishandled without any alternative tried. Synthesis - designing networks whose weights come out of these inequalities - is deferred as future work, which means the entire family is still a measuring instrument and not a training method.

## What we take from it
Three things. First, a **method** shape we can reuse directly: the bound is obtained by a backward recursion over layer boundaries carrying a *metric* rather than a scalar, and the metric is the only object the network's weights enter through. That is a compositional read-out of the weights that predicts a property of the whole map, produced without any optimisation over inputs - which is exactly the kind of predictive instrument our account needs, and it is layer-decomposable in a way the parameterised constructions are not: the slack at boundary k is a per-layer object, so "which layer is carrying the slack" is answerable in principle even though this paper never asks. Second, a **warning** with teeth: on the deeper nets the tightest known certificate is a small factor away from the spectral-norm product, and both are hundreds of thousands to hundreds of millions, so any account that reports "the Lipschitz constant of this trained net" and means a certificate is reporting a number whose usefulness depends entirely on the scale at which it was computed. Third, an **off-by-one-method detail worth copying**: this paper's ancestor was corrected for using a full matrix multiplier in a quadratic constraint that only holds with a diagonal one, and the corrected version yields tighter bounds than the original publication - a reminder that in this literature a tightening sometimes comes from fixing a proof, not from a new idea, and that the corrected form should be the baseline in any comparison we make. The citation trail also names the two papers that would complete this thread for us: the state-space realisation of convolutions that Lemma 1 rests on, and the paper that extends the quadratic-constraint library beyond slope-restricted activations.
