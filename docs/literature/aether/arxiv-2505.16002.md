## What they wanted, and why

Linguistic theory largely assumes that superficially different English filler–gap constructions — wh-questions, relative clauses, clefts, pseudo-clefts, topicalization — share an underlying mechanism, and that this shared structure is the reason they behave alike in processing. Behavioural work on language models has tested the *outputs* of these constructions but not the mechanisms producing them, so it could not say whether a model reaches the same behaviour through one mechanism or seven. The authors want to ask the question at the level linguistics actually talks about: do the models' internal causal mechanisms for these constructions coincide, and if they only partly coincide, which linguistic properties predict where the overlap holds? A second aim is methodological — to show that mechanism-level analysis of models is evidence a linguist can use, not just a benchmark score, and that it can generate hypotheses the linguistic literature has not posed.

## What they studied

Pythia models at 1.4B, 2.8B and 6.9B parameters (open weights, open data, public checkpoints), with the 1.4B results in the main text and the larger two in an appendix. Seven filler–gap constructions, each instantiated from four templated variants: two clause depths (one or two clauses between filler and gap) crossed with two animacies of the extracted element (animate, inanimate), giving 400-sentence balanced training sets of sentence plus minimal pair. Two control tasks are designed to be adjacent but not filler–gap: subject–verb number agreement over a similar distance (same distance, no long dependency), and a transitive-versus-intransitive continuation prediction whose lexical items at filler, article and noun-phrase positions are faux-contrasts, so the surface tokens offer no signal. The unit of analysis is a **learned linear direction inside one Transformer block's output at one position** — that is, a per-position, per-layer distributed feature, not a head, neuron or whole circuit.

## How they did it

They use one-dimensional Distributed Alignment Search with the model frozen, following the variant Arora et al. used. An intervention takes a base representation `b` and a source representation `s` and replaces the component of `b` along a learned unit direction `a` with the corresponding component of `s`, leaving the orthogonal remainder intact; `a` is the only trained parameter, fit by cross-entropy on interventions where the correct continuation is the source sentence's. Training runs at every position from the filler onward and at every layer. Two training regimes are run per construction: a *single-source* regime trained only on that construction, and a *leave-one-out* regime trained on all the others, so that evaluation on the excluded construction is transfer to a construction the direction never saw. Lexical boost is tested by crossing the animacy of the training and evaluation sets. Scoring uses the "odds" measure of Arora et al. — how much more probable the source-consistent label becomes relative to the base-consistent one after intervention — averaged over evaluation sets that are disjoint from training, then max-pooled over layers at each position and normalised by the trained-on value so that the reference condition sits at 1.00. Three experiments follow: (1) transfer across constructions of the same clausal depth, against the two controls; (2) the transfer matrix treated as a weighted directed graph, with in- and out-degree centrality as sink and source scores and a linear mixed-effects model over four linguistic properties read off each construction; (3) single-clause directions evaluated on the matrix and embedded clauses of the two-clause sentences. Regressions are `lmer` fits with maximal random slopes over training and evaluation construction, Holm–Bonferroni-corrected pairwise t-tests, and PCA on the transfer matrix at each position for a visual check.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **causal abstraction at the level of one syntactic property, validated by transfer**. The account is a single learned direction per construction and position that, when swapped between a minimal pair, carries the grammatical judgement with it; the claim about shared structure is that the direction fitted on one construction also produces the correct judgement on another it was never fitted to. It is partial by construction — nothing is said about where the direction comes from, what else it does, or how positions combine — and it is a prediction only in the narrow sense that a transferred direction makes a specific counterfactual label more likely than it would otherwise be. The linguistic content enters as four binary properties (filler class, head-child inversion, syntactic parent category, discourse-versus-necessity fronting), which are hypotheses about what makes one mechanism transferable to another rather than parts of the fitted model.

## How they judged success

The odds metric under intervention, expressed relative to the value on constructions the direction was trained on, with the two control tasks as the floors; positions are aggregated by max over layers because the authors want the most causally efficacious localisation. Statistical support is pairwise t-tests with Holm–Bonferroni correction for each test group against each control, at every position, plus linear mixed-effects regressions with maximal random-slope structure for the frequency, overlap, animacy and linguistic-property claims. Transfer-network source/sink status is measured as area under the in- and out-degree centrality curve as the edge-inclusion threshold is swept. Replication at two larger model sizes is reported with the same regressions in an appendix.

## What they found

Transfer is consistently strong: every non-control group sits above both controls at every position of the single-clause templates, and at nearly every position of the two-clause ones. Overlap between training and evaluation construction helps, and matching animacy helps more — in the leave-one-out regression the animacy coefficient is the larger of the two at three of four reported positions, and both are positive and significant. Frequency matters and in opposite directions: frequent constructions are poor sources and infrequent ones are poor sinks (negative frequency–in-degree, weak positive frequency–out-degree), with two exceptions the authors argue are linguistic rather than noise — topicalization is low-frequency but also phrasally different from everything else and so receives little, while restrictive relative clauses are the most frequent construction and still export little because they are the only one embedded under a noun phrase and the only one fronting by necessity. The other three properties come out of the regression with significant positive effects at most positions (syntactic parent category at all four). Transfer does not cross clause boundaries cleanly: single-clause directions transfer into the matrix clause's first positions and into the embedded clause's final verb, but drop at the matrix verb and are weak in between, which the authors attribute to the left-to-right model not knowing a clause is embedded until it meets an embedding verb. Larger models replicate the pattern of significances.

## Limits and open problems they name

What counts as rule-like behaviour in a neural system is unresolved, so a partial-transfer result can be read either as noisy human-like structure or as fuzzy constructs in humans too; the paper does not adjudicate. Frequency is shown to matter but not shown to be the only driver of source strength, and inductive biases of the Transformer are not ruled out as a cause. Everything is English, on templates that differ systematically from natural sentences, and the matched-pair constraint is why naturalistic data were not used. Recorded here rather than by the authors: the odds metric is max-pooled over layers and normalised by the trained-on value, so it is a relative score with no external unit; the directions are fitted with supervision from minimal pairs, so a transferred success is evidence of a shared *learnable* direction rather than of a shared direction the model actually uses; and the animacy effect is measured on sentences where animacy also changes the wh-word, so the lexical and the animacy accounts of that coefficient are not separated.

## What we take from it

A **measure** and a **framing**. The measure is transfer-as-score: fit a mechanism on one task, evaluate it on a different task it never saw, and report that number relative to the fitted-on value with the two adjacent-but-different controls underneath — this is the folder's held-out-intervention discipline applied at construction level, and it is the cheapest possible test of whether two things share a mechanism. The framing is that a shared structure claim should be a *matrix*, not a number: the full source-by-target matrix carries which construction is a source, which is a sink, and which pairs are isolated, and any single aggregate hides exactly the finding (topicalization's asymmetry, restrictive relative clauses' isolation). Two warnings transfer directly. The controls chosen here are the right shape for our own batteries — a task with the same distance but not the same dependency, and a task with the same predicted label but no real contrast — because only the pair of them rules out distractor explanations separately. And the clause-boundary result is the sharpest negative result in the paper: an account that transfers within a family can still fail at a structural boundary that the model cannot see in advance, which is a reason to test our read-outs at the positions where the model's information changes, not only at the positions where the phenomenon is realised.
