## What they wanted, and why
Causal discovery with interventions still runs the classical loop — infer a model, design a probe from it, run the probe — and each half is expensive: inference means searching graph space under a likelihood, and design means estimating information gain, which needs the very likelihood that empirical settings such as gene regulation do not give you. The authors want to break the loop by *amortising* the design half: train one network offline that, given the history of what has been observed and interfered with so far, emits the next intervention in a single forward pass, with no graph fitting at test time. They insist they are not proposing a new discovery method, only a new way to choose experiments, and they frame the target as sample efficiency under a fixed count of experiments. The larger goal is lab-in-the-loop design for single-cell perturbation screens, where one trained policy could serve several cell lines and platforms.

## What they studied
Linear additive-noise structural causal models with Gaussian and Gumbel noise and a heteroskedastic variant, plus SERGIO, a differential-equation single-cell expression simulator with realistic scRNA-seq technical noise (dropouts, library-size effects, outliers; roughly three quarters of entries missing). Both design environments are 10 variables with 50 initial observational samples and a budget of 10 interventions. The unit of action is the intervention itself — which variables to perturb and, in the synthetic domain, with what value — and the unit of judgement is the adjacency matrix recovered by a separate amortised posterior network. Both are evaluated over 100 randomly drawn test environments with parameters never seen in training, plus out-of-distribution settings that change the graph prior (Erdős–Rényi to scale-free), the mechanism prior, the noise distribution, the intervention type, or the variable count.

## How they did it
The loop is posed as a hidden-parameter Markov decision process whose hidden parameters are the true graph and its mechanism parameters: the state is the accumulated history of samples plus their intervention labels, the action is an intervention, and each simulated step draws data under that intervention through a simulator (the prior can be sampled because training is in silico). Rewards come from a *frozen* pretrained amortised graph posterior (AVICI, the same alternating-attention transformer used for discovery): the reward for a step is the rise in the expected number of correctly predicted adjacency entries after that step, so with no discounting the cumulative reward telescopes into the accuracy of the final posterior alone. The policy is a transformer that applies self-attention alternately over the variable axis and the sample axis, pools over samples, and outputs the parameters of a squashed Gaussian over targets and values, thresholded to a binary target mask; because the attention pattern is permutation-equivariant in variables and permutation-invariant in samples, the same weights transfer to reordered and differently sized problems. It is trained off-policy with soft actor-critic in its randomised-ensembled variant, using several Q-networks with the same equivariant encoder. Success is reported as the cumulative reward (returns) on 100 test environments, backed by structural Hamming distance, edge F1 and area under the precision–recall curve, and compared against random interventions, extra observational sampling, and two likelihood-based design methods that fit a bootstrapped equivalence-class posterior at every step.

## What counts as an explanation here
Neither a model of the system nor a certificate — a *policy*. The thing produced is a mapping from history to next experiment, learned rather than solved, and the paper is explicit that it gives no account of why a particular probe was chosen; the understanding on offer is the reward's connection to expected information gain, namely that a lower bound on multi-step expected information gain about the graph follows from substituting an approximate posterior for the true one, and that the concrete reward they train on is that bound with log-likelihood replaced by per-entry accuracy. It is a partial, predictive-in-the-weak-sense object: it predicts nothing about the network's internals, only about which probe would reduce an external estimator's error most.

## How they judged success
Returns (cumulative reward, undiscounted) as the headline, with the posterior's structural Hamming distance, edge F1 and AUPRC as corroborating metrics (the paper states that the metrics are largely correlated in its results). Every setting is 100 random test environments with 95% confidence bands. The comparisons are random interventions, observational-only, and two likelihood-based design baselines; the baselines needing a data likelihood were omitted where none exists. Out-of-distribution tests are zero-shot, i.e. obtained by a single forward pass of the trained policy with no adaptation. Training cost is reported separately (3 A100 GPUs for 3 days for the policy, about 216 GPU-hours, with 70,000 hours in total including tuning), against test-time cost of seconds on a CPU.

## What they found
On the synthetic domain the policy's returns sit near 76 after the 10 interventions against roughly 72 for random, and it beats both likelihood-based design baselines in-distribution and in the shifted settings; it also survives a switch from perfect to shift interventions and from homoskedastic to heteroskedastic noise. On SERGIO it overtakes random after about five acquisitions in-distribution and holds up zero-shot when the sequencing platform changes, the intervention becomes a knockdown rather than a knockout, or the knockouts are noisy (a tenth of the time missing or off-target, with the policy not told). Two dissociations are the substance. First, the advantage shrinks as the problem grows: the relative gain over random falls monotonically with variable count up to thirty while staying positive. Second, and the authors say so themselves, at higher dimension on the single-cell environment the policy is *on par* with random rather than better, and their explanation is that with most of the data missing the permutation symmetries the network relies on buy less. When the intervention type changes on the gene environment the random arm stays competitive, and the paper notes that in some shifted settings the policy's returns first dip while it explores before overtaking random.

## Limits and open problems they name
The policy inherits the training distribution: zero-shot size generalisation beyond the trained width is weak in the high-missingness domain, and the authors disclaim any claim of superiority over random there. The reward is defined through a *separate* learned posterior, so the policy's ceiling is that estimator's accuracy, and the objective covers the graph only, not the mechanism parameters. Training requires a simulator able to sample from a prior over graphs, which many real settings do not have. They also name as future work larger batch sizes and reuse of real offline data in design, neither of which is attempted.

## What we take from it
Three things, in order of value. (1) A *reward-typing* move we can copy cheaply: scoring a probe by the rise in a fixed estimator's per-entry accuracy is a tractable stand-in for expected information gain, but it is also a candidate trap, because the designer then maximises exactly the number that will be used to grade it — the paper is careful to freeze the estimator during policy training yet its headline metric is still the same estimator's output. Every score we publish should therefore pair a probe plan with a *different* estimator than the one it was optimised against, or state that the two coincide. (2) The *transfer* result is the interesting empirical finding for us, with its sign: a design policy transfers across priors, mechanisms, noise, intervention type and platform, and its advantage over random decays with problem size and can vanish outright under heavy missingness — so "generalises zero-shot" is not the claim to make, "was better than random by a shrinking margin" is. (3) A concrete warning about symmetries: an architecture whose inductive bias is permutation equivariance over samples and variables is worth exactly as much as the data is informative per entry, and degrades to a random-orderish policy where most entries are missing — the same caution applies to any learned probe selector we build on masked or sparse observations, and argues for reporting the missing-data rate next to the comparison rather than in an appendix.
