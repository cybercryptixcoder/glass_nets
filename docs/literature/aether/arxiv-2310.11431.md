## What they wanted, and why
Two things are needed before anyone can claim a representation has been decomposed into features: a way to say how interpretable a direction is that does not require one researcher staring at pictures, and a way to find directions more interpretable than the individual neurons. The authors want both, and they want the answer to hold on brains as well as on networks, since the superposition hypothesis makes no claim that is specific to gradient descent. The larger goal is automated interpretability for vision models — the image-domain analogue of what language-model researchers had started doing with dictionaries — validated against human judgement rather than assembled by hand. A secondary motivation is to adjudicate a disagreement in neuroscience: earlier work on face-patch neurons argued for disentangled single-unit codes, and this paper asks whether the same data read through directions instead says something else.

## What they studied
Unit of analysis: **directions** in the activation space of a layer, compared against the canonical basis (individual neurons / units) of that same space. In artificial systems: a ResNet50 trained on CIFAR-10, with analyses focused on its first PyTorch module (256 units), plus untrained instantiations of the same models as a control. In biological systems: three public recording datasets — 449 IT-cortex sites responding to 1,379 images (447 faces, 932 non-face objects) from Vinken et al.; 159 neurons in macaque anterior-medial face area presented with 2,100 greyscale human and monkey faces from Higgins et al.; and the Allen et al. 7T fMRI dataset, 8 human subjects, over 16 brain areas, 9,000-10,000 natural images across 30-40 scan sessions. Comparison methods for finding directions: PCA, ICA, NMF, K-Means with cosine similarity, and a single-layer L1-penalised sparse autoencoder in the style of Sharkey et al.

## How they did it
The pipeline is three steps and is the whole method. (1) Push images through the network up to the layer of interest and, for convolutional layers, average activations across space, giving one point in R^N per image. (2) Produce candidate directions — the basis vectors themselves, or the output of the decomposition algorithm. (3) Score each direction, and this is the contribution: take the images that most excite the direction (maximally exciting images, MEIs), rank all dataset images by the direction's response, keep the top five, and compute the average pairwise similarity among those five across all five-by-five pairs. That average is the interpretability index (II). Similarity can be low-level (mean colour difference per channel), mid-level (LPIPS, a perceptual distance computed from feature maps of a pretrained VGG or AlexNet across several layers) or high-level (same CIFAR-10 label or not). To validate the index, they replicate a large paid crowdsourced psychophysics experiment in silico: humans had been shown nine MEIs for a neuron and asked which of two query images also strongly drives it; the automated version replaces the human with a maximum-similarity rule over the same nine MEIs, and agreement is scored with AUC against the human answers. Candidate directions are then compared on II and on in-silico psychophysics accuracy, with task difficulty varied by restricting query and reference images to a narrow central quantile band of activation (e.g. the 45th to 55th percentile) rather than the extremes. For pairs, they add two z-scored neuron responses and define synergy as the pair's II minus the larger of the two single-neuron IIs, computing it for every pair in a layer. The same code runs on the three neural datasets, with voxels or sites in place of units, label structure supplied by the image conditions, and K-Means centroids transferred to a target recording by recomputing centroids under the source clustering labels.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **direction plus a number**: an interpretable feature is a vector in activation space, and its interpretability is a scalar computed from the images it maximises. There is no feature caption, no reconstruction claim, no circuit, and no attempt to say what the model computes — the account is deliberately partial and is a measurement rather than a mechanism. The paper is explicit that the notion of feature it uses is pragmatic, tied to human discriminability measured by psychophysics, and it cites the formal definition of a feature elsewhere rather than adopting one. What it predicts is conditional on the metric: that a high-II direction will look coherent to a human observer, that its responses are more predictable by a psychophysics-style query, and that it will be more sensitive to some kinds of perturbation than others. It does not predict activations from weights, and nothing in the account maps back to the model's parameters.

## How they judged success
The primary validation is human agreement: AUC between in-silico and human psychophysics decisions on the Zimmermann et al. neuron-interpretability data, per layer. Directions are then ranked by comparing distributions of II across methods (K-Means with K in {128, 256, 512} in a 256-unit layer, so both narrower and wider than the basis), across LPIPS layers used inside the metric, and on subsets of neurons split at the median II into "interpretable" and "uninterpretable" halves to check the method is not merely re-finding the good neurons. In-silico psychophysics accuracy is the second measure, swept over task difficulty and over the three semantic levels. Qualitative checks are done by quantile — figures show five neurons and five directions sampled at five II quantiles rather than the best examples. Biological datasets reuse the same three measures. Appendices add: a sparse-autoencoder baseline swept over hidden width and L1 penalty, trained 200 epochs on 50,000 activations with Adam at 1e-3; a manifold-sparsity analysis using t-SNE embeddings of pairwise LPIPS distances among MEIs; a sensitivity analysis interpolating and extrapolating between pairs of centroids (chosen by the Hungarian algorithm as maximally separated) with the input-gradient norm measured along the path, plus a Gaussian-noise robustness test at sigma up to 0.1; a co-activation correlation against II as a test of the privileged-basis account; a spectral analysis of the activation covariance in the style of Stringer et al.; and the untrained-model control.

## What they found
The automated metric is a fair but not strong stand-in for humans: AUC reaches up to 0.71 on the early ResNet50 layers, agreement is good for layers 1 and 2 and weakens deeper (that layer refers to PyTorch module names, layers 10 and 23), and the same early-layer-only pattern holds for CLIP-ResNet50 and GoogLeNet in the appendix — so all further analysis is confined to layer 1 of the CIFAR-10 ResNet50. On that layer, K-Means directions beat the neuron basis on II regardless of which LPIPS layer defines similarity, and K-Means is the best method overall with mean II of −0.0159 against a best baseline of −0.0188; the index is in arbitrary units and the numbers are close, so the ordering is the finding rather than the gap. The number of clusters does not change II in the regime tested, which is why K = N = 256 is used for a matched comparison. K-Means run separately on the below-median and above-median halves raises II in both, so the effect is not just picking out the already-good neurons. In-silico psychophysics accuracy is higher for directions than for neurons across low, mid and high semantic levels and across difficulty; the advantage is largest for colour and shrinks as the semantic level rises. For pairs, most additions are worse than the better member (the synergy histogram is skewed negative), but a substantial minority improve on it; correlated pairs are more synergistic without correlation explaining everything; and the more interpretable a neuron is, the lower its best achievable synergy, consistent with a neuron that already carries a clean feature being diluted by any partner. All three patterns — the MEI gain, the psychophysics gain, and the synergy triad — reproduce on the IT dataset, the AM face-patch dataset, and across brain areas and sessions in the 7T data, with low-level visual areas gaining more on the colour metric and high-level areas more on the label metric. Two extra results cut against easy readings. Directions transfer across subjects and across scan sessions better than units do, which is offered as a universality result and sits alongside representational drift. And on the 400 beta-VAE models of the Higgins et al. setup, the K-Means features score higher on DCI and MCC disentanglement than the individual neurons do, reversing the original paper's single-unit reading of the same recordings — though the authors note interpretability and disentanglement are logically separable, one being source recovery under ICA assumptions and the other a function of human semantics, and that their obtained relationship is offered as a hypothesis. The sparse-autoencoder comparison reproduces the sparsity-versus-source-recovery curve of Sharkey et al. from II alone, and the SAE beats the neuron basis but loses to K-Means. On the anti-shortcut control, the interpretability gap between units and clusters exists at initialisation, before any training; what training changes is the semantic level of the result, moving from colour-predictable at init to label-predictable after training. Interpretable directions are also less sensitive at their preferred inputs (a weak but significant negative rank correlation between II and minimal input-gradient norm) and slightly more robust to additive Gaussian noise.

## Limits and open problems they name
The validation is confined to early layers, and they say plainly that this is because the metric is only trustworthy there — so every claim about later-layer features, where mixed selectivity is supposed to bite hardest, is untested by their own human-agreement standard. The AUC of 0.71 is described as a first proxy with room for improvement. The interpretability index is in arbitrary units, its magnitudes are not comparable across datasets, and K-Means was chosen over an SAE largely because measured on this index it won rather than because the account explains why. The directions are cluster centroids in the input-mean-averaged activation space of a convolutional layer, so each is an average over space and time; the paper does not claim a centroid is what the unit computes. The biological results read voxels and sites into the same framework, and the authors concede that an fMRI voxel already mixes many neurons and has its own filter, so the "superposition" claim in the brain is an inference from an analogy rather than a direct observation. Disentanglement and interpretability are declared separable in principle and then shown to correlate in one dataset, which is left as a hypothesis. The translation-invariance and pooling of a convolutional layer, the use of a single model instance per analysis, and the absence of an intervention that edits a direction and predicts the output are all outside the paper.

## What we take from it
Three things, one of them a shortcut worth adopting and two warnings.
1. **A dictionary-free control for any "our basis is more interpretable than neurons" claim.** K-Means on activations is not a straw man here — it beat a sparse autoencoder on the authors' own interpretability metric at the same width. Any entry in this folder that reports a learned dictionary beating the basis should carry a K-Means or PCA row, because the cheapest explanation for a dictionary's win is that *any* set of overcomplete directions does better than the axes; entry `arxiv-1610.01644`'s untrained-network baseline and entry `arxiv-2305.01610`'s random-rotation control are the same instinct, applied to training and to the coordinate system rather than to the method family.
2. **The measure is a candidate but is not transferable to our setting as-is.** II needs top-M maximally exciting *real examples* and an image similarity metric; the language-model analogue requires a choice of similarity on text and a decision about what counts as the same concept, which is exactly the open failure mode of automated-interpretability scoring that entries `arxiv-2411.13117` and `arxiv-2606.24026` report. What *is* portable is the validation protocol: score your metric against human judgements, report per-layer agreement, and restrict claims to the layers where agreement was demonstrated. Their honest concession that the metric is only trusted in early layers is the reporting convention our own accounts should copy — a metric's validity has a domain and should be printed with it.
3. **The untrained control reframes what "the geometry comes from the data" means.** The unit-versus-direction gap is present at initialisation; training changes *which* semantic level the directions align with, not whether they exist. For a white-box model this says the interesting object learned by training is the *mapping from directions to concepts*, not the direction set itself — and it puts a hard constraint on any claim that a probe structure reveals something the network learned rather than something the input distribution already had. Coreference `arxiv-1610.01644` (untrained networks matching trained ones on structural measures) and `arxiv-2410.10811` (unlearned readers matching learned ones). The synergy result is the third warning: pairs of individually good units can be worse than either, and a pair's interpretability is not predictable from its members' correlation, so any account that composes features from a per-unit reading needs a synergy term and a measured pairwise check, not an additive assumption — which is the same lesson as `arxiv-2610.21261`'s interaction-order residual arriving from the biology side.
