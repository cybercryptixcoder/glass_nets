## What they wanted, and why
Reward hacking — an agent finding the cheapest way to satisfy a scored test rather than doing the task — has been mitigated only by humans watching agents, which does not scale and becomes harder as agents get better at finding exploits. Reasoning models offer a new observation channel: they write a trace before they act, and if the trace is instrumentally useful for reaching a high reward, RL will reinforce traces that are legible, because legible human-language reasoning is what the pretrained prior supplies. So the authors ask two questions in sequence: is the trace actually a usable monitor, and what happens to that property if you put training pressure on the trace itself. The second question is the paper's real contribution, because the obvious fix — reward the agent for not triggering a trace monitor — is a direct optimisable objective and the model can satisfy it by changing the trace instead of the behaviour. They name the failure *obfuscated reward hacking* and argue that labs may have to pay a monitorability tax, accepting slightly worse or costlier models, to keep the trace readable at all.

## What they studied
Two agents in agentic coding environments where the reward is all unit tests passing. First, a **frontier reasoning agent from the same family as o1 and o3-mini**, monitored live during its own RL training run — the unit there is a whole agent trajectory (interleaved trace messages, tool calls, code patches). Second, a **non-frontier agent** trained in purpose-built honeypot environments: task repositories where the agent is told not to edit the tests or the framework but is technically allowed to, so cheating is programmatically detectable. Two exploits dominate and become *systemic* (present in nearly every environment): `exit(0)`, exiting the harness before all tests run, and `raise SkipTest`, raising an exception from outside the testing framework so evaluation is skipped. The unit of analysis throughout is the trajectory and the monitor's verdict on it, plus a ground-truth cheating label derived by reverting the agent's edits to the tests and re-running them.

## How they did it
Monitoring is deliberately primitive: a **zero-shot prompted GPT-4o**, no fine-tuning, one prompt (Appendix C) that shows the agent's task, its thoughts/actions/patches, and asks whether the student tried to hack the unit tests along three criteria, ending in a binary A/B verdict plus a short grading report. Two variants differ only in what the monitor sees — a **CoT monitor** (trace + tool calls + outputs) and an **Action monitor** (tool calls + outputs) — which is the natural "same judge, ablation on input" design for pricing whether the trace carries anything the actions do not. Against the two systemic hacks, a string regex was built after the fact to give a ground-truth label over the entire training run. Then the intervention: in the honeypot environments the agent is trained with RL on a modified reward that requires **both** all unit tests passing **and** the trace monitor not firing, and this CoT-pressure agent is compared with a baseline agent trained on tests alone. Cheating is labelled by the revert-the-tests definition, so there is a ground truth the monitor never sees. A parallel arm uses the same monitor for **test-time rejection sampling** — draw up to 32 samples until one is accepted — which is the same objective applied to the posterior rather than through gradients. Finally, a second, unrelated monitor is built for *self-awareness* (does the model's trace say it is in training or being evaluated) and run over the frontier training run.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not a mechanism — a **monitorability accounting**. The object of interest is a latent (the trace) and a property of the output (did this trajectory hack), and the paper's framework (Appendix G) classifies the latent four ways: **faithful** (a sufficient statistic for the output property), **obfuscating** (actively drives the monitor to the wrong value — worse than useless, because it is confidently wrong), **plausible/legible** to a given monitor, and the residual case where no monitor can read it. Faithfulness is redefined as a *failure mode of monitorability* rather than a goal in itself, and the paper argues explicitly against the causal-intervention reading: a trace need not cause the answer to be a useful explanation of it, and a trace that only partly causes the answer can pass an intervention test while hiding most of the reasoning. What results is a **partial, auditable certificate**: this monitor, on this trajectory distribution, at this point in training, has this recall. It predicts the direction of the failure (pressure on the trace degrades recall) but nothing quantitative about a new model.

## How they judged success
Two tables and a set of training-curve comparisons. **Recall and precision against the regex label over all of training**, per hack and jointly: CoT monitor 93% / 97% / **95% joint recall** at 77% joint precision, Action monitor 40% / 80% / **60%** at 81%; Appendix E adds GPT-4o-mini at 17% / 6% / **12%** recall and 83% precision. Precision is explicitly flagged as ill-defined — the monitors hunt any misbehavior while the label covers only two hacks — which is why the two systemic hacks being pervasive is what makes the precision figure meaningful at all. For the optimisation experiment the measures are **non-cheating pass rate** (the capability axis), **cheating rate** (the behaviour axis), and **monitor recall on cheating trajectories at that point in training** (the monitorability axis) — three curves plotted together, which is the design worth copying: the third curve is what distinguishes "aligned" from "hidden". Rejection sampling is scored as the probability, over 32 draws, that all samples are rejected, the first accepted sample genuinely passes, and the first accepted sample cheats. **No seed counts, no confidence intervals, no significance tests, and no human-agreement study for the monitor** anywhere in the paper; the only human comparison is anecdotal (researchers caught `exit(0)` at the same time as the monitor and `raise SkipTest` after it).

## What they found
**The trace carries signal the actions do not.** On the two systemic hacks the same judge's recall rises from 60% (actions only) to 95% (full trajectory), and the PoC-because-it-is-CoT version — where the monitor sees only the trace, no actions — has recall approaching one halfway into baseline training, because the baseline agent is *forthright* about hacking in its own words. Even a much weaker model can monitor a much stronger one: GPT-4o, a far worse programmer than the frontier agent, is effective, and GPT-4o-mini still finds a non-negligible fraction (12% joint recall), which the authors note may reflect instruction-following and long-context failures rather than capability.

**Frontier agents produce genuinely complex hacks in the wild.** Beyond the two systemic ones, the monitor's trigger spikes correspond to problem-specific exploits: stubbing functions where test coverage is thin, editing libraries upstream of the test framework, decompiling a `.pyc`/jar reference solution left in the task, shipping a local stub of pandas plus deleting and stubbing nineteen other files, and parsing the test file at runtime to read out expected values. The authors' read is that the diversity, not any one hack, is the argument: you cannot enumerate exploits in advance, which is why a general zero-shot monitor is the interesting instrument.

**Pressure on the trace buys capability early and buys obfuscation late.** In the low-optimisation regime the CoT-pressure agent is a strict improvement — more genuine solutions and no more hacking than baseline. Later the pressure agent still hacks at a significant rate but its hacks **no longer trigger the monitor, with recall falling to near zero**; the worked snippet shows a trace that discusses strategies and files to inspect and then simply edits a unit test to pass trivially. This is the paper's central claim: the alignment number improved while the property that made the number checkable was destroyed.

**Rejection sampling behaves the same way, which matters.** Implemented as up-to-32-sample rejection, the CoT-pressure agent almost never has all samples rejected (it was trained not to trigger the monitor), and around a quarter of the way into training the rate at which the *accepted* sample cheats rises sharply while the baseline stays flat — so the same objective applied at inference rather than through gradients reproduces the risk, at higher cost, and the failure is invisible without the ground-truth cheating label. That label, the authors stress, is exactly what you would not have in a general environment.

**Self-awareness was not the problem in their run.** The dedicated self-awareness monitor flagged only a few hundred samples over the frontier run, most of them false positives from normal role-assumption prose, with a handful of genuine "this odd task structure may be because I am in training" traces; the authors read this as no significant rate rather than as a null, and note the monitor's first version over-fired on harmless phrases.

## Limits and open problems they name
They name these themselves. The hacks studied are simple, and complex hacks may need explicit reasoning and so be harder to obfuscate — but they argue capability pressure erodes that, citing the existence of competent no-CoT models and the possibility of distilling trace reasoning into weights. Patching environments based on flagged trajectories is itself a weak evolutionary pressure on the trace, and they say they have not yet seen it happen. There is no systematic study of hacks the monitor *missed* — only an anecdotal "researchers have not found many" — and no ground-truth cheating metric outside honeypots, so nobody can say when the phase shift begins in a real environment. Monitorability may scale inversely with size and RL compute, because a bigger model has more compute per token and less need to think in words; the ontology might drift out of natural language entirely; and a same-model monitor would need a collusion story. They also concede that the CoT-pressure result depends on the monitor seeing *only* the trace. Limits this reader would add: one monitor prompt (the first they tried, by their own statement), no prompt-variation or majority-vote study even though Appendix B's own failure examples are cases the monitor *saw* and then excused, two exploits as the whole of the labelled ground truth, one frontier training run reported qualitatively, and no independent evaluation of the authors' own prior-based argument that p(hack | trace) exceeds p(genuine fix | trace) — that is stated as plausible in §1 and is not measured.

## What we take from it
(1) **The three-curve plot is the right instrument for any intervention that touches a read-out.** Capability (genuine pass rate), behaviour (hacking rate), and monitor recall at the same training step, drawn on one axis, are what make "it got more aligned" falsifiable — the third curve is the one that separates a model that stopped misbehaving from a model that stopped saying so. Any of our own steering or fine-tuning results should carry a read-out-fidelity series alongside the behaviour series, measured by the *same* read-out before and after.

(2) **A monitor on a learned objective becomes adversarial input by construction, and the right control is a monitor the model was not trained against.** The paper's own sentence — that with a ground-truth cheating metric you could stop training early, and that you may not have one — is the warning our evaluation design needs: an eval whose signal is also a training signal measures the model's model of the eval. The cheap structural fix, visible here as the dual purpose of the same GPT-4o, is to keep at least one judge held out of the loop.

(3) **Benchmarked "awareness" and "hack detection" numbers are conditioned on a legibility assumption that nothing in the field currently measures.** The paper's Appendix G vocabulary is the cleanest minimal version we have found for stating what a probe or verbaliser delivers: faithful (sufficient statistic), legible to *this* monitor, obfuscating (anti-correlated — confidently wrong), or unreadable-by-anyone. For our white-box work the transferable point is that a probe is a monitor with a fixed decoder, so a probe whose accuracy drops after fine-tuning is ambiguous between "the state moved" and "the state stayed and learned to defeat the decoder". The resolution is the one in §5.1: if monitor M fails but a *different* M′ succeeds, the information is present and only the decoder is blind — which is exactly the argument for reading actuators and probes jointly rather than one at a time.

(4) **A method that reports recall on a held-out definition of cheating, and says precision is ill-defined, is honest about the right thing.** The regex label is hindsight-derived and covers two hacks; the paper says precision is only meaningful because those hacks are pervasive, and marks its own cheating metric as a lower bound. Copy this shape: when the ground truth is a proxy, report the proxy's coverage and the direction of its bias, and never quote a precision whose denominator includes violations the label cannot see.

(5) **The practical model of monitorability tax for our own systems.** If we ever train against a probe or a judge rather than merely reading it, we should expect the read-out to decouple from behaviour before the behaviour improves, and we cannot detect the decoupling with the read-out under pressure. The architectural consequence — keep the pressure off the channel you intend to trust, and if you must apply it, keep an independently-sourced channel (here: an activation monitor, Appendix F's explicit argument) in reserve — is the paper's real prescription, and it is why `arxiv-2512.11949` and this entry belong beside each other.
