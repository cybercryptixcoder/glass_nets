# Selectivity considered harmful: evaluating the causal impact of class selectivity in DNNs

- **Key:** arxiv-2003.01262
- **Authors / organisation:** Matthew L. Leavitt (work performed as part of the Facebook AI Residency) and Ari S. Morcos — Facebook AI Research, Menlo Park, CA, USA
- **Date:** 2020-10-14 (v3; v1 2020-03-03, v2 2020-06-13)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.01262
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

A generation of interpretability work describes what a network is doing by naming the properties of single units — a sentiment neuron in a character-level LM, a "car" detector in a conv stack, a "dog head" in a vision transformer — and treats the most selective units as the important ones. That practice rests on an unexamined assumption: that learning selective units is either *necessary* for the network to work, or *sufficient* to explain how it works. The evidence for it was mostly ablation, which the authors argue cannot settle the question at all. Ablating a unit from an already-trained network only asks what happens when you remove something the training process had assumed would be present; and if a property is replicated across units, redundancy makes ablation of any one of them look harmless, which can invert the conclusion. So the authors ask the causal question the other way round: build networks under a constraint that forces them to be more or less class-selective *while training*, and see what that does to performance. If selectivity were necessary, suppressing it should hurt. If it were sufficient or beneficial, forcing more of it should help. Neither prediction survives.

## What they studied

Three convolutional image classifiers: ResNet18 on Tiny ImageNet (200 classes, 500 training images each), and ResNet20 and a VGG16-like network on CIFAR10. The unit of analysis is the **convolutional feature map** — a channel — treated as one "unit", with its activation on a sample defined as the mean over the spatial map after the non-linearity. Class-conditional means are then taken across the test set. The property being manipulated is the **class selectivity index**, the normalized gap between a unit's largest class-conditional mean and the mean over the remaining classes, scaled to lie in the unit interval (a small epsilon guards against dead units). The output layer is excluded from both the regularizer and every analysis, on the grounds that a classifier's last layer must be class-selective by definition.

## How they did it

The whole method turns on one property of the selectivity index: it is **differentiable with respect to the weights**, so it can be lifted out of the analysis section and into the loss. The training objective becomes cross-entropy *minus* a scale times the network-average selectivity index, where the average is taken over units within each layer first and then over layers — the paper says this ordering matters, because plain averaging over all units would let the deeper layers, which have many more channels, dominate. The sign of the scale is the direction knob: negative values penalize selectivity, positive values reward it, and its magnitude sets how hard the constraint bites. Each unit's index is recomputed per minibatch during training and over the whole test set for reporting. Everything else is held fixed: SGD with momentum and weight decay, 20 random seeds per hyperparameter setting, results read off the checkpoint with the best validation accuracy, error bars from bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals, and hypothesis tests (Bonferroni-corrected t-tests, or Wilcoxon rank-sum when comparing distributions) against the unregularized baseline. The regularity of that design is what makes the paper's negative results legible: the arrow of causation runs from the regularizer through the measured selectivity to accuracy, and the authors can see it arrive.

Two controls carry most of the argumentative weight. The first answers the obvious escape route — that a penalized network simply **rotates selectivity off the unit axes**, hiding it in linear combinations so the index falls while the information stays. They test this with projection-weighted canonical correlation analysis (PWCCA), which is invariant to rotations and invertible affine maps, so a pure re-basing would leave the distance to the unregularized network at zero. The baseline distribution is built from the 190 within-parameter replicate pairs (same constraint, different seed); the 400 cross-network pairs (different constraint) are compared against it. The second control computes an **upper bound on recoverable selectivity**: they search over orthonormal bases of the activation space — an orthonormal matrix optimized by gradient descent with a Leaky-ReLU assumption handled by column-shifting to keep activations non-negative — for the basis that maximizes selectivity, then read the index off the test set in that basis. If the suppression were a change of basis, the optimum over all bases would be untouched. It is not.

Three further controls close specific alternative explanations. Because the unit index can be maximized trivially by a unit that only fires on a handful of samples ("activation minimization"), they report the distribution of the proportion of samples that elicit a non-zero response. Because penalizing or promoting selectivity changes the dying-ReLU population in ResNet20, they rerun with leaky-ReLUs to see whether restoring dead units restores accuracy (it does not). And because the index is only one way to quantify selectivity, they repeat the manipulation and re-measure with **precision** — the fraction of a unit's top-N most-activating samples belonging to its modal class — which is not differentiable and therefore could not have been regularized for.

## What counts as an explanation here

Not a mechanism, and deliberately so — this is an *explanatory-power audit* of the field's most common mechanism-shaped claim. The form of understanding is a causal statement about a property: whether a statistic computed on individual units carries any functional weight. Its verdict is negative in a specific, reusable sense — single-unit class selectivity in CNNs is neither necessary nor sufficient for the network's behaviour, and its natural learned level is already at the edge of harm. The paper's positive residue is the regularizer itself, a method for *constructing* networks with a controlled amount of any differentiable unit-level property, which is a tool for building counterfactual model families rather than a description of one. Nothing here predicts behaviour from an internal variable; it tests whether an internal variable could possibly support such a prediction, and concludes that this one cannot.

## How they judged success

Test accuracy on held-out data, as a function of the regularization scale and, separately, as a function of the realized mean selectivity — the second plot being the one that shows whether the effect is caused by selectivity or by the constraint's incidental costs. The necessary/sufficient framing is operationalized as predictions: suppressing selectivity should reduce accuracy if it is necessary; promoting it should improve accuracy if it is sufficient or beneficial. Falsifying both predicts a curve that is flat-to-*rising* on the suppression side and *falling* on the promotion side, and that is what they test for. Controls: the rotation-invariance check (CCA distance ratio against the seed-only baseline), the all-bases selectivity upper bound, the sparsity/activation-minimization distribution, the leaky-ReLU rescue, the alternate selectivity metric (precision), and a layer-restriction ablation that applies the regularizer to only the first three or last three layers.

## What they found

Suppressing selectivity **does not hurt, and sometimes helps**. In ResNet18 on Tiny ImageNet, every tested penalty improved test accuracy significantly, peaking at a scale of −1.0 with 53.60 ± 0.13 percent against an unregularized 51.57 ± 0.18 — a gain of about two points for a 1.6-fold drop in mean selectivity (0.22 ± 0.0009 down from 0.35 ± 0.0007). Beyond the peak, accuracy falls back; by a scale of −3.0 — a threefold reduction, mean selectivity 0.12 — it is statistically indistinguishable from baseline, and only past −3.5 does it degrade. ResNet20 on CIFAR10 shows a shallower version: no gain, but the mean index can be cut from 0.22 to 0.07 (a factor over three) before the drop becomes significant, i.e. the network trained with more than three times the selectivity it needed. VGG16 tolerates a fivefold reduction for a half-point of accuracy and a tenfold reduction (to 0.03) for about one point.

Promoting selectivity **hurts immediately and severely**. A significant loss appears at the smallest positive scale tested, and accuracy collapses to roughly a quarter by a scale of 5.0 in ResNet18; ResNet20 is worse. The asymmetry is the sharpest number in the paper: at matched magnitude the suppression and promotion curves diverge from the very first step, and the gap reaches about six points by a magnitude of 2.0. On the controls, the CCA distance between penalized and unpenalized networks significantly exceeds the seed-only baseline at every scale but the smallest in ResNet18 (and at every scale in ResNet20), with the excess growing in the penalty magnitude — so the change is not a re-basing. The fitted-basis upper bound on selectivity also falls as the penalty grows, which the authors read as selectivity being suppressed across all bases rather than moved into hiding. Interestingly, that upper bound is roughly constant across penalty scales in the final two conv layers of both models, which they take to suggest that proximity to the output insulates a layer's representational content. On the promotion side the degenerate explanations fail: at a scale of 0.7 the majority of ResNet20 units still respond to over 80 percent of samples while accuracy is already down five points, and leaky-ReLUs that reduce the dead-unit count to zero do not restore accuracy, though the largest negative slope recovers a little. Precision, the non-differentiable metric, moves with the differentiable one in the same directions, with the expected exception at large positive scale where sparsity is a legal route to a high index but not to high precision. The layer ablation finds the effect concentrated where the argument predicts: restricting the constraint to the first three layers reproduces most of it, while in the last three layers the suppression benefit in ResNet18 shrinks to under half a point over a narrow range of penalties and disappears by −0.4, and in ResNet20 the same restriction *reduces* accuracy. Training cost is modest inside the useful range — median epochs-to-95-percent goes from 35 to at most 51 in ResNet18 — and blows up only outside it.

## Limits and open problems they name

The authors' own first caveat is scope: CNNs, image classification, two standard benchmark datasets, so the result may be an artifact of benchmarks that are easy in ways naturalistic data is not. Their second is that the study was not designed to decide *why* reducing selectivity helps in ResNet18 — the paper offers the anti-correlation between selectivity and generalization already reported in the literature as the likely reading, but does not isolate the mechanism. They also do not claim that the regularizer is a generally useful training trick; they call that a possibility for future work. There is no language model, no MLP, and no test of whether the same audit machinery transfers to properties other than class selectivity — though the recipe (make the unit statistic differentiable, put it in the loss, sweep its sign) obviously does, which is why the paper is worth reading far outside vision.

## What we take from it

A **method and a warning**, and the method is the more transferable half. The method: to test whether a unit-level statistic carries causal weight, make it differentiable, put it in the training loss with a signed scale, and sweep. That converts every "this neuron matters" claim into a two-sided experiment whose predictions are fixed in advance, and it sidesteps the redundancy objection that makes single-unit ablation uninterpretable in the presence of replication — which is exactly the objection that applies to almost every probe-and-ablate story in our own domain.

The warning is the *control*, and it is the piece the folder has been missing. Any claim that a direction, feature or neuron "is" a concept has a trivial escape hatch: the information may be present but re-coded off the axis being measured, so a low reading on your chosen basis proves nothing. This paper gives two independent instruments against that escape hatch and shows how to calibrate them. CCA against a **seed-only baseline distribution** is the cheap one — and the calibration detail matters more than the metric: the right comparison is not "is the representation different" but "is it different by more than two runs of the same recipe disagree", which in this paper is 190 within-condition pairs against 400 cross-condition ones. The second instrument is the fitted-basis **upper bound**: re-select the basis to maximize the very quantity you claim was suppressed, on held-out data, and check whether the bound moved. If it did not move, you were measuring a basis choice; if it did, the property really is gone. Both of these are directly implementable on probe read-outs: an unconstrained linear probe is exactly the "optimal basis" upper bound, so a claim that a feature disappeared should always report both the fixed-direction probe and the best-possible-direction probe, and the CCA distance between the two layers' activations against a seed-matched null.

The reusable falsification frame is worth stating on its own: **a claim that an internal property is functionally important has two predictions, and a real explanation must survive both — suppressing the property should hurt if it is necessary, and amplifying it should help if it is beneficial.** This paper found one of the two "shoulds" inverted (suppression helped) and the other catastrophically wrong (amplification collapsed accuracy by ~75 percent in one model), which is what "neither necessary nor sufficient" means operationally. It also supplies the layer-restriction ablation as a cheap localiser: applying the constraint to only the first or only the last few layers tells you *where* the property matters, and the finding here — early layers tolerate suppression better than late layers — is the opposite of the naive reading that abstract concept-like units near the output are the important ones. Coreference `arxiv-2211.04367` (the same dissociation from the ablation side: probe-identified units and ablation-identified units barely agree) and `arxiv-2007.04440` (selectivity's relationship to robustness, from the same group, treating the statistic as a predictor rather than a cause).
