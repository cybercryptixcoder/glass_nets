# ONAM: Fitting Orthogonal Neural Additive Models for Post-Hoc Interpretability of Black-Box Predictions

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.21550
- **Authors / organisation:** David Köhler, Matthias Schmid; Institute for Medical Biometry, Informatics and Epidemiology, University Hospital Bonn
- **Date:** 2026-11-16
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.21550
- **Kind:** paper (tool paper for an R package)
- **Read from:** full text (HTML, abstract, §§1–5, acknowledgements, references, appendix proof; no tables and no results grid, only the worked console output in §3.2 — see Limits)
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
The paper is about the oldest reliability problem in post-hoc explanation: an additive surrogate is only useful if *which* effect belongs to *which* feature is decided by something other than the fitter's choice. Functional decomposition replaces a black-box prediction function with a sum of subfunctions over feature subsets, but without constraints that decomposition is wildly non-unique — the authors give the degenerate example where one full-order interaction swallows the entire function and every lower-order effect is set to zero, satisfying the same sum while explaining nothing. Hooker's fix was hierarchical orthogonality: each subfunction must be orthogonal to every lower-order subfunction it contains, which pins the decomposition down and forces as much of the function as possible into the readable low-order terms. Their goal is a software package whose fitted decomposition *provably* satisfies that condition rather than approximately or by convention. The larger aim is medical and actuarial practice, where a model has to be defensible to a regulator.

## What they studied
The object is a fixed prediction function F over d features, treated as a black box of any class — the demonstrations use XGBoost on the Boston housing data (506 districts, 13 features) and on the Cleveland heart-disease data (binary outcome). The unit of analysis is the *effect*: a main effect per feature, a two-way interaction per feature pair, up to a d-way term. Each effect is represented by its own small feed-forward network whose last layer is linear, and the whole object is called an orthogonal neural additive model.

## How they did it
Three steps, of which the third is the contribution. First, generate an explanation sample: feature rows together with the black box's predicted values on them, either the training data, a subsample, or a grid over the data's support — this sample *is* the distribution the orthogonality is defined against. Second, fit a neural additive model to those predicted values, with one sub-network per effect in a user-declared set Θ, plus a catch-all full-order interaction term, all trained jointly by backpropagation. Third, run an orthogonalization pass that descends from the highest interaction order to the main effects, and at each level projects the higher-order network's penultimate-layer output onto the concatenated penultimate outputs of all lower-order networks, subtracts that projection from the higher-order term and adds it back into the lower-order terms as linear last-layer weight updates. Because the shift is a projection onto the space the lower-order terms already span, the fitted predictions are unchanged to numerical precision, so the surrogate stays a faithful copy of the black box while the information migrates downward into the readable effects. They prove (Theorem 1, with the projection identity in an appendix) that the resulting estimates are pairwise orthogonal at a strictly lower order on the empirical measure of the explanation sample, and mean-centered as a consequence.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **replacement model that is also a complete variance decomposition**: F is rewritten as an intercept plus main effects plus interactions, with nothing left over, and the split between them is unique rather than conventional. The form is a set of one- and two-dimensional functions that can be plotted (line plots and heatmaps) plus a scalar per interaction level — the fraction of the surrogate's variance carried by order-k terms — and their sum over interpretable orders is named the *degree of interpretability*. It is complete in the sense that the decomposition accounts for all of the function; it is explicitly partial in that only low orders are human-readable, and the higher orders are a residual by construction. It can predict exactly (it is fitted to predict), but the explanation is the split, not the fit.

## How they judged success
Two things only, and both are internal. Fidelity is a correlation between the surrogate's predictions and the black box's on the explanation sample. Interpretability is the variance share I_1 + I_2, reported by the package's `summary` method. There are no held-out black boxes, no competing explainers, no ground-truth function, and no simulation study. The one worked number is the Boston/XGBoost demonstration: correlation 0.9635 between surrogate and black box with a four-member ensemble, I_1 = 0.9038, I_2 = 0.0543, so a degree of interpretability of 0.9581. Their own prose then restates those as 91.08% and 5.05% and totals 96.14%, which does not agree with the values printed in the same block — a transcription slip in the text, recorded here rather than resolved. Numerical stability is addressed operationally: the least-squares projection is solved through a QR decomposition with column pivoting that drops numerically dependent columns, and the R-to-Python boundary is set to double precision to stop the orthogonalization from perturbing the output it is supposed to leave intact.

## What they found
The theorem is the finding: the algorithm's output satisfies hierarchical orthogonality (and, following the prior work it implements, stacked orthogonality too), which is what licenses calling any single effect *the* effect of that feature. The worked example reports that roughly nine tenths of the XGBoost housing model is carried by main effects and a further five percent by the four declared two-way interactions, i.e. the model is almost entirely summarizable by plots a practitioner can read. Two design claims are worth noting as results because they are backed by the pacing of the piece rather than by an experiment: the cost of the orthogonalization pass is of order n·|Θ|²·b̄² and is normally less than one percent of the fitting time (the full Boston fit with 508 explanation rows, 18 effects, 8 penultimate units and 500 epochs took about 350 seconds on a single machine), and the readouts for single features — as opposed to whole interaction levels — must be recovered by other means, such as generalized Sobol indices computed from the same decomposition.

## Limits and open problems they name
They state that the fit depends heavily on sample size, architecture, optimizer and hyperparameters, and recommend not one fit but an ensemble of multi-initialization runs, each orthogonalized, then averaged, then orthogonalized once more. They also concede that the number of subfunctions grows as the power set of the features, so a practitioner must restrict to a declared set Θ and let the full-order term absorb the rest — which reintroduces a choice the software does not test. What they do not name, and what matters most for our purposes, is bigger: every property they establish is a property of the fitted surrogate, so the whole object stands on the fidelity of that surrogate to the black box, and fidelity is reported as a single in-sample correlation with no held-out set, no error pattern, and no probe of where the surrogate and the black box disagree. The orthogonality guarantee is exact with respect to the explanation sample and silent about any other input distribution. There is also no comparison against additive baselines (a plain generalized additive model, an explainer built on tree structure, or the accumulating-local-effects style summaries the introduction cites), so "our decomposition is unique" is never shown to buy anything over "our decomposition is conventional".

## What we take from it
1. **An identifiability argument we can steal outright.** The degenerate-decomposition example — one full-order interaction absorbing everything — is the sharpest one-paragraph statement the folder has of why additive summaries of a black box are meaningless without a constraint. Our own decompositions (per-head attributions, per-feature probes, per-component contributions) have exactly this freedom unless we impose an analogous ranking, and the constraint to impose is the same one: lower-order terms get the mass, higher-order terms absorb the residual by construction. Adopting it makes our readouts comparable across models rather than across fitters.
2. **A transfer-with-no-output-change operation as the implementation of that constraint.** The third step is a projection and re-add at the last linear layer, so the surrogate's predictions are numerically invariant while the mass distribution among terms changes. That is the cleanest pattern in the folder for "change the explanation, hold the prediction fixed, and verify the holding" — the same inversion we want when attributing mass among components, and the invariant is checkable to floating-point tolerance rather than by correlation.
3. **A measure to report next to any additive summary: the interpretability budget.** The variance shares I_k sum to one and I_1 + I_2 is a single number for how much of the model is readable at all. For us the analogue is the share of a prediction's attribution mass that lands on main effects versus interactions *within the same feature group*, and it should be printed beside the explanation rather than inferred by the reader — it converts "here is an interaction" into "this model is X% additively readable", which is the quantity that decides whether a white-box summary can be trusted on this model at all.
4. **A warning about the one metric this tool reports.** Fidelity here is fit quality on the sample the surrogate was trained on, and that is a validation of arithmetic, not of mechanism — the folder has met the same failure three times (a distilled student whose internals need not match its teacher, a random dictionary that scores like a trained one, a selector scored by its own objective). Any surrogate we publish should be scored on a held-out sample *and* on the inputs where its errors are largest, and the orthogonality claim should be stated as relative to the sample distribution, because that is precisely how it is proved here.
5. **A cheap companion readout to demand alongside it.** Their own structure has the answer: single-feature importance is a *different* functional of the same decomposition (Sobol indices) than whole-level variance share, so a decomposition can be nearly all main effect and still have its mass concentrated in two features. For our per-component summaries the same split applies — level share versus feature share — and publishing only one of them is publishing the wrong one of the two.
6. **Coreference:** arxiv-2611.08767 (a white surrogate fitted to a teacher and then explained, with the paper's own caveat that matching outputs is not matching mechanism) and arxiv-2611.20371 (a probe that decodes is not a probe that intervenes).
