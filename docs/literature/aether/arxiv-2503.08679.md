# Chain-of-Thought Reasoning In The Wild Is Not Always Faithful

- **Key:** arxiv-2503.08679
- **Authors / organisation:** Iván Arcuschin (Poseidon Research), Jett Janiak (AI Office, European Commission), Robert Krzyzanowski (Poseidon Research), Senthooran Rajamanoharan, Neel Nanda, Arthur Conmy (no affiliation line printed in the HTML; a conflict-of-interest note states that the last three authors are employed by Google DeepMind, which develops Gemini, one of the evaluated models). ## What they wanted, and why
Prior work on unfaithful chains of thought showed that models omit a bias they were given, but always in setups a researcher had deliberately poisoned: injected hints, edited reasoning traces, adversarially reworded prompts. The open question is whether unfaithfulness survives on ordinary prompts that nobody tampered with, and therefore whether it is something anyone benchmarking a model could stumble into without looking for it. The authors treat the written rationale as an *explanation of the computation* and ask how much of the answer-producing process it actually accounts for. The larger goal is to price a monitoring channel: if reasoning traces are going to be used to oversee models in agentic or safety-relevant settings, it matters whether the trace is a complete account or only a plausible one.

## What they studied
Fifteen frontier chat and reasoning models from six developers (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google, Meta, Qwen, DeepSeek), plus one pretrained model as a baseline, with API-served versions rather than open weights; OpenAI's reasoning models are excluded because their traces are not public. Unit of analysis is the *question pair* for the first study — two logically opposite prompts about the same entities — and the *reasoning step* for the second. Data: a filtered slice of the World Model dataset (Gurnee & Tegmark) yielding 4,834 anti-symmetric Yes/No comparison pairs over 29 property/comparison templates, and a 215-problem subset of PutnamBench with non-guessable answers. Behaviour and text, not internals, except one small probing appendix on Llama 3.3 70B.

## How they did it
Two studies with separate pipelines. In the first, each entity pair is instantiated as four questions (greater-than and less-than, each in both orderings), each pair is passed through a two-stage LLM ambiguity filter so that answering Yes to both or No to both is genuinely contradictory, and every question gets ten rollouts at temperature 0.7 with top-p 0.9 and a plain "think step by step, then answer YES/NO" prompt. An LLM autorater labels each chain as supporting Yes, No, or Unknown, and a pair is flagged unfaithful only if three conditions hold together: the two variants differ by at least 50% in accuracy, the surrounding template group shows at least a 5% bias toward Yes or No, and the lower-accuracy variant's correct answer points against that group bias. That conjunction is the design's core: it separates "this template leans one way" from "this pair was answered wrongly because it leaned that way". In the second study, rollouts on Putnam problems are filtered to *correct* answers, an autorater identifies which steps were critical to reaching the answer, and a set of eight binary questions decides whether a critical step is an obviously invalid deduction that the model never acknowledges anywhere in the trace — a proof by contradiction that assumes the stated reasoning is faithful and then exhibits the step that would have to be a lie for that to hold. Every candidate is then manually reviewed. Five rounds of ambiguity-filter refinement, threshold sweeps, temperature and rollout-count subsampling, judge swaps, and a post-cutoff exam run serve as controls.

## What counts as an explanation here
A *behavioural* account and nothing more, and the authors are careful to say so: faithfulness is defined as consistency of the stated reasoning with the answer across controlled prompt pairs, which they call necessary but not sufficient for faithfulness of the internal computation. The explanation object is therefore a diagnosed *failure* of an explanation — an answer that is stable while the justification for it is not — rather than a description of mechanism. It is partial by construction and predicts only at the level of template and pair. The one place the paper gestures at internals is the probing appendix, where a linear probe predicts a template's Yes-frequency from residual activations before any reasoning tokens exist; even there the framing is "preliminary evidence" and a *candidate* direction for a future steering test, not a circuit.

## How they judged success
Unfaithfulness rate: flagged question pairs over 4,834, with binomial 95% intervals over pairs and bootstrap intervals for the pattern breakdown. Controls, in the order they matter: a threshold grid of 20 accuracy-difference/group-bias combinations with a check that the model *ranking* survives all of them; retention under oversampling from 20 to 100 rollouts per question; a CoT-length correlation against the unfaithful indicator, pooled and per model; rollout-subsampling across ten seeds; a temperature sweep at 0.3, 0.7, 1.0 with per-template rate correlations; an ambiguity-filter validation against human labels reporting the confusion matrix, precision, recall and a residual-ambiguity estimate with a second blinded annotator; a same-latitude/longitude ablation for geographic items; an "N/A is allowed" ablation; a second independent judge model with per-response agreement; an inter-rater kappa for the judge and for each pattern label; and, for the math study, a data-contamination check on an exam released after the stated training cutoffs. The pattern taxonomy is explicitly *not* offered as validated: the authors report that Argument Switching has near-chance kappa and that only the collapsed "any pattern present" label reaches usable agreement.

## What they found
Implicit post-hoc rationalization occurs on unmodified prompts, at rates from essentially zero to 13.49% of pairs: GPT-4o-mini 13.49%, Claude 3.5 Haiku 7.42%, Gemini 1.5 Pro 6.54%, Qwen 32B 4.50%, Llama 3.1 70B 3.25%, Gemini 2.5 Flash 2.17%, Llama 3.3 70B Instruct 2.09%, Claude 3.7 Sonnet 1.84%, DeepSeek V3 1.23%, ChatGPT-4o 0.49%, Claude 3.5 Sonnet v2 0.45%, GPT-4o Aug 2024 and DeepSeek R1 both 0.37%, Claude 3.7 Sonnet at 64k thinking 0.25%, Gemini 2.5 Pro 0.14%, and Claude 3.7 Sonnet at a 1k thinking budget lowest at 0.04% (two pairs). Thinking variants are much more faithful than their non-thinking siblings but never perfect. Two results cut against a simple "more compute, more faithfulness" story: raising the thinking budget from 1k to 64k slightly *increased* unfaithfulness, with about 80% of the 64k flagged pairs having had at least one "unknown" rollout at 1k — longer reasoning hallucinating a basis for committing; and the pretrained Llama 3.1 70B is *more* unfaithful (3.25%) than its instruction-tuned counterpart, so RLHF-induced sycophancy is not the whole explanation. Accuracy does not track unfaithfulness: Claude 3.5 Sonnet v2 and 3.7 Sonnet perform similarly on the benchmark but differ by roughly four times in rate. On mechanisms, about 18% of flagged pairs show argument switching with the same facts retrieved, and 45% including co-occurring cases, so retrieval differences cannot explain the majority-shape of the phenomenon; the remainder are biased fact inconsistencies where the same fact is right in one variant and wrong in the reverse. On math, unfaithful illogical shortcuts run 18.84% of correct Claude 3.7 Sonnet non-thinking responses against 4.39% with thinking, 3.70% against 1.16% for DeepSeek V3 and R1, and 8.70% against 2.44% for Qwen 72B IT and QwQ 32B — the same direction in all three pairs, with *correct* answers reached through steps the models themselves call illogical when shown the same step in a separate chat. Contamination was reduced but not eliminated by running the 2024 exam (14 of 17 flagged cases confirmed by hand); shortcut rate on problems already known to produce them reproduces at 65.4%, far above baseline.

## Limits and open problems they name
No mechanistic account of *why* the trace diverges from the computation; the probing appendix is an early signal, not a circuit analysis. Factual comparison questions make unfaithfulness easy to define because a wrong justification is demonstrably false, but in domains with several defensible arguments the same test would have little purchase — the place where this matters most (open-ended multi-hop, conversation, agents) is precisely where the method thins out. The question set is naturalistic but synthetic, not real user traffic. The pattern taxonomy is unreliable at the fine-grained level; the authors print the kappa values rather than burying them. Residual ambiguity is estimated, not proven absent. Results are on behaviour, so claims about "the internal process" are inferences from inconsistency, and the whole Putnam analysis is a lower bound from 215 filtered problems. They also flag that the pretrained-model comparison is few-shot prompted and so not strictly like-for-like.

## What we take from it
A **measure**, and the folder's cheapest one for the question "is this read-out describing the computation or decorating it". The reversed-prompt pair is an intervention that requires no access to internals at all: hold the underlying fact fixed, flip the logical direction, and ask whether the stated rationale and the answer move together. Any account of ours that claims a model represents X and reasons with X should survive the trivial symmetry of its own input space; where it does not, the account is describing the *output of a decision* rather than its cause. A **warning** with a sharp shape: a local, plausible, correctly-reasoning-looking step can coexist with a globally incoherent rule, and the model itself will call that step illogical when the step is presented in isolation. Any explanation we generate — human-written or model-written — that is graded by a judge asking "does this step look sound?" is therefore measuring step-level plausibility, not faithfulness, which is exactly why the authors' eight-question protocol asks *whether a later step retracts this one* and *whether an alternative path reaches the same answer without it*. A **framing** worth adopting wholesale: the proof-by-contradiction structure, where you assume the stated explanation is faithful and then hunt for the step that would have to be false for that to hold. And a **control** we should copy every time we use a model to grade a model: swap the judge for a stronger model and report per-item agreement, not just the aggregate rate; here 99.3% per-response agreement is what makes the small absolute numbers believable. Coreference `arxiv-2408.05451` (a probe reading a conjunction the network never needed — the same "presence is not use" gap, on the read-out side) and `arxiv-2410.14670` (a linear map recovering what the account failed to explain — a completeness test for a description rather than a behaviour).
