## What they wanted, and why
Constraint-based discovery reduces structure learning to a long sequence of conditional-independence questions, and every one of those questions needs a conditioning set that is usually partly missing in real data. The two standard fixes each break somewhere: deleting incomplete rows distorts the sample whenever the data are not missing completely at random, and completing the data once and then testing treats the completed values as if they were observed. The authors' complaint is stronger than "imputation adds noise" — they prove that if the imputation model is asymptotically wrong *and* the imputation error manufactures dependence between the two test variables, then any test that is consistent on the completed data will reject the truth with probability going to one, at any fixed level. That makes the standard pipeline structurally miscalibrated rather than merely underpowered, and it matters for us because a probe ledger built on such a test acquires edges that no amount of extra probes will remove.

## What they studied
No neural networks: the object is the conditional-independence test itself, understood as the oracle inside PC. Data are tabular with a designated response, a candidate variable and a conditioning set, of which only the conditioning set is assumed incomplete; missingness is generated with a mechanism whose marginal rate is calibrated to a target and whose mass concentrates in the upper tail of a driver (a fully observed covariate under MAR, the variable itself under MNAR). Benchmarks are three synthetic data-generating processes (linear Gaussian, post-nonlinear, latent confounder), Erdős–Rényi DAGs at ten, twenty and thirty variables, and the ALARM (37 variables, 46 edges) and HAILFINDER (56 variables, 66 edges) networks under linear and nonlinear structural equations. The unit of analysis is a single test's decision and then, one level up, the recovered graph.

## How they did it
The construction is a cancellation argument turned into a test. Step one, complete the conditioning set many times over with a proper multiple-imputation procedure, giving multiple completed datasets. Step two, inside each completed dataset, split into folds and train two predictive models of the response on the training folds: a full model that sees the candidate variable plus the completed conditioning set, and a partial model that sees the same completed conditioning set plus a *placebo* column built by conditionally resampling the candidate from its nearest neighbours in conditioning-set space, which keeps its dependence on the conditioning set while severing the link to the response. Because both models receive the identical imputed conditioning set, any distortion the imputation introduced enters both and cancels in their loss difference. Step three, score both on the held-out fold and take the difference of out-of-sample losses, averaged over folds and imputations. Step four, and this is the part they regard as the novel composition: the combined variance must absorb *two* separate sources — overlap between cross-validation training sets and variation across imputations — so they nest a consistent cross-validation variance estimator inside Rubin's pooling rule to get the total variance, and compare the normalised loss difference against a t reference with a small-sample degrees-of-freedom adjustment. For graph recovery they cache a fixed set of completed datasets and reuse them across all tests rather than re-imputing per query, masking the test variables out of each query; they report that the cached and per-query strategies agree on the overwhelming majority of decisions and argue the caching residual is measured rather than assumed. Baselines are Fisher's Z with single imputation and with pooled multiple imputation, the generalized covariance measure with random forests, and kernel conditional-independence tests, each on complete cases and on imputed data, plus missing-value PC, test-wise deletion, and a score-based competitor. Power is mapped separately as a function of sample size, signal strength and conditioning-set size.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not a description of a model but a *statistical guarantee about an instrument*: the "explanation" is that a calibrated decision rule exists for a question whose data are corrupted, plus a decomposition of what the guarantee covers. The claim is explicitly two-tiered — an "internal" null about the completed data for which asymptotic size is provably at most the nominal level in the many-imputation limit, and a "scientific" null about the truth, which inherits that bound only under a consistent imputer and only up to a caching residual they measure rather than bound, and which under the hard missingness regime rests on an empirical diagnostic instead of a theorem. It is partial by construction: calibration is claimed, power is conceded to be lower than a well-specified parametric test, and the graph-level guarantee is inherited from an external consistency result rather than proved end-to-end.

## How they judged success
Primary measure is the empirical rejection rate under the null against the nominal level, pooled and then broken down by sample size and conditioning-set size, with the caveat printed that comparisons of power against miscalibrated baselines are uninformative. Secondary: power as a function of signal strength and sample size, leading to an explicit sample-size planning rule (80% power at a moderate signal with a five-variable conditioning set needs roughly five hundred observations for the linear Gaussian process, a thousand for the latent-confounder process and two thousand for the post-nonlinear one; a weak signal needs more than two thousand everywhere). Graph recovery is scored by structural Hamming distance against the true graph, skeleton Hamming distance, skeleton F1, precision, recall and median edge count, with paired graph-clustered bootstrap intervals for the one claim they flag as small. Notably they *tune* the tunable baselines to their own edge count before comparing, precisely to test whether their gains are an artefact of a sparser output.

## What they found
Under the null, their test's pooled false-positive rate stays at or below about four percent across mechanisms while the imputation-based Fisher pipelines run at twenty-eight to forty-five percent under the adversarial mechanism and flexible kernel or covariance tests are miscalibrated even on complete data (roughly fifteen to twenty-one percent), which the authors attribute to those tests' regularity conditions failing for forest-based regressions. Complete-case Fisher's Z is calibrated at every mechanism — because selecting rows on the conditioning variables leaves the conditional law of the test variables intact — at the cost of about half the rows and, inside PC, of selection effects. Their rate drifts upward with sample size toward nominal from below, which they present as consistent with their derived conservatism, and is mildly above nominal only in cells with five or more conditioning variables. Graph recovery splits by functional form: with linear edges the parametric missing-value-PC pipeline is best overall at the larger scales, and with nonlinear edges their test has the lowest distance at every scale, with the gap over the Fisher pipelines widening from one unit at ten variables to fifteen at thirty. On the real network topologies under the hard mechanism at twenty percent per-column missingness, their distances are about sixty-four against seventy-two for missing-value PC and one hundred three and above for test-wise deletion and the Fisher pipelines on the weather network; complete-case analysis reaches a comparable distance only by returning an empty skeleton.

## Limits and open problems they name
The conservatism is derived only for the limit of infinitely many imputations; at a fixed number the size bound rests on a reference distribution they explicitly do not prove and only examine empirically. The extension from the completed-data null to the truthful null under the easy missingness regime needs a consistent imputer, and the cached design satisfies their key lemma only up to a residual they measure. Under the hard missingness regime that consistency is false by construction, so both size and power there sit on a measured diagnostic — a product of residual-correlation terms that predicts the inflation (rank correlation with sensitivity around nine tenths) — rather than a theorem. Degree-of-freedom theory does not cover the case with test variables themselves incomplete. Only PC under causal sufficiency is covered by the recovery corollary, and the simplified search used in the experiments is not covered by the corollaries at all; the algorithm is agnostic to the discovery procedure but the confounding-allowing version is left as future work. The variance result is general over cross-validated statistics on multiply imputed data but only illustrated for this test. And where linear parametric assumptions hold, they recommend the parametric competitor outright.

## What we take from it
A *method*: the paired-placebo design — feed an intervention's side-effect into both arms of a comparison so it cancels in the difference — is directly portable to probed comparisons where the "distortion" is a nuisance we cannot avoid (a shared adaptor, a shared tokenizer, a shared batch composition, a shared imputation of missing activations). A *measure*: the sample-size planning rule, which is the first thing in this line of the folder that names a number of draws for a stated power against a stated effect size. A *framing*: the two-tier guarantee (proved for the proxy question, measured for the real question) stated as such, with a diagnostic that predicts the gap. A *warning*: complete-case analysis is calibrated but selects, and a calibrated test inside a pruning search still produces a biased skeleton. And a *template*: mapping every assumption to an implementation counterpart, a diagnostic and a failure behaviour in one table.
