# Quantifying LLM Attention-Head Stability: Implications for Circuit Universality

- **Key:** arxiv-2602.16740
- **Authors / organisation:** Karan Bali, Jack Stanley, Praneet Suresh, Danilo Bzdok; Mila - Quebec Artificial Intelligence Institute and McGill University, Montreal
- **Date:** 2026-02-17
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.16740
- **Kind:** paper

- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Interpretability work routinely asserts that a transformer implements some function with a named set of attention heads, and treats that set as a fact about the architecture. The authors point out that a reported circuit is only ever observed on one trained instance, and that two teams training the same architecture on the same data with different seeds may end up with entirely different internal mechanisms that happen to predict equally well. They frame the question as the missing empirical half of the universality hypothesis: if universality is claimed, its strength should be measurable, and the natural measurement is agreement between independently initialised refits of one architecture. The practical stake they name is safety monitoring — a white-box monitor built on features or heads that do not survive a reseed is monitoring a coincidence, and the paper's motivating example is a disease model whose "explainable" feature may reflect nothing about the disease. So the goal is to turn universality from a qualitative assumption into a layer-by-layer score, and to find out which training choices move that score.

## What they studied
Decoder-only GPT-style language models built with TransformerLens, trained from scratch under a small grid rather than at frontier scale: depth 2, 4, 8 and 12 layers, 8 or 16 heads per layer, each in a with-MLP and an attention-only variant, each in an Adam and an AdamW mirror — 26 architectures in total (Table 1). The 2-, 4- and 8-layer models train on a 2-billion-token subset of C4; the 12-layer GPT-2-small-shaped models (12 layers, 12 heads) train on 9 billion tokens of OpenWebText; the largest model is 124M parameters and the smallest 7M. Number of refits is 50 per architecture except the two 12-layer architectures, which get 5 each. The unit of analysis is the attention head, indexed by layer, and the comparison object is the post-softmax attention score matrix of a head over a fixed prompt set. A secondary unit is the residual stream at each layer, compared with CKA.

## How they did it
For a pair of refits of the same architecture — an anchor m and a pair n — every head h_i of the anchor's layer l is scored against every head h_j of the same layer in the pair. The score for one prompt is the cosine similarity of the two heads' flattened attention score matrices; the score for the pair is that similarity averaged over a 100-prompt set; the stability of h_i against one refit is the maximum over all candidate heads in that layer; and the head's reported stability S is the mean of those per-refit maxima over all the other N-1 refits. Averaging over heads in a layer gives a layer-wise curve. Because the maximum over candidates replaces any assignment procedure, matching is permutation-invariant and needs no Hungarian algorithm. A relaxed variant drops the layer constraint so an anchor head may match any head in the other refit, and the resulting best-match layer indices are tabulated into a row-normalised alignment matrix. Within-layer uniqueness is computed the same way but against peer heads inside the anchor itself, so a high mean peer similarity means a redundant head and a low one a distinctive head. Two exploratory correlates are then correlated against layer stability: the mean L2 Frobenius norm of the layer's query-weight matrices, and prompt length (six truncated subsets of twenty base prompts at 5, 10, 20, 30, 40 and 50 tokens). Functional importance is measured by a head-ablation study on the anchor: zero one head's output in the forward pass, record the perplexity change against the unablated baseline over the same prompts, and correlate that change against the head's stability, layer by layer, with Pearson correlation. The residual stream is compared across refits by CKA on post-attention residual activations with an RBF-kernel Gram matrix. Finally all heads from all refits and architectures are embedded into one space by meta-SNE and coloured by relative depth l/L.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not a circuit and not a description of what any head computes. The paper's product is a **stability score attached to a location** — a number per head, per layer, per architecture, saying how reliably that component can be found again in another training run of the same recipe. It is deliberately partial and task-agnostic: it says nothing about function, only about reproducibility of the object that a functional claim would be made about. The authors cast this as a prerequisite rather than an explanation: an interpretability claim is licensed only to the extent the unit it names is stable, and their conclusion is that universality is a matter of degree that varies with depth, width, optimizer and the query distribution. The scores themselves do predict something — the layer-level shape of stability, and its relation to post-ablation importance — but nothing that would predict what a head does or what the model outputs under an intervention.

## How they judged success
There is no held-out benchmark and no accuracy metric; the judged quantities are the stability score itself and comparisons of it across conditions. Success takes the form: (i) a layer-wise shape that repeats across architectures (early and late layers high, middle layers low); (ii) an interior minimum whose position, expressed as relative depth, clusters for the deeper architectures; (iii) an alignment matrix that is strongly diagonal-dominant, showing that same-layer correspondence survives reseeding at all; (iv) an ablation correlation showing whether the least stable heads matter functionally; (v) the Adam-versus-AdamW contrast on otherwise identical refits, with validation perplexity on wikitext-2-raw checked for performance parity so that a stability gain is not paid for by a worse model; (vi) the residual-stream comparison as a control on whether anything internal is stable at all. The statistical support is largely visual and verbal — per-layer correlations are explicitly described as small and noisy, the significance statements are made in prose without printed test statistics or p-values, and no null distribution for the cosine score is constructed, so the absolute value of a stability number has no calibrated chance level in the paper.

## What they found
The central pattern is a dip: in the 8-layer 8-head with-MLP model the layer-wise stability sits near 1.0 at the first layer and falls to roughly 0.70 at layer 5 (relative depth 0.625), with early and late layers consistently more stable than middle ones across the architectures shown. The least stable layer tends to come after the most stable layer, and for the 8- and 12-layer models the minimum falls in the relative-depth band from 0.4 to 0.8; the anchor example for the 8-layer model has its most stable layer at index 1 (relative depth 0.125) and its least stable at index 5. The stability gap between a model's most and least stable layer widens with depth and is modestly larger for 8-head than for 16-head configurations. Relaxing the layer constraint raises scores slightly, as a larger candidate pool must, without changing the shape, and the alignment matrix remains strongly diagonal-dominant — best matches mostly come from the same layer — but with visibly flatter, lower-kurtosis rows in the middle layers, i.e. off-diagonal spill into neighbouring layers precisely where in-layer stability is weakest. The same middle-layer heads are also the most distinctive: mean similarity to their own layer's peers is lowest at mid-depth and highest at the ends. Two correlates line up: stability falls as the layer's mean query-weight norm rises, and it falls sharply as prompt length grows from 5 to 50 tokens. Against this, AdamW raises head stability clearly in the deeper with-MLP models and in all attention-only architectures, with little change in the shallow with-MLP models, and validation perplexity is described as nearly unchanged; a mechanistic check shows AdamW suppresses the mean L2 norm of head output activations. The residual stream is consistently the more stable object at every depth, with no large optimizer effect. Ablation correlations are small and noisy per layer but trend negative overall, which the authors read as unstable heads mattering more for performance the deeper their layer. The meta-SNE embedding separates heads by relative depth rather than by architecture or absolute size.

## Limits and open problems they name
No exhaustive hyperparameter sweep beyond the optimizer mirror, and model sizes are capped at 124M parameters for budget reasons, so nothing here measures a frontier model. They leave the relation between stability and the wider hyperparameter space to future work. The paper's own framing limits it further in ways worth naming: stability is matched by best cosine over a single layer's candidates, so a head that has genuinely moved to a different layer registers as unstable rather than as relocated, and a head that merged with a neighbour registers as unmatched rather than as changed; the prompt set is 100 model-generated prompts, and since stability falls with prompt length the headline numbers are conditional on a prompt-length choice the paper varies only in the sweep; the importance measure is a single-head zero ablation with a perplexity delta, which cannot see redundancy between heads; and no downstream capability or intervention experiment is run, so the link from an unstable head to a wrong prediction is argued from the ablation correlation rather than demonstrated.

## What we take from it
The **measure** is the reusable object, and it is cheap: for any two structurally identical models — two refits, or two checkpoints, or an edited model and its original — score each component by the best cosine match in the other model and average the max over many inputs. Because the max over candidates needs no assignment, it is robust to permutation symmetry, which is exactly the confound that kills naive weight or activation comparison across instances. The **warning** is sharper than any method here: a component's stability is not uniform across depth and is lowest in the middle, so a white-box model that reads a mid-depth feature and reports it as a property of the architecture is reporting a property of one training run. The **control** we should copy outright is the residual stream — if the components we name are less stable than the pathway they write into, the thing that carries the behaviour across refits is the shared stream-plus-objective, not our named units, and that comparison is a five-line sanity check before any claim about a feature. The **shortcut with a caveat**: AdamW improves stability at no apparent perplexity cost in this setup, which means seed instability is partly a training-regularisation artefact rather than an intrinsic property of the architecture — worth knowing before attributing irreproducibility to the model. The **framing** to import into our own write-ups: report the number of independent fits our conclusions rest on, and where the answer is one, state that the result is a property of that fit and not of the architecture.
