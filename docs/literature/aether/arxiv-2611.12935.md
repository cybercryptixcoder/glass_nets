# Train on prefill, monitor at decode: Using sparse autoencoders for topic monitoring

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.12935
- **Authors / organisation:** Rishikesh Mallagundla, Pranav Patel (Mercity — the corporate "Simula" data library cited in the paper is theirs)
- **Date:** 2026-11-06
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.12935
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
A deployed assistant is usually scoped to one job, and it drifts off that scope mid-conversation even after a system prompt or fine-tune. Guardrails in the safety line already label a response as it streams, but they read the generated text with a separate model and answer a two- or three-way harm question; topical guardrails decide once per prompt or per finished response, so a mid-response change of subject is caught late and the single label does not say where the change happened. The authors want a monitor that assigns a topic to *every* token of the response while it is being written, using the generating model's own internal state rather than its text. The larger goal is a cheap, inspectable runtime read-out that turns activation-level monitoring from a binary safety check into a many-class, position-resolved one.

## What they studied
Gemma 3 1B and Gemma 3 4B, with the Gemma Scope 2 JumpReLU sparse autoencoders (16,384 features per layer) on the residual stream at ten consecutive late layers (16–25 for the 1B, 24–33 for the 4B). Unit of analysis is the per-token hidden state and its SAE feature code; the object being learned is a small sequence model over those features that emits a topic per token. Topics are seven business categories (enterprise documents, general news and content, customer service, legal, financial, HR and people operations, healthcare).

## How they did it
A teacher model (GPT-5.6 Luna, used as both generator and critic) writes 10,000 synthetic prompt–response pairs whose spans are labelled by topic before the text is written; a share of the multi-topic records uses explicit connectives, a share a light connective, and a quarter no surface marker at all, so a boundary cannot be found from wording alone. Every conversation is passed through the frozen backbone in one prefill forward pass, the SAE code at each of the ten layers is reduced to a fixed-width vector, and only a small causal detector is trained — a three-layer GRU, a six-block ConvNeXt-style dilated convolutional net (379-token receptive field) or a two-layer causal Transformer encoder — on token-level cross-entropy for 15 epochs. Feature reduction is the variable under test: three static rules fitted on the training split (largest summed activation; an F-statistic on between- versus within-topic activation; a group lasso linear probe over the seven topics) each keep 150 features per layer, a dynamic rule keeps each token's own top-k and writes them into feature-named columns, and a NoSAE control skips the SAE entirely and feeds the raw hidden states. The trained detectors then label, with no retraining, 1,000 responses the backbone writes itself with topic tags removed; those responses have no gold labels, so they are scored for the share of tokens carrying a requested topic and for the share of requested topics that each earn at least a tenth of the tokens, with the teacher's responses to the same prompts as the reference. Robustness is a four-condition perturbation battery (inserted off-topic asides, one topic removed from the response, sections reordered, a same-topic passage substitution as control); dependence on individual layers and features is measured by zeroing one layer's input block at a time and by gradient-times-input attribution inside the most important layer.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not a description of a concept and not a dictionary entry: the explanation is a **causal sequence read-out** — a token-level classifier over a named subset of a frozen dictionary's features, meant to be partial (topics only, seven of them) and predictive (it forecasts the label of the next token from the tokens before it, during generation). Its interpretability claim is structural rather than semantic: every input column corresponds to an SAE feature that can be looked up and inspected, and the layer-knockout and attribution numbers say which layers and which features the prediction rests on.

## How they judged success
Macro-F1 averaged equally over the seven topics and an overlap score (intersection-over-union of predicted and true tokens per topic, averaged over topics, stricter than F1 because correct tokens are not double-counted), both on 1,500 held-out records in prefill. In decode, purity (share of tokens labelled with a requested topic), coverage (share of requested topics above a 10% token share) and full coverage (responses where every requested topic clears that bar), with teacher-text scores as the reference line that separates monitor error from response drift. Robustness is the change in macro-F1 under each perturbation against the unedited original, plus an AUROC for whether detector confidence separates real from inserted tokens. Layer dependence is the overlap lost when one layer is knocked out and the sum over all ten; feature dependence is the cost of zeroing the ten top- versus ten bottom-attributed features. Baselines are the three selection rules against each other and the NoSAE control, with detector size and inference cost printed per input.

## What they found
Prefill is nearly saturated and the reduction rule barely matters on the small model: from 1,500 of 163,840 features, summation reaches 94.3–96.2 macro-F1 on Gemma 3 1B and label-based selection adds at most about a point. On Gemma 3 4B the gap opens — every summation detector scores below its 1B counterpart and the GRU falls to 87.4 — and switching only the selection rule to the F-statistic or group lasso brings all detectors within a point of their 1B scores and lifts that GRU to 96.4. The two supervised rules sit within 0.4 points of each other everywhere, so the author's recommendation is the F-statistic, a single deterministic pass with no hyperparameters. ConvNeXt is best in six of ten rows. Decode transfer holds: with dynamic or label-selected features, 90–98% of the backbone's own tokens (responses roughly 1,240 tokens against the teacher's 820) get a requested topic, and summation on the 4B is the exception that widens. Full coverage is the weak score — 95–99 on teacher text, falling by about 30 points on the 1B's own text and further on the 4B's, and dropping from near 100 on single-topic prompts to 34–62 on four-topic prompts, which the authors attribute mostly to the backbone answering fewer topics in a crowded prompt (a raised generation cap changed nothing because responses end well before it). Perturbations cost at most 5 macro-F1 points, ConvNeXt loses at most 1.1 under all four, reordering hurts the Transformer most, and the same-topic substitution control moves scores negligibly, which is the evidence that passages are labelled by their own content. Removal leaves 1–4% of tokens on the removed topic, worst for enterprise documents at 7–8%. On the question the practitioners asked: the SAE is **not needed for accuracy** — raw hidden states are outright best in two of six cases and within 0.3 points in three of the rest, and the sole losing cell is dynamic ConvNeXt on the 1B by 0.6 — but it sets the size: a static SAE detector stays at 1.4M–3.6M parameters at either backbone width, while a raw-state detector grows with the backbone to 7.6M–20.7M on the 4B, and at inference the static stage needs only the selected encoder rows (1.73M / 3.84M) instead of the ten whole SAEs (378M / 839M). Selection also changes fragility: zeroing one layer costs the 4B summation GRU 32.5 overlap points and 124.9 summed over all ten, against 1.2 and 4.2 under the F-statistic, and the ten highest-attributed features in the key layer cost 0.1–10.3 points against at most 0.16 for the ten lowest in all eighteen detector-and-rule combinations, with about half of each layer's feature budget contributing little under group lasso. Attribution is the less trustworthy instrument across layers — it sums absolute contributions, so a high-activation layer looks important regardless of use, which is exactly what the summation rule inflates — and the authors therefore rank layers by knockout rather than by attribution share.

## Limits and open problems they name
Decode scoring can only test *which* topics appear, not *where* a switch is placed, because generated text has no gold token labels; that is left to future work, and the perturbation analysis is not repeated for the raw-state detectors. There is no "none" class, so off-topic asides still receive a topic and can be flagged only through a drop in confidence — which fails for dynamic ConvNeXt (AUROC 54 and 51). Everything is synthetic teacher text over one fixed seven-topic taxonomy and two backbones from one family, with the same SAE suite; multi-turn production conversations, gradual drift, other model families and other dictionary suites are untested. Not named by the authors but visible in the tables: no seed variance or significance testing appears anywhere, one seed (42) per configuration, and the "SAE not necessary" conclusion sits inside a narrower band of differences than the 4B GRU's 5–25-point swings that the selection rule alone produced.

## What we take from it
- **Method (measure):** the prefill–decode identity as a Q&A pair. A monitor trained where labels exist and deployed where they do not is tested by the gap between the two regimes, and the paper's reference line is the move worth copying — score the detector on teacher-written text and on the model's own text with the same metric, so a drop can be attributed to the monitor or to the response, not both at once.
- **Method (instrument):** layer knockout beside gradient-times-input attribution, ranked by knockout. The paper shows why: attribution sums absolute contributions and so rewards whichever layer has the largest activations, which is precisely the selection rule (summation) that then looked most fragile.
- **Framing:** "is the decomposition necessary?" answered with three currencies — accuracy, size, and inspectability — instead of one. The SAE bought no accuracy and a much smaller, named-column monitor; that is a narrower and more checkable claim than "SAE features are interpretable", and it is the shape our own dictionary-versus-raw-activation comparisons should take.
- **Warning:** a monitor's robustness to perturbation is not its correctness; the same-topic substitution control passing while removal still leaks 1–4% of tokens (7–8% on the hardest topic) is the pattern that a control can be satisfied by the weakest failure while the specific one survives.
- **Shortcut:** full coverage collapsing with the number of requested topics (near 100 on one, 34–62 on four) is a denominator effect worth stating whenever a monitor score is reported per prompt: the metric is a function of how much was asked.
