# When is Your LLM Steerable?

- **Key:** arxiv-2606.11599
- **Authors / organisation:** Chenrui Fan, Yize Cheng, Ming Li, Soheil Feizi & Tianyi Zhou; University of Maryland, College Park / MBZUAI
- **Date:** 2026-06-10
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.11599
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

Activation steering is cheap to apply and unreliable in practice: the same vector and strength can work on one prompt and fail on the next, and the working strength changes across concepts and models. The field's answer so far has been to pay for it — sweep strengths, roll out the full response, and have a judge score each attempt. The authors ask a sharper question: is the success or failure of an intervention already visible in the hidden states a few tokens into the steered generation? If it is, then steerability is not noise to be searched around but a structured property of the state the intervention lands in, and a predictor of that property can be used both as a diagnostic and as a search accelerator. This is the same bet our white-box project makes generalised from "what will the model output" to "will my edit to the model work at all".

## What they studied

Three instruction-tuned models of 1.7B-3B parameters — Qwen3-1.7B, Gemma-2-2B-it and LLaMA-3.2-3B-Instruct — with two vector-construction methods (a difference-of-means vector, and a linear-probe weight vector trained with binary cross-entropy) applied at one chosen layer. The unit of analysis is a *steering attempt*: a (prompt, concept, strength, method) tuple. There are 150 concepts stratified into low, mid and high abstraction (50 each: surface formatting such as emoji use; discourse moves such as asking a clarifying question; persona and framing), 50 fixed Alpaca prompts shared across all concepts, 45 strengths for the mean-difference method and 18 for the probe method, giving 1.42M labelled steered generations in a testbed they call ASTEER. Prediction features are computed on a sampled grid of token positions and layer offsets relative to the intervention layer, not on one position.

## How they did it

Each rollout is scored by a judge model into three classes: under-steer (concept absent but the answer coherent), success (concept present and the answer coherent), and over-steer (the answer has broken down). Empty or gibberish responses count as over-steer. Prompt–concept pairs whose *unsteered* output is already judged a success are removed, so that any success at non-zero strength is attributable to the intervention. Three humans labelled 600 sampled generations; agreement was 0.83 against the humans' own consensus and 0.74 against a stronger judge model, which the authors read as validation of the automatic labels. For prediction they decode only the first few tokens with the vector applied, then run a single *unsteered* forward pass over that same token sequence; every difference between the two passes is then attributable to the intervention alone. From that paired pass they build three feature families at each sampled (token, layer) cell: geometry (cosine between the steered state and the vector, norm of the deviation from the unsteered state, cosine between steered and unsteered states, and cosine between the *deviation* and the vector), decoding dynamics (ratios and shifts of those quantities relative to the first token, so the features describe how the effect evolves), and steering-condition scalars (vector norm, applied strength). Ratios and summary statistics (mean, standard deviation, max, min) are added across the grid, and an XGBoost classifier with inverse-frequency class weights maps the feature vector to the three-way label. Generalisation is tested on held-out prompts for 120 in-distribution concepts and on 30 fully held-out concepts at every abstraction level. Finally the predicted success probability is used as a ranking function over candidate strengths: generate short traces for every candidate, rank, roll out the top K, stop at the first judged success.

## What counts as an explanation here

Not a mechanistic account of why a direction works, and the authors do not claim one. The explanation is a *predictive classifier over named geometric quantities of the intervention's own footprint*: the paper's claim to understanding is that the small set of quantities it reads off (alignment of the induced change with the vector, and how far the representation has travelled) carries the predictive mass, so the quantity that decides success is the *direction* of the induced shift rather than its size or the state's fidelity to its unsteered self. It is explicitly partial — a three-way label re-derived in advance — and it predicts, but only the label, and only for the two vector-construction methods and one intervention layer it was trained on.

## How they judged success

Macro-F1 on the three-way outcome, over five random seeds, reported separately for in-distribution prompts and for the 30 out-of-distribution concepts; row-normalised confusion matrices to expose which class is being absorbed into which. Feature-group ablations retrain the classifier on each family alone. A linear probe trained directly on a single steered hidden state at each of the 28 grid cells is run as an honest alternative, including an oracle-choice-of-cell variant. The application section is scored as an average successful-search rate — whether the returned candidate set contains at least one judged success — plotted against decoded tokens per prompt, against a per-concept oracle grid search, a per-item exhaustive grid search, ascending and descending orderings of that search with early stopping, and a training-set per-concept transfer baseline that has the same access to labelled data as the predictor.

## What they found

Outcome as a function of strength follows a consistent shape on all six model–method pairs: under-steer falls monotonically, over-steer rises monotonically, and success forms an inverted-U whose window location and width differ sharply from concept to concept, less so from prompt to prompt. Aggregate success rates are low — between 10.69 and 23.18 percent across the six pairs — and low-abstraction concepts are consistently the hardest (for example 6.88-14.72 percent against 13.15-29.83 for mid-level on the same model–method). The strength required by the probe method is roughly twenty times the mean-difference method's on two of the three models, and the probe method's range on LLaMA is over twenty times smaller than on the other two, i.e. the conversion from a vector to behaviour is model- and method-specific by more than an order of magnitude. The predictor reaches about 0.80 macro-F1 in-distribution and about 0.72 on unseen concepts for mean-difference vectors, and 0.68-0.74 in-distribution / 0.65-0.69 out-of-distribution for probe vectors. Errors are structured: over-steer is recalled at 87-93 percent, under-steer at 68-77 percent, and success is the class most often lost, misread as under-steer 20-37 percent of the time. The first two decoded tokens carry over 75 percent of the gain-based importance, while importance along the layer axis is spread evenly. Geometry features dominate; dynamics add a little; the condition-only family drops hardest from in-distribution to out-of-distribution (69.70 to 56.21 on Qwen with mean-difference). Geometry alone matches or beats the full feature set on four of six out-of-distribution settings while losing in-distribution, which the authors read as a robustness/specialisation trade-off. The single-state linear probe is competitive in-distribution and even beats the full feature set for one probe-vector target, but loses 9-13 macro-F1 points from in-distribution to out-of-distribution on every model–target pair. In the search application, ranking by predicted success with K = 15-20 approaches the per-item oracle at roughly half the cost of the early-stopping baseline, and at K = 20 recovers about 98 percent of the oracle's success rate using about 11 percent of the decoded tokens of exhaustive item-level search.

## Limits and open problems they name

The stated limitation is scope: two vector-construction methods and three small models, because each model–method dataset took about a day and a half on fifteen GPUs. Beyond what they name: the label is judge-derived, so the predictor can only ever predict the judge's notion of coherence; the layer of intervention is fixed rather than swept, so "steerability" here is steerability *at one site*; nothing is said about whether the same features predict the magnitude of the behavioural change rather than its coarse bucket; the success-window finding says the strength needed varies by an order of magnitude across models but gives no calibration rule that would remove the search altogether.

## What we take from it

Concretely: (1) a **measurement protocol** we should copy — the paired steered/unsteered pass over an identical token prefix, which makes "what the intervention did" a clean difference rather than a comparison of two generations; (2) a **framing** — treat the success of an edit as a property of the post-intervention state trajectory, predictable from a handful of geometric scalars, which is exactly the kind of claim a predictive white-box model should be able to make; (3) a **measure** — the cosine between the *induced deviation* and the intended vector, which is a better-behaved quantity than either the raw alignment or the deviation norm and which this paper independently isolates as the most informative feature; (4) a **warning** — the best single state-position is unstable across models and splits, so any predictor we build on a fixed (token, layer) cell is overfitting to one deployment, and the ablation table's geometry-only arm is the cheap way to check whether the rest of the features are carrying concept-specific regularities that will not transfer; (5) a **baseline we owe** — if our model claims to predict intervention outcomes, this paper's single-state linear probe is the strawman-to-beat, and it is strong in-distribution.
