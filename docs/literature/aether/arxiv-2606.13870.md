# Mirage Probes: How Vision Models Fake Visual Understanding

- **Key:** arxiv-2606.13870
- **Authors / organisation:** Daniel Ben-Levi, Judah Goldfeder, Weiliang Zhao, Raz Lapid, Amit LeVi, Allen G. Roush, Ravid Shwartz-Ziv, Hod Lipson; Columbia University, Intuit, Technion, Thoughtworks, New York University
- **Date:** 2026-06-11
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.13870
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Vision-language models sometimes answer image questions confidently and correctly with no image attached at all. Any benchmark score built on those answers measures the wrong thing. The authors argue the field has been treating this "mirage" behaviour as one failure and that it is at least two: a model answering from text priors, and a model behaving as if it had seen a picture that isn't there. The point of the paper is to bring the question down from outputs to internal state, and then to argue that the two cases need different cures, since cleaning the text distribution can only reach the first. A secondary motive is a warning about benchmark scores: a right answer is not evidence that the picture was read.

## What they studied
Two open-source VLMs are the main subjects: Ovis2.5-2B and Qwen3-32B-VL-Instruct. A third, GLM-4.6-flash, produced too few non-mirage responses on three of four benchmarks to probe, and is dropped from the main results. The unit of analysis is a **linear direction in an activation vector**, read at four sites per layer: residual-stream state, MLP output, post-attention output, and each individual attention head's output — probed at every layer, with pooling over three token-position schemes (non-special response text only; vision tokens plus the final special token; both together). The vision encoder and the projection into the language model were tried first and carried no detectable signal under any probe, so they were removed from the analysis. Stimuli come from four VQA benchmarks — VQA-RAD (free-form and yes/no radiology), MMMU-Pro, MedXpertQA, and MicroVQA — with MicroVQA also dropped for too few non-mirage items. The authors' own account of *which mechanism* a mirage belongs to is a two-way hypothesis, not a measured label.

## How they did it
Each base question is rewritten four times by GPT-4o-mini, preserving meaning and adding no information. Every variant is then run twice, once with the image and once without. A with-image response is labelled **mirage-like** if the two answers match and their bag-of-words cosine similarity exceeds 0.7; **non-mirage-like** if the no-image response says in so many words that it cannot answer without the picture (regex-detected); **ambiguous** if the no-image response was itself mirage-like but the with-image answer differed. Two alternative labelling schemes were tried and cut probe accuracy substantially, so this scheme is used throughout. Contrastive pairs are drawn only from question groups that produced at least one of each label, one pair per base question, so the two sides of a pair share an image and near-identical wording. A second, larger *all-examples* dataset takes one example per class per question. Four probe families are then trained per site: plain logistic regression; a two-layer MLP with a 512-wide GELU hidden layer; logistic regression on all layers concatenated; and logistic regression on the **difference of activations** between the with-image and without-image passes of the same example. Five seeded splits, three initialisations each, and an L2 sweep over 0, 1, 10, 100 give twelve probes per seed, of which the best on validation accuracy (early stopping, at most 800 epochs) is tested. A **Naive Bayes** classifier over unigrams and bigrams of the response text, Laplace-smoothed, is trained under the same protocol as a lexical-confound baseline, and also transferred across benchmarks. Finally a **Prior Harnessing Index** is defined as the log-probability the model gives the correct answer under the real question minus that under a null prompt ("What is the answer?"), as a per-question measure of how much the text alone supplies. Separately, GPT-5-mini is few-shot prompted to mark each question as answerable from text alone or image-reliant, and probes are trained on human-image-reliant versus image-free subsets.

## What counts as an explanation here
A feature direction plus a classifier: the understanding offered is that the property "this image-conditioned generation is a mirage" is linearly decodable from a chosen site, and that the strength of that decodability differs systematically by benchmark. The decomposition into "spurious images" versus "textual biases" is explicitly a hypothesis consistent with cross-benchmark separability and the index, not a demonstrated structure — the authors say plainly that causal validation (steering, ablation, patching) is not done and is left to future work. Nothing here is a circuit, a dictionary or a predictive model of the network's behaviour under intervention; the claim is decodability at rest.

## How they judged success
Held-out accuracy of each probe family, per site, on class-balanced and benchmark-stratified evaluation sets, averaged over five seeded splits. Baselines: the two-layer MLP and concatenated probes against plain logistic regression (a linear-versus-nonlinear contrast), and the Naive Bayes text classifier against each probe (a surface-confound control). Further controls: the ambiguous class kept as its own label; two rejected alternative labelling schemes; phrase-group match-rate differences between the two classes (six groups: reasoning scaffolding, image-grounding language, uncertainty, hedging, answer boilerplate, radiology terms) reported per benchmark; Naive Bayes transfer matrices across benchmarks; and a human-versus-model image-reliance comparison, where probes trained only on image-reliant examples are compared with probes trained only on image-free ones. There is no steering, no ablation and no patching, and the paper does not present one.

## What they found
Mirage labels are decodable in image-present activations nearly everywhere, and only modestly. Residual-stream linear probes reach at most 75.00 percent (Ovis, VQA-RAD, contrastive) and 72.00 percent (Qwen, VQA-RAD, contrastive); on attention/post-attention sites Ovis reaches 74.67 percent contrastive and 85.71 percent on the small MedXpertQA all-examples cell, Qwen 81.00 percent contrastive on VQA-RAD. MLP probes do not meaningfully beat logistic regression, and concatenating all layers does not help — the authors read this as linear encoding. The difference-of-activations probe is in a different class: 97.40 percent (Ovis residual, VQA-RAD, contrastive) down to 71.00 percent (Ovis residual, MedXpertQA, contrastive), 95.67 percent (Qwen attention, VQA-RAD) and 83.33 percent (Qwen attention, MedXpertQA), with the all-examples aggregate residual figures 92.10 and 84.38 percent. The Naive Bayes baseline sits between 45.45 and 66.67 percent in the contrastive setting and 67.50 to 81.50 percent in all-examples, so the contrastive probes stay above it everywhere while the all-examples probes do not, and the authors say the all-examples gains on MMMU-Pro and MedXpertQA track rising lexical separability. VQA-RAD is the most separable benchmark and often beats training on all three pooled. The index separates the benchmarks: mean values -0.41 (VQA-RAD), 1.20 (MMMU-Pro), 0.30 (MedXpertQA), with mirage correlation -0.36, -0.23 and 0.14 respectively. Human-marked image-reliance does not help probes (58.00 to 61.36 percent whether trained on image-free or image-reliant examples), which the authors take as evidence that model and human image-reliance differ. The contrastive datasets are small: 98 to 222 pairs per (model, benchmark) cell, and 18 for Ovis on MedXpertQA.

## Limits and open problems they name
The labels are heuristics and admit noise. Without an observable with-image mirage there is no causal check, so the central claim is correlational and the Naive Bayes baseline only rules out one class of confound. The two-mechanism account is a hypothesis. They name no claim about mitigation working. What they do not name but the numbers show: the contrastive MedXpertQA and several attention cells rest on datasets of a few dozen items with no intervals printed anywhere; the Ovis contrastive split uses hard-coded validation values; and the difference-of-activations probe is a scoring function over a pair of forward passes, so its accuracy is not the same kind of object as a single-pass probe's — it can be computed only for a question whose no-image response the model was allowed to generate.

## What we take from it
Three transferable instruments. **(1) The matched-difference readout.** The single largest accuracy jump in the paper comes not from a better probe but from probing the *contrast* between two passes that differ only in the presence of the input. For us, any question of the form "is this feature actually doing work in this input" is better posed as a readout on the difference of states between two conditions than on either state alone, because the difference cancels whatever the prompt, topic and format contribute to both. **(2) The lexical-confound baseline as a mandatory row.** A text-only classifier over the same outputs, trained under the same protocol, is the cheapest available test of whether a probe read the model or read the response, and the paper's pattern — lexicon scores 67.5 to 81.5 percent whenever the probe loosens, and the probe's apparent gain rides on it — is the shadow we should expect on our own tables. **(3) An external-index control.** The Prior Harnessing Index is a quantity the probe cannot influence, computed by the model itself, and it explained cross-benchmark variation better than human annotation did; it is the same class of anchor as an ability floor or a no-edit ceiling, and the lesson that *human* notions of what a stimulus requires are a poor proxy for the model's is one we have to apply to our own task design. The warning to carry: decodability at four sites and every layer is not localisation, and the authors are right to call it a diagnostic.
