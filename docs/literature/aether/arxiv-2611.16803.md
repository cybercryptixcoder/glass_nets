## What they wanted, and why
A monitor that reads a language model during generation can be useful three different ways: it can label the finished response, it can flag a failure once it is under way, or it can raise an alarm before the failure starts. Only the third leaves room to stop, resample or steer. The paper's claim is that the field's standard evaluation — AUROC, accuracy or F1 over whole responses — cannot tell these three apart, so a probe that only fires once the text has visibly gone wrong scores the same as one that fires first. The larger goal is an evaluation protocol that makes "warns before the onset" a measurable, comparable quantity, using loops that repeat until the token budget runs out as the worked case and linear probes on residual-stream activations as the candidate monitors.

## What they studied
Apertus-8B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Mistral-7B-Instruct, all 32 layers. Data: 3,630 prompts from seven sources (two mathematics sets, AIME 2025, instruction following, Nemotron reasoning traces, Codeforces, medical reasoning), each answered 10 times per model at temperature 0.7 and top-p 0.9 with a 4,096-token cap, splits assigned per prompt and two sources held out entirely for testing. Only about 3–11% of responses degenerate depending on model. Unit of analysis: a token position inside a response — the residual stream at one layer at that position — and two derived objects per response, the first alarm position and the annotated onset.

## How they did it
The onset is the first token of the first loop, produced by having Claude Sonnet 5 quote where the repetition begins and locating that quotation in the tokenised response, then having one of four human reviewers confirm, move or overturn the verdict on every capped test response. The protocol is three steps and deliberately minimal: calibrate a threshold on validation to the lowest value at which the false-alarm rate on healthy responses stays inside a budget the deployer picks (they use 1, 5 and 10%), run the monitor with that threshold fixed on test, then score alarms against the onset on three tasks — response classification, failure detection, early warning. Early warning is scored inside a 256-token window before the onset, with four quantities: coverage of the warning window, the share of flagged responses whose first alarm precedes the onset, the median lead in tokens, and coverage of everything before the window, which has no preferred direction and exists to stop a scorer that simply flags more everywhere from looking early. The chance reference is a random-alarm monitor with per-token probability chosen so its expected false-alarm rate matches the budget exactly. Scorers are linear probes on the layer-normed residual stream at one layer, trained with BCE on a target that is positive from 256 tokens before the onset onward, layer chosen by validation warning coverage subject to a floor of 0.3 on post-onset coverage; a variant trains rank-16 LoRA adapters on every attention and MLP projection up to the probe's layer jointly with the probe, so the representation itself changes. Six untrained baselines read length, emitted tokens (seq-rep-n, rep-over-l and repeated-n-gram), or the generator's own confidence (windowed entropy and negative log-probability), each with its window tuned on validation by the same rule. Twenty-six training strategies are compared, and two control transforms are used on the probe's own scores: permuting each response's token scores at random, and keeping only the argmax, both of which leave the response-level highest score — and therefore the AUROC and the calibrated threshold — exactly intact.

## What counts as an explanation here
No mechanism and no description: the object is a scalar per token, a probe direction plus a threshold, and the claim it carries is temporal rather than structural — that this position, read this way, is informative about something that has not happened yet. The paper is explicit that the useful content is where the score rises relative to an independently annotated event, and it treats the separation of activations (before-window, window, post-onset) as the geometric correlate of that rise rather than as an account of the computation. It predicts alarm timing, and detection and classification as by-products.

## How they judged success
Onset-aligned metrics at a fixed error budget: warning-window coverage, early-warning rate, median lead, detection rate, post-onset coverage, pre-window coverage, false-alarm rate. Both test splits are reported (human-verified and judge-labelled), plus held-out sources and both other budgets. Selection is by validation warning coverage, justified by a Kendall rank-transfer table. Controls: random alarms, permutation and argmax-only transforms, persistence of 16 and 64 consecutive flags with the threshold recalibrated, judge-versus-reviewer onset agreement, and per-seed spread over three seeds.

## What they found
Response-level metrics collapse on this failure. On Apertus the best accuracy is nearly indistinguishable across four scorers, and a rule that only scores the final token of a capped response reaches the highest AUROC on two of three models — while flagging almost nothing of the loop and being structurally incapable of warning. Across layers AUROC stays above 0.96 everywhere while warning coverage varies sevenfold, so the layers that are best for classification are uninformative about where the early signal is readable. AUROC's own ordering does transfer (Kendall τ = 0.79 validation to test) but barely predicts which probes warn (τ = 0.33); validation warning coverage predicts it at τ = 0.94.

The permutation control is the sharpest single result: shuffling each response's token scores leaves AUROC and the calibrated threshold untouched while moving the early-warning rate from 0.24 to 0.96 and the lead from about +124 tokens (after the onset) to about −816 (before it). The identical ranking of responses is compatible with almost any timing.

Frozen probes warn on most loops for Apertus and Llama — early-warning rate above one half, median first alarm 137 to 232 tokens before the onset, warning coverage 1.4 to 3 times pre-window coverage — but on Mistral every probe fires after the onset (lead positive even for the adapted probe at its own layer), with warning coverage close to pre-window coverage: a usable detector and not a warner. LoRA adaptation below the probe more than doubles warning coverage on every model (Apertus 0.186 to 0.523 at the same layer; Llama 0.087 to 0.488) while the early-warning rate barely moves (0.658 to 0.695, 0.594 to 0.655), because the extra flags fall long before the window rather than spilling into it — and on Llama the adapted probe at layer 6 is a lower-AUROC probe that warns less, so adaptation's benefit is layer-and-model-specific rather than monotone. No untrained baseline warns much more than random: the long-window repetition rules cover more of the warning window but also cover much more of everything before it, and their alarms come well after the onset. Thresholds calibrated at 5% carry over to in-domain test (4.5–5.6%) but the frozen probes lose most of their warning on the two held-out sources.

## Limits and open problems they name
Lead times are definition-sensitive: a later onset convention makes every alarm look earlier. A response that loops and then emits the end of sequence counts as healthy, which makes the false-alarm rate conservative. Repetition that the prompt asked for, or that does work (brute-force search, enumeration), is labelled healthy but is never tested as a case the probes might confuse with degeneration. On Apertus and Llama the onset usually comes after a healthy response would have ended, so part of what the probes flag may be response length, and on Llama the adapted probe's early flags plausibly do partly track length. Each response is read by one reviewer only, so inter-reviewer agreement is unmeasured, and on Apertus the judge's onset runs 380 to 1,250 tokens late on ten responses, which the authors show inflates warning coverage by up to 0.12 on the held-out split. The training strategy and selection rule were chosen on this one failure; acting on a warning is left to future work and is not evaluated.

## What we take from it
A protocol (calibrate-then-freeze at a chosen error budget, then score alarms against an independent onset on all three tasks) and a set of metrics we can lift almost verbatim for any time-indexed read-out of ours. A measure we did not have: warning-window coverage paired with pre-window coverage as a simultaneous pair, because either alone is gameable in opposite directions. A control worth stealing outright: an order-destroying permutation of the per-token scores, which holds the response-level aggregate fixed and leaves only the timing to explain — the cheapest possible test of whether a monitor's performance lives in its ordering or in its placement. A warning that matters for us directly: AUROC over whole responses is not merely insensitive here, it is actively misleading, and a length-only rule beats every learned scorer on it. And a structural lesson about representation-versus-read-out: adaptation did not make the Mistral probe early, because changing the representation cannot supply a signal that is not yet present in the prefix, whereas on Apertus the adapted probe flags degenerate responses more than it flags healthy ones at any of the first 2,048 positions, which is the pattern of a judgement rather than a length artefact.
