## What they wanted, and why

Sparse autoencoders are sold as the instrument that resolves a model's activations into human-readable features, and the standard justification says that if the true features are sparse and linear, dictionary learning recovers them. The authors ask what happens to that promise when the true features are not merely sparse *and* linear but also **nested** — when one concept always fires alongside a broader one. Their answer is that the same sparsity penalty that makes the dictionary readable then makes it wrong in a specific, silent way, and they give that failure a name. The larger goal is to stop the field from quoting a latent's maximum-activating examples as evidence of what it does: a latent can look like a clean classifier of a concept while quietly refusing to fire on a subset of the very inputs that concept covers. They frame the stakes as the difference between a dictionary that is a set of features and a dictionary that is a set of gerrymandered classifiers.

## What they studied

Mostly Gemma 2 2B with the released Gemma Scope residual-stream dictionaries — the full 16k- and 65k-width grid across layers 0-25 — plus their own trained dictionaries on Qwen2 0.5B (L1 and TopK arms) and Llama 3.2 1B (L1 and TopK arms) over the first eight layers. The task that makes the failure measurable is deliberately small: given an in-context prompt of the form "<word> has the first letter: <letter>", read the residual stream at the probed token and predict the token's first letter. The unit of analysis is the individual latent, judged by its precision and recall as a classifier of one first-letter feature and by its causal effect under ablation, with two toy models (4 orthogonal features in 50 dimensions; 12 features for the TopK arm) used to isolate the mechanism.

## How they did it

Three layers of evidence. First, an analytic one: they parameterise a one-parameter family of encoder/decoder pairs for a parent and child feature by an absorption strength, show by direct case analysis that reconstruction is exactly perfect for every value of that parameter whenever the child never fires without the parent, and show the sparsity term for those two latents falls linearly in the parameter — so the loss strictly prefers full absorption, and the proof is arithmetic over three input cases, not a training run. Second, a toy replication: four orthogonal unit-norm features, one firing at 25 percent and the rest at 5 percent, an SAE with exactly as many latents as features, so anything learned is a choice about how to represent rather than about capacity; then the same setup with the child allowed to fire only when the parent fires, with firing magnitudes given noise, with 95-percent rather than exact co-occurrence, and with a batch-TopK objective in place of L1. Third, the real dictionaries: for each of up to 26 first-letter features per dictionary they find the latent with the highest cosine similarity to a logistic-regression probe trained on the same activations (and check that a k=1 sparse probe selects the same latent), then compare that latent's F1 against the probe's, broken down by width, L0 and layer. Recall failures are then explained causally: for the tokens the probe gets right and all the split latents miss, they run an integrated-gradients ablation over every latent, and if the latent with by far the largest negative ablation effect is aligned with the probe at all — small cosine, large ablation lead — they call the case an absorption and count these against the probe's true positives. The causal requirement is what bounds the metric's usable depth, since once attention has moved the spelling information to the final position, ablating the source token does nothing.

## What counts as an explanation here

Two explanations of different kinds. The formal one is causal-mechanistic and complete in a tiny world: a parameterised weight construction plus a proposition showing that a degenerate-looking dictionary is strictly loss-better than the readable one, so the failure is not an optimiser accident. The empirical one is a *detector* — a named per-dictionary statistic, the absorption rate, defined as a ratio of causally-confirmed absorptions to probe true positives — accompanied by one worked case at latent-level granularity: which latent carries the concept, which latent steals it, how much of the probe direction each contributes, and what happens when the stealing component is projected out before the ablation. It is an explanation of *why a latent's measured behaviour differs from its apparent interpretation*, not a model of the network.

## How they judged success

For the toy models: whether each latent's encoder and decoder cosines with the true features match the ground-truth basis — checked against an independent-features control arm where the SAE recovers all four perfectly. For the language models: per-feature F1 of the best probe-aligned latent against the logistic-regression probe as an upper reference (not as ground truth — it is a decoder ceiling, not the model's own computation), precision and recall separately against L0 and width, and the causal ablation metric of correct-letter logit minus the mean of the other letter logits. The absorption rate is itself a measure with named thresholds — a minimum probe cosine of 0.025, an ablation lead of at least 1.0 over the runner-up latent, a feature-split detection threshold of 0.03 F1 jump — and the paper prints the sensitivity analysis showing that any threshold in a stated range gives the same answers. The strongest single check is the projection control: when the probe direction is removed from the stealing latent before ablation, its ablation effect collapses, which rules out the stealing latent mattering for some unrelated reason.

## What they found

The control arm is clean — independent features are recovered exactly, one latent per feature — and adding the hierarchy alone breaks it: the parent's encoder acquires a hole at the child, and the child's decoder absorbs the parent direction. The real-model case is the memorable one: in a layer-3 16k dictionary at L0 59, a latent scoring 0.81 F1 on "starts with S" simply does not fire on the token for "short", while a token-aligned latent for that word fires with roughly five times the magnitude at about a fifth the probe cosine, contributing a comparable share of the "starts with S" direction, and carries a much larger ablation effect than any other latent; projecting out its probe component removes that causal effect. F1 by L0 traces the precision-recall trade-off rather than a best operating point — sparse dictionaries give high-precision, low-recall latents and dense ones the reverse — and no dictionary matches the probe. Absorption, by their conservative count, occurs in every architecture family tested and rises with L0 and with width, which is the paper's central negative claim: the standard knobs (wider, sparser) make the problem worse, not better. Even the batch-TopK toy, with no L1 term at all, learns the same absorbing solution, so the cause is the preference for fewer active latents rather than the specific penalty.

## Limits and open problems they name

The metric needs causal confirmation, so it cannot be applied past roughly layer 18 in Gemma 2 2B, where attention has already moved the spelling information to the prediction position; the authors say absorption almost certainly continues there and give three alternative formulations in an appendix, each with the trade-off printed. The metric is deliberately conservative — it only counts cases where a single latent carries a large ablation lead and the main latents are fully silent — so the reported rates are floors; partial absorption, multiple simultaneous absorbers, and weakly-firing parents are all excluded and discussed. The task is one narrow feature family (first letters), so cross-feature generality is asserted from the sweep rather than proven. Their own comparison against the probe is an upper reference, and they caution against reading an "optimal L0" off their curves because the variance by letter is large. Proposed fixes are named but not tested: meta-dictionaries that decompose a latent into atoms, attribution-based dictionary learning, and structured sparsity such as group lasso or hierarchical sparse coding.

## What we take from it

A measure, a warning, and a proof template. The measure is the absorption rate, and the part to copy is its construction rather than its thresholds: a candidate false-negative set defined by an independent probe, a causal test that the *substitute* latent is the one that matters, and a projection control that isolates the shared direction from everything else the latent may carry. Our own accounts need the same discipline whenever we say "this direction means X" — the claim is about what the direction does on the inputs where the probe says it should fire, not about the inputs where it happens to fire. The warning generalises past dictionaries: any account of a network as a set of parts plus a sparse combiner inherits an incentive to trade a shared part for a more specific one, and the more specific part will look cleaner in isolation. The proof template is worth keeping verbatim in spirit — a one-parameter construction that keeps reconstruction exactly fixed while moving the penalty monotonically is the cheapest possible demonstration that a failure is built into the objective, and it is a five-line check we can run for any stack of parts we propose. Finally, this is the folder's clearest statement that **a latent's recall is not observable from its dashboard**, which is the same lesson as the earlier entries on reconstruction-versus-recovery, arrived at one level down. Coreference `arxiv-2505.11756` (the mirror failure: correlated features merged across latents rather than a single direction split into an encoder hole, and the diagnostic that decoder motion means hedging while encoder-only motion means absorption), `arxiv-2508.16560` (the L0-as-budget argument that predicts both), and `arxiv-2605.09887` (feature directions as local objects whose validity is input-dependent — the manifold-side version of the same "the direction is not a property of the latent alone" claim).
