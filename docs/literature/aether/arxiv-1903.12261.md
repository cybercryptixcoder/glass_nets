# Benchmarking Neural Network Robustness to Common Corruptions and Perturbations

- **Key:** arxiv-1903.12261
- **Authors / organisation:** Dan Hendrycks (University of California, Berkeley); Thomas G. Dietterich (Oregon State University)
- **Date:** 2019-03 (v1 2019-03-28; ICLR 2019 camera-ready)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.12261
- **Kind:** benchmark
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

The authors set out to replace ad-hoc robustness testing with two fixed instruments. On their account the word "robustness" had become a synonym for worst-case adversarial attack — classifiers are scored on perturbations chosen specifically to fool them — while the degradations that actually accumulate in deployment (sensor noise, defocus, weather, compression) were measured on bespoke test sets that no two papers shared. They also wanted to separate two things a single worst-case number conflates: how well a network *classifies* degraded input, and how stable its *decision* is when it is nudged. The motivation is deployment safety, stated plainly in the abstract and repeated in the conclusion, and the secondary ambition is to settle whether a decade of architectural progress bought any robustness at all or merely accuracy. The paper is an instrument paper: no mechanism is proposed, and the object built is a pair of datasets plus a set of deliberately ratio-normalised metrics.

## What they studied

Image classifiers trained only on clean images, evaluated on two new datasets derived from the ImageNet validation set. **ImageNet-C** is fifteen algorithmic corruption families — three noise, four blur, three weather, four digital — each at five severities, giving 75 test conditions, plus four *extra* families held back for validation. **ImageNet-P** is ten *perturbation sequences* per validation image, each more than thirty frames, where consecutive frames differ by a small, non-adversarial change: noise perturbations are re-drawn independent of each other, while motion blur, zoom blur, snow, brightness, translate, rotate, tilt and scale are applied cumulatively so each frame is a perturbation of the previous one. Models are the standard ladder of the period (AlexNet, SqueezeNet, VGG-11/19 with and without batch norm, ResNet-18/50) plus a set of robustness interventions (CLAHE preprocessing, multigrid and MSDNet multiscale architectures, DenseNet-121/161, ResNeXt-50/101, stylised-ImageNet training, Adversarial Logit Pairing) and four deliberate failure cases. The unit of analysis is the whole model: one scalar per model per condition per metric.

## How they did it

The paper first writes the three robustness concepts out as expectations — corruption robustness averages accuracy over a distribution of corruption functions, perturbation robustness averages the probability that the prediction is unchanged under a perturbation function, and adversarial robustness is the minimum over a small ball — so that the two new benchmarks are explicitly approximations to the first two, with the perturbation distribution chosen to be classifier-agnostic and the corruptions drawn from categories that occur in natural photographs. Corruption scoring then goes through three normalisations: per-corruption error summed over the five severities and divided by AlexNet's sum, which the authors call Corruption Error and justify as cancelling the fact that fog is intrinsically harder than brightness; the mean over the fifteen families, mCE; and a second version that subtracts each model's clean error from numerator and denominator before the ratio, giving Relative mCE, described as the part of the error attributable to the corruption rather than to the model's general quality. Perturbation scoring counts, per sequence, how often the top-1 prediction changes between adjacent frames — or between a frame and the clean frame for the non-temporal noise sequences — divides by sequence length and example count to give a Flip Probability, and ratios it against AlexNet's to give a Flip Rate, with no relative version because the authors say they found no natural formulation. A second perturbation metric compares the *ranked* top-5 lists at each pair of frames through a permutation distance that only counts displacement inside the top five, giving Top-5 Distance and its normalised mean. Statistics are never used: no seeds, no intervals, no significance tests — each model is a single number per condition. Four interventions are run on ResNet-50 and scored on both metric families, and each is deliberately reported even when it fails.

## What counts as an explanation here

A measurement instrument, and explicitly nothing else. The explanatory form is a *normalised comparative score*: mCE and Relative mCE place heterogeneous models on one scale by using a single reference network as the unit, and the perturbation metrics do the same with a reference flip rate. It is meant to be partial and durable — the claim is never why a network fails, only that under this fixed instrument these failures are comparable across architectures, papers and years, which is what makes the number citable evidence rather than advocacy. The one causal-sounding passage is architectural: multiscale networks appear to suppress distracting pixel noise because they carry fine detail alongside coarse representation, but the authors present that as a hypothesis the benchmark is consistent with, and their implementation confounds it with parameter count and clean accuracy.

## How they judged success

The benchmark is validated by divergence and by honest failure, not by a held-out prediction test. Construct validity is argued structurally: the corruptions are diverse and numerous, the generation code is published so the test set can be rebuilt, training on the scored corruptions is forbidden, and a *separate* set of four validation corruptions exists so that a method can be tuned for robustness without contaminating the score. The two corruption metrics are run in parallel precisely because they can disagree, and the disagreement is treated as the result. Perturbation robustness is validated as a distinct construct by showing that models that improve on one axis do not automatically improve on the other — VGG nets are worse than ResNets under corruption yet as good or better under perturbation, and batch norm *helps* corruption while *hurting* perturbation in VGG-19. The intervention ladder serves as a sensitivity check in both directions: some interventions work and some fail, and the failures carry their numbers.

## What they found

Under raw mCE the architecture ladder reads as steady progress — AlexNet 100.0 at 43.5% clean error, SqueezeNet 104.4 at 41.8%, VGG-11 93.5 at 31.0%, VGG-19 88.9 at 27.6%, VGG-19+BN 81.6 at 25.8%, ResNet-18 84.7 at 30.2%, ResNet-50 76.7 at 23.9% — and the authors restate their earlier conclusion that the gain is mostly explained by clean accuracy. Under Relative mCE every post-AlexNet model in the ladder is wearing: SqueezeNet 117.9, VGG-11 123.3, VGG-19 122.9, VGG-19+BN 111.1, ResNet-18 103.9, ResNet-50 105.0, so none of them degrades better than AlexNet, and the summary line is that corruption robustness "in itself" has barely moved. On perturbation, instability is large and unprompted by any adversary: a ResNet-18 flips its top-1 between adjacent frames of the scale sequences with probability 15.6% and has an unstandardised top-5 distance of 3.6 there, and the mean flip rates span 58.0% for ResNet-50 up to 112.6% for SqueezeNet against AlexNet's 100. Improvements are real and are reported per intervention: CLAHE preprocessing plus five-epoch fine-tuning lowered ResNet-50's mCE from 76.7 to 74.5 while clean error fell 23.87% to 23.55%; MSDNet 73.6 and multigrid 73.3 at clean errors of 24.6; DenseNet-121 73.4 with Relative mCE 92.8 at 25.6% clean; ResNeXt-50 68.2 and 88.6 at 22.9%; DenseNet-161 66.4 and 84.6 at 22.9%; ResNeXt-101 62.2 and 80.1 at 21.0%; stylised-ImageNet training took ResNet-50 to 69.3. The same ordering reappears on perturbation — ResNet-50 mFR 58.0 and mT5D 78.3, DenseNet-121 56.4 and 76.8, ResNeXt-50 52.4 and 74.2, DenseNet-161 46.9 and 69.5, ResNeXt-101 43.2 and 65.9 — so the large feature-aggregating models gain on both axes, and the authors note the robustness gain outpaces the accuracy gain. Four interventions fail: stability training is worse than baseline mCE at every tuned noise strength and stability weight, and barely moves perturbation (best tuned mFR 57% against the baseline's 58%); non-local-means denoising raises mCE from 76.7 to 82.1 because the residual smoothing damages the non-noise families; smaller CondenseNets are less robust than larger models of similar accuracy (80.8 mCE at 26.3% error, 84.6 at 28.9%); ten-crop averaging buys accuracy and not robustness. The most striking single result is Adversarial Logit Pairing — a defence that was broken as an adversarial defence — which cuts Tiny ImageNet-P mean flip probability and mean top-5 distance by 41% and 40% relative, i.e. a mechanism designed against worst-case gradients transfers to average-case natural perturbations far better than anything purpose-built. A subtype experiment on ImageNet-22K closes the paper: after fine-tuning only the last layer to 25 broad types, accuracy on unseen subtypes trails accuracy on seen ones, and the architectures tested hardly depart from a common trendline.

## Limits and open problems they name

The scoring is single-run per model, with no seeds, no error bars and no statistical comparison, so the intervention table's small deltas carry no uncertainty. Both metrics depend on AlexNet as denominator, and the sensitivity of conclusions to that choice is not tested — the authors note any baseline would work, including a uniform one, but do not show what changes. There is no matched human measurement on ImageNet-C or ImageNet-P; the "subhuman" framing rests on prior literature. The Top-5 Distance is a bespoke permutation metric with no external validation, and the authors decline to define a relative version for perturbation, so their one degradation-relative instrument has no counterpart on the stability axis — a real asymmetry given that Relative mCE is the paper's headline result. Calibration differences between models are cited as the reason not to use predicted probabilities at all, which leaves confidence-based stability scores untested here. The authors also state that fitting the scored corruptions is forbidden while offering four validation-only families, and their own failure cases show how easily a targeted fix damages the untargeted families — a warning they do not turn into a policy.

## What we take from it

- **A ratio-to-a-reference-normaliser is a construct choice, not a formatting choice, and the pair of ratios is the deliverable.** Here the raw aggregate and the clean-error-subtracted aggregate support opposite conclusions about six years of architectures, and the paper leads with the second. For our batteries: any score computed under intervention should be published twice, raw and with the reference model's own score subtracted, with the reference named; if the ranking flips, that is the finding.
- **Stability of a decision is a different measurement from correctness of a decision, and it needs a sequence construction.** ImageNet-P's design is copyable in a paragraph: take a real input, apply an imperceptible *cumulative* change across many frames, and count how often the predicted label changes between adjacent frames. For probing we can build the analogue — interpolate activations along a small path between two real examples and count how often a read-out's argmax flips — and it costs no labels and no adversary.
- **Score every intervention on the whole battery, including the families it was not designed for.** The denoising result is the cleanest example in this folder: a fix that removed the noise it targeted raised aggregate error by 5.4 points because its residual smoothing leaked into the other families. Our probe-battery rule follows: an intervention claimed to improve a read-out is scored on every condition, and its failures are published with their numbers.
- **Report the ladder, not the champion.** Five success cases and four failure cases, each with numbers, is what makes the paper citable as evidence; the failure list also does real theoretical work, because ALP showing that a broken adversarial defence produces large natural-perturbation robustness is the paper's most surprising result and could only appear in a table that had room for failures.
- **A fixed reference model is also a fixed bias, and the fix is a second reference.** Because every number is divided by AlexNet's, the absolute scale is arbitrary and the paper's headline "no progress" claim is a claim *relative to AlexNet*. When we anchor a battery to a reference model, the report should say so in the units line and, where feasible, repeat the table against a second reference — the same discipline as a seed-matched null.
