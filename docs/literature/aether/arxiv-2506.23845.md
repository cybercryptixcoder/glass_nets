## What they wanted, and why
Two large evaluations had just reported that sparse autoencoders lose to plainly baser alternatives — logistic probing on the raw representation for detection, and ordinary prompting or fine-tuning for steering — and part of the mechanistic-interpretability community read that as a verdict on dictionaries as such. The authors of this position paper want to argue the verdict is scoped: those evaluations all test *acting on a concept named in advance*, while the literature that reports dictionaries working is uniformly about *finding a concept nobody named* — feature-level hypothesis generation over a text corpus, or accounts of what a model does while performing a task. The larger goal is to keep a research programme alive by restating what its success criterion is, and to push the method out of mech-interp into fairness auditing, model explanation and the social and health sciences, where the useful object is a natural-language concept that predicts or explains an outcome.

## What they studied
No model of their own and no new experiments. The unit of study is the *published claim*, and they sort the SAE literature into two regimes by whether the concept is an input or an output of the workflow. The models and representations that appear are the ones their cited papers use: Gemma-2-9B's final-token representations and a Gemma-2-9B SAE in the detection evaluation, Gemma-2-2B representations in the steering one, and, on the positive side, SAEs trained on sentence embeddings of news headlines and Congressional speeches, and sparse-coding methods run over a language model's internal states during poem completion and arithmetic. The unit of analysis is the SAE latent — a sparse feature dimension — and the qualitative concept its top-activating texts are summarised into.

## How they did it
The argument is documentary. First a primer: an autoencoder with an overcomplete latent, sparsified either by an L1 penalty on the latent vector or by keeping only the K largest activations after the encoder, with a linear decoder reconstructing the input; sparse features arise because a single input contains few concepts even though the dataset spans many. Second, the negative results are described in enough detail to classify them: 113 binary detection tasks with classifiers fitted on SAE latents versus on the raw representation, across class imbalance, data scarcity and label noise; 500 concepts with synthetic texts and several baselines including a bag-of-words model and plain prompting; a steering setup where the most concept-predictive feature is scaled up at generation time and an LLM judge rates fluency, relevance and concept inclusion. Third, the positive results are described the same way — a pipeline that trains an SAE, selects features predictive of a target variable, auto-interprets them, and emits the descriptions as hypotheses to be tested; and an account in which a neuron standing for a rhyme-relevant word is read as evidence of forward planning, confirmed by further interventions. Then a use-case survey and a section rehearsing the two obvious objections. Every consistent finding is reorganised into a two-by-two of named-versus-unnamed against detector-versus-generator, and the argument is that the cells line up.

## What counts as an explanation here
A dictionary of natural-language concept descriptions, each attached to a sparse latent, produced automatically by showing a language model the texts that activate a feature most and least and asking for the shared property. The paper makes no claim that this dictionary is complete or that a feature honours its description. Its distinctive move is a *second, weaker contract*: the description only has to be good enough to name the concept, at which point the researcher annotates the data directly with that concept and stops using activations altogether. The explanation is thus a *hypothesis generator* whose output leaves the interpretability pipeline and is tested downstream — as a predictor of engagement, as a discriminator between two model sizes, or as a claim about how a computation is staged. Predictions are made only in that downstream sense: a named concept should correlate with the target, or should be active during the task, and a human-designed study can check it.

## How they judged success
By re-reading other people's evaluations, not by running one. Detection is judged by classifier accuracy under the stated perturbations; steering by LLM-judge scores on fluency, relevance and concept inclusion; hypothesis generation by the count of statistically significant hypotheses against baselines and by human ratings of helpfulness; and model-behaviour accounts by whether an intervention confirms the mechanism the feature suggested. There is no baseline, ablation or quantitative control introduced here that the cited papers did not already own. The paper's own success condition is argumentative: whether every negative result it cites genuinely falls in the named-concept regime and every positive result in the unnamed one, which the reader is invited to check against Table 2's four example prompts.

## What they found
The paper claims a clean split. Negative evaluations cover concept detection, steering and unlearning, all with the concept fixed beforehand; positive results cover feature-level hypothesis generation and mechanistic accounts of behaviour, all with the concept to be discovered. It offers a mechanism for the negative half that follows from the architecture rather than from an experiment: a reconstruction cannot carry more information about a token than the activation it was built from, so a classifier fitted on latents has strictly less signal than one fitted on the representation, and for steering there is a cheaper path because instruction-following is already trained in. The positive half is attributed to the joint tractability and specificity of the latent set — a description precise enough to separate a single animal from animals in general is what makes a planning claim answerable. The paper then lists concrete discovery targets in fairness and auditing (which unnamed input features move a high-stakes decision), in the social sciences (which concepts in a speech corpus track a historical shift) and in health (which note features predict an outcome), and frames all of them as closing a prediction-explanation gap.

## Limits and open problems they name
They state their own inverse explicitly: a reader who thinks the important problems are steering and detection will not be moved, because the paper grants that regime and has nothing to add to it. They also concede the dichotomy may not be a complete explanation — they show the negative results sit in the named regime and give reasons to expect that, but do not rule out dictionaries becoming competitive at named-concept tasks as the method improves, and point at work already reporting that. Nothing here is measured: the load-bearing claims are a classification of prior results and an information-theoretic intuition about reconstruction, and no experiment separates "dictionaries are worse at named concepts because of reconstruction" from "the baselines at named concepts are simply strong". The cited positive results also carry their own baselines, which this paper reports as given rather than re-checking.

## What we take from it
A *framing*, and a warning about how we state our own goal. The framing is that a concept can be an input or an output of the account-building step, and that these are different tasks with different ceilings — a distinction our write-ups should carry explicitly, because a record of probing that found concept C is an input-concept claim and belongs in the regime this paper says does not need a dictionary. The warning is sharper. The argument that reconstruction cannot exceed the representation is a general information bound on every dictionary-first account in our folder: whatever we build on a sparse reconstruction is bounded by what the reconstruction retained, so the unexplained share should be measured and reported rather than assumed small. The constructive half is the falsifiability clause, which is the most portable thing in the paper: once a concept is named, discard the activations and test the description against annotation of the data, so that an unreliable feature is caught downstream instead of silently costing a detection score. That is a cheap experiment we can run and do not.
