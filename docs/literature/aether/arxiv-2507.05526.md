## What they wanted, and why

The question is the interventional one: given only observational data, what is the distribution of an outcome when we force some other variable to a value? If the causal graph is known this is standard causal inference; if it is not, the graph must be discovered, and observational data typically cannot single out one graph — it pins down a Markov equivalence class, and two graphs in the same class can license very different interventional answers. The authors' motivating complaint is that methods which commit to one structure are therefore overconfident in a way that is invisible downstream. The principled fix — average the interventional distribution over a posterior over both structures and functional mechanisms — is only a formula: the graph space grows super-exponentially in the node count, and the inner posterior over functions is analytically tractable only for toy mechanisms. The larger goal is to make that double marginalisation *computable at run time* while keeping the uncertainty it expresses, and to do so without a hand-tuned approximation pipeline in which every stage can be wrong.

## What they studied

Synthetic structural causal models generated at training time from an explicitly declared prior. The units are small graphs (two-node bivariate linear Gaussian; three-node Erdős–Rényi; 20 to 40 nodes Erdős–Rényi and scale-free) with two functional-mechanism families — Gaussian processes with squared-exponential kernels and random lengthscales, and residual or shallow networks with a latent noise input — plus one real proteomics dataset (Sachs, 11 proteins). The unit of analysis is neither a node nor an edge but the **posterior interventional distribution** itself, `p(x_i | do(x_j), D_obs)`: a full mixture distribution per intervention query. The model is MACE-TNP, a transformer neural process with alternating sample-wise and node-wise attention, whose decoder emits a mixture of Gaussians; the trained network is a function from dataset to interventional distribution.

## How they did it

They set out the Bayesian target explicitly: a hierarchical model over graphs, mechanisms and data, and the posterior interventional distribution as the sum over graphs of the integral over mechanisms of the mechanism-conditioned intervention. They then replace that computation with a learned map. Training minimises expected KL divergence from the true posterior interventional distribution, over a task distribution they construct by simulation: sample a graph from the prior, sample every mechanism conditional on it, draw a fixed budget of observational samples, then — reusing the same graph and mechanisms — draw intervention values and sample an interventional dataset, and pick one outcome node. Because the tasks come from a known generative prior, the supply of tasks is unlimited and the KL objective, in the infinite-data and infinite-capacity limit, is minimised only by a network that reproduces the exact posterior map. The architecture is chosen to respect the invariances they argue the target has: permutation invariance over observational samples and over marginalised nodes, permutation equivariance over queries, and over swaps of outcome and intervention node; six separate embedding networks encode the role of each (node type × sample type) cell, and the model factorises over queries so it emits a conditional mixture per query. They deliberately do not learn correlation across interventional samples. Evaluation splits a closed-form case from the rest: where the posterior interventional distribution has a known analytic form (bivariate linear Gaussian, identifiable and non-identifiable) they measure KL to the analytic answer as a function of observational sample size; everywhere else they score the held-out interventional outcomes under the model's predicted density — a negative log-posterior interventional density — against five baselines (three GP-network Bayesian methods differing in how they infer over graphs, one neural Bayesian method with point-estimate mechanisms, one non-Bayesian single-graph method). In-distribution, out-of-distribution (train on GP data, test on network data and vice versa), node-scaling, and a zero-shot transfer to real data reduce to those same comparisons. Ablations vary mixture-component count, the sample-attention scheme, model width and depth.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **replacement model for the inferential pipeline** — not a description of a mechanism, not a graph, not a feature dictionary. The trained network is a surrogate for the whole Bayesian computation: its output is a distribution over the quantity a Bayesian would have computed, and the claim is that this surrogate converges to the Bayesian answer rather than being an approximation of convenience. It is explicitly *partial*: the network carries no reportable posterior over graphs, no per-edge attribution, and no mechanism. The prior is not inspectable in the trained object either — it is baked into the task distribution, so a reader cannot ask which graph was favoured, only what distribution was predicted. The single place where the surrogate is opened up is the non-identifiable case, where the output mixture's components can be read as the two members of the equivalence class each retaining half the mass; the paper shows this compactly and does not develop it into an analysis tool.

## How they judged success

Three measures, cleanly separated. (1) **KL divergence to the analytic posterior interventional distribution** where it exists, reported as median with ten- to ninety-percent quantiles across datasets, plus a second KL against the distribution conditioned on the *true* graph and mechanism, which isolates whether the graph was identified rather than merely averaged over. (2) **Negative log-posterior interventional density (NLPID)** on held-out outcomes when no analytic answer exists — the score of the true interventional outcomes under the predicted density, a strictly proper predictive comparison, with the error of the mean over 100 datasets. (3) A **single-graph baseline** (non-Bayesian) included precisely so that the value of carrying uncertainty can be measured, not assumed. Controls are the two-directional out-of-distribution test, the in-distribution-versus-out-of-distribution pairing of identical architectures, and the ablation grid. There is no statistical test beyond the reported error of the mean, and no held-out calibration check on the uncertainty the model reports.

## What they found

In the bivariate linear Gaussian case the KL to the analytic posterior interventional distribution goes to zero as the observational sample grows, **in both the identifiable and the non-identifiable prior** — the model reproduces the Bayesian mixture including its stubborn bimodality. The second KL tells a different story and is the most instructive number in the paper: against the distribution conditioned on the true mechanism it does *not* go to zero in the non-identifiable setting, which is exactly correct, because no method can. Supplying merely five true interventional samples lowers that residual, and with fifty to three hundred the network recovers the true generating mechanism's interventional distribution almost exactly even with little observational data — identifiability bought with interventions rather than with assumptions. In the three-node case the network implicitly performs the right adjustment: it converges for confounder and mediator graphs alike, and flattens at a positive constant when the confounder is unobserved and the target is not identifiable. Against baselines, in-distribution, it wins on both mechanism families (GP data 563.9 against 644.2 for the best Bayesian baseline and 749.4 for the single-graph one; network data 527.9 against 588.0 and 815.6), and it holds from 20 to 40 nodes where the most expensive MCMC baseline is simply infeasible and the single-graph baseline degrades sharply (660.4 against 686.3 and 942.7 at twenty nodes, 665.8 against 683.0 and 986.0 at forty). Out of distribution the gap opens as expected — a GP-trained model on network data 608.3 against 527.9 in-distribution — and training on the union nearly restores it (531.0 and 583.9). Zero-shot on Sachs, with no retraining, it is statistically tied with the strongest baseline (998.9 against 1000.9) and well ahead of the GP-network methods (~1400–1420) and the single-graph baseline (1763.7). Ablations: mixture components matter a lot from one to three and little from three to ten, adding cross-attention over samples helps, and scaling width and depth helps modestly.

## Limits and open problems they name

Training-time compute and data are the stated cost: the largest model is 2.5 million synthetic datasets for two epochs, roughly twenty hours on an A100-class GPU, and the three-node models four to six hours. Generalisation depends on the test regime being covered by the training prior — the out-of-distribution results show the failure directly and the fix (add data, retrain) is cheap but not free. Attention is quadratic in variables and samples. They name two open items: the interventional *sample complexity* needed for accurate estimation, and how to construct the prior over Bayesian causal models so that it covers the complexities of real data. Unexamined by them but visible in the design: no hidden confounders is assumed throughout, and every identifiability claim is inside that assumption.

## What we take from it

Three things, one of them a warning.

**A "prior over accounts" is a training distribution, and that is both the defence and the soft spot.** The method's principled character rests entirely on the simulation prior encoding what the model is allowed to believe, and the out-of-distribution table is the receipt for what happens outside it: the same architecture drops by about eighty points of NLPID when the test mechanism is not the trained one, and recovers most of it when the prior is widened. Mechanical rule for us: **when we amortise a white-box readout — training a network to predict a posterior instead of computing it — the write-up prints the task distribution the network was trained on as an explicit list of regime dimensions, and reports a table of scores *outside* it, dimension by dimension, before any claim that the readout works.** An amortised predictor without that table is a claim about its own training prior.

**Two errors, two numbers, and they must never be combined into one.** The single most transferable design choice: they always report the KL to the Bayes-optimal answer and the KL to the *true-structure* answer separately, because the first can be zero while the second cannot. That is what lets the package of results say something honest — the model is right about what any method could know and wrong about what none could, and the paper can point at the two curves. Mechanically: **every predictive account we report carries (a) a score against the best achievable under the information we had, and (b) a score against the truth, and a good (a) with a bad (b) is reported as a statement about the information, not about the model.**

**Amortisation converts per-query cost into a per-regime cost, and that trade only pays under a specific usage pattern.** They pay twenty GPU-hours and millions of simulated datasets once to make each subsequent query "a forward pass", and that is the honest framing of what the amortised route to a guarantee buys: not a guarantee, but a *fixed* cost that is independent of the query. For a single query it is absurd; for the several hundred queries per evaluation regime this folder's own account-fitting pipeline implies (cf. the per-account cost note of entry 545) it is the only affordable shape. Mechanically: **any amortised readout we propose states the number of queries it will answer, the per-regime training cost, and the break-even query count against the direct method — and if the projected query count is below break-even, the direct method is used and the amortised one is a research item.**
