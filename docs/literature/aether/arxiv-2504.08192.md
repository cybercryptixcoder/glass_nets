# SAEs Can Improve Unlearning: Dynamic Sparse Autoencoder Guardrails for Precision Unlearning in LLMs

- **Key:** arxiv-2504.08192
- **Authors / organisation:** Aashiq Muhamed, Mona Diab, Virginia Smith (Carnegie Mellon University); Jacopo Bonato (work done at Leonardo Labs, now at Prometeia S.p.A.)
- **Date:** 2025-04-11 (v1), 2026-10-10 (v3); COLM 2025
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.08192
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (abstract, SS1-SS5, references, Appendices A-M with Tables 3-16; license CC BY 4.0)
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

An earlier attempt to unlearn with sparse-autoencoder features - find the features that fire a lot on the forget set and clamp them every time they fire - produced such collateral damage on general ability that it lost to ordinary gradient-based unlearning. This paper's question is whether that verdict was about SAEs or about the way the earlier work intervened: clamping globally, with no notion of whether a given input had anything to do with the knowledge being removed. The authors argue that the same feature set, applied *only when the input looks forget-relevant*, can beat the gradient methods on both forgetting and utility. Their larger aim is an unlearning method that is cheap (no backward pass through the model), stable under its own hyperparameters, survives sequential requests and relearning attacks, and is legible enough that a human can read the feature list and say what is being switched off - which is the part that matters to our project, because it means an internal unit is being named, selected, amplified/clamped, and scored on behaviour.

## What they studied

Google's Gemma 2 2B instruction-tuned model with the Gemma Scope residual-stream SAE at layer 3 (16k width; L0 142 for the data-set experiments and 59 for the zero-shot ones), used on its own - no training of either model or dictionary in the main experiments. The unit of analysis is the single SAE feature, plus a per-sequence statistic built from a set of them. Two target models are also made by fine-tuning Gemma 2 2B on each MUSE corpus. Benchmarks: WMDP-Bio and WMDP-Cyber (hazardous-knowledge multiple choice, with PubMed papers and GitHub repositories as the respective forget sets and WikiText as the retain set), MUSE NEWS and BOOKS (verbatim memorisation, knowledge memorisation, privacy leakage, utility, scalability, sequential unlearning), MMLU and MT-Bench for utility, and a relearning attack that fine-tunes an unlearned model on the forget-set test questions.

## How they did it

**Selection.** For every feature, average the *squared* activation over all tokens of the forget set and of the retain set, and take the ratio of the two. Keep features above a percentile cut of that ratio (95 for Biology, 90 for Cyber), rank the survivors by the forget-side average and take the top n (20 for the main WMDP runs, 30 for Cyber). The justification is a two-step argument: for a Gaussian decoder likelihood, the trace of the Fisher information block for a feature's decoder row equals a constant times the expectation of its squared activation, and under an additional assumption that the output distribution responds affinely in the feature over the range from zero to its observed value, the expected KL divergence from zero-ablation is the same squared-activation term times a per-feature constant. Because that constant cancels in the forget-over-retain ratio, the ratio is claimed to equal the *relative* zero-ablation influence across the two datasets - so a cheap statistic that needs no ablation is being used as a stand-in for an interventional one. **Intervention.** At inference, compute for each sequence the fraction of tokens on which at least one selected feature is positive, call that ρ(x), and compare it with a threshold set as the p-th percentile (95 for most runs, 90 for MUSE NEWS) of ρ computed on the retain set. Sequences above the threshold get every selected feature on every token set to a fixed large negative value −c (500 for the best configurations); sequences below it are untouched. A Neyman-Pearson argument gives the threshold rule optimality at a fixed false-positive rate on the retain set *if* the likelihood ratio between forget and retain sequences is a non-decreasing function of ρ, which the authors state as an assumption. **Comparisons.** Every gradient baseline (gradient ascent, NPO, SimNPO, GradDiff, SCRUB, SSD, RMU) and the earlier static-clamping SAE method is swept over its own hyperparameters (Tables 3-9), and each is reported at the configuration that minimises WMDP accuracy subject to holding at least 99% of MMLU, so the comparison is a point on each method's best frontier rather than a fixed setting. Ablations vary the clamp strength over eight values, the feature count over 10/20/30, p_dyn over 60-97 and p_ratio over 75-95; the classification statistic is checked against a raw-count alternative by total variation distance; latency is measured over 100 samples at three sequence lengths.

## What counts as an explanation here

Not a full mechanistic account, and the paper is explicitly partial. The explanation of "why the model can answer a bioweapons question" is a short list of SAE features selected by a differential statistic, which are then treated as mediators in a causal diagram from data through hidden states through features to output, severed by an intervention. What the reader can predict from it is narrow and testable: that clamping these features on inputs whose ρ crosses the threshold will drop the dependent benchmark while leaving unrelated benchmarks alone, and that the threshold's position moves the trade-off. The feature names themselves are not the explanation - they are used for the zero-shot variant, where Neuronpedia's descriptions are queried with the words "Biology" and "Cybersecurity" and the top 20 features are taken with no data at all.

## How they judged success

Forgetting: accuracy on permutation-stable WMDP test questions (only questions the target model gets right under all 24 orderings of the four options, following SAEBench, giving 522/1273 Bio and 275/1987 Cyber). Utility: MMLU accuracy on a filtered subset, MT-Bench fluency scored by GPT-4 and averaged over five runs. MUSE's six criteria with its own thresholds. Controls and ablations as above plus a static-versus-dynamic comparison at matched feature sets, a per-component ablation, a data-volume sweep from 20% to full data, a sequential-unlearning run over four disjoint NEWS folds with two aggregation strategies, a relearning attack at two learning rates, and an inference-latency measurement. A zero-shot condition with no forget or retain data at all stands in for a generalisation test. No confidence intervals are printed on the headline accuracy numbers comparable to the variance the MT-Bench captions give (0.16 and 0.13 across five runs).

## What they found

On WMDP-Bio, at configurations that hold MMLU at or above 99%, DSG reaches 29.64% WMDP accuracy against RMU's 50.00%, the static SAE method's 59.22% and 94.97-99.44% for the gradient baselines, with MMLU 99.34% (target 100.00% on the filtered subset) and the highest MT-Bench score in the table (7.78 against 7.36 for the target). On WMDP-Cyber the ordering is the same and the gap larger: 26.74% against RMU's 88.00% and 52.73% for static clamping. On MUSE the picture is mixed and the paper's own table shows it: DSG cuts knowledge memorisation by 98.5% on NEWS and 94.7% on BOOKS, but cuts verbatim memorisation only 44.2% and 44.7% (RMU gets 54.6% on NEWS, gradient ascent 97.1% and 83.5% on the two corpora), sits outside MUSE's acceptable band on privacy leakage on both corpora (12.08 and −23.18 against the −5% to 5% range) and loses 4.2% of retain-set knowledge on NEWS. The ablations are where the mechanism is priced: with the same feature set, static clamping holds WMDP down only at clamp strengths above 100 and loses MMLU, while the ratio-based selection (as opposed to the earlier method's sparsity-based one) is reported as 8% lower WMDP accuracy at matched clamp strength; 20 features is the stated optimum, with 10 sometimes under-forgetting and 30 beginning to cost retain-set accuracy at high clamp strength; clamp strength is flat over 100-500; and the percentile-based ρ separates forget from retain distributions far better than a raw token count (TVD 0.90 against 0.41 for module-versus-forget separation on Bio), while the raw count aligns the two retain corpora worse (0.88 against 0.38). Data volume can be cut to 20-80% with WMDP staying below 40% while RMU swings. Sequential unlearning over four folds stays in the ideal region for both aggregation strategies while gradient methods degrade. Against relearning at learning rate 1e-5, test-time DSG on top of RMU holds WMDP near random throughout; DSG on the base model erodes gradually; applying DSG during the attacker's fine-tuning (with the SAE frozen and clamped features' gradients blocked) holds near-random for about six epochs and then recovers; at 1e-6 nothing relearns. Zero-shot, with features picked purely from Neuronpedia descriptions and no data, it beats RMU and the static method. Latency overhead is about 5% over the base model (3.6% to 7.3%), almost all of it the SAE's own forward pass.

## Limits and open problems they name

One model family, 2B scale, one SAE site (layer 3) - the authors flag extension to larger models, other architectures and other SAE widths as future work. Unlearning is approximate: the method reduces the likelihood of generating forget-set content and does not claim removal, and the relearning experiments show the mechanism resting on activation geometry staying put under fine-tuning, which they attribute to the Superficial Alignment Hypothesis and demonstrate only as a cosine-similarity concentration in one figure, not as a tested invariant. The theory carries real weight: the Fisher-to-influence equivalence needs an affine-Gaussian response with a feature-constant sensitivity and approximate feature independence, and the "relative influence" claim inherits all of it - the paper itself notes that the per-feature constant can differ, so activation magnitude alone need not rank different features by influence. The Neyman-Pearson optimality needs a monotone likelihood-ratio assumption the authors call stronger than stochastic dominance, and they concede that with atoms an exact false-positive rate needs randomisation, and that the empirical retain-set percentile is an estimate whose population and transfer false-positive rates require evaluation. The dominance theorem for dynamic over static clamping requires a separation condition (a band of ρ values containing no forget-set inputs) that is assumed, not checked on their own data. Privacy leakage fails MUSE's band on both corpora, verbatim memorisation is far from complete, sequential unlearning uses four folds of one corpus, and the relearning result is scored on the same WMDP-Bio set the method was calibrated against.

## What we take from it

**A selector built from activation statistics is a hypothesis about intervention effect, and this paper is the folder's clearest case of that hypothesis being used without being tested against the thing it stands for.** The squared-activation ratio is presented as a proxy for a zero-ablation KL divergence, and the reasoning is correct as far as it goes, but it goes only as far as an affine-Gaussian response with a per-feature constant - and the paper's own appendix says the constant varies across features, which means the ranking the selector produces is a ranking of *activations scaled by unknown per-feature sensitivities*, not of effects. What this run takes is the discipline that follows: **when our white-box model uses a proxy statistic to choose which internal units to act on, we compute the thing it proxies for on a sample - here, an actual clamp-and-measure - and report the rank correlation between the proxy's ordering and the measured effect ordering, at the feature set size actually used.** Scored at n = 20 out of 16k, as this paper effectively does, the selector is probably fine and the correlation is cheap; the danger is extending the same statistic to name individual units, which is exactly what our project wants to do.

**A per-sequence gate is a second instrument layered on the first, and the paper's ladder shows the gate doing most of the work on the utility axis while the selection does the work on the forgetting axis.** Static clamping with DSG's own Fisher-based feature set and dynamic clamping with the earlier sparsity-based set are different rows, and the ablations separate them: the gate buys the MMLU retention (above 99% at clamp strengths where static has already broken the model) and the ratio-based selection buys an 8% WMDP reduction at matched clamp strength. Read this as a mechanical rule: **every intervention of ours that fires conditionally names which component (selector, gate, magnitude) is responsible for which axis of the result, and reports each component's contribution on the axis it is meant to move, with the other components held at their minimum - because here neither component alone reproduces the headline, and merging them into one row hides that the gate's optimal threshold is calibrated on 90-95% of a distribution the gate's own statistic defined.**

The warning worth carrying in the other direction: **the mechanism this method relies on - that a clamp at one site on a sequence classified as forget-relevant severs a causal pathway - is validated on benchmarks that share the calibration data.** The relearning results are scored on WMDP-Bio questions, the WMDP hyperparameters were chosen on WMDP-Bio questions, and the privacy-leakage criterion, which is the one MUSE criterion the method fails on both corpora, is the one whose inputs it was never tuned for. When our model under test gets a favourable score on a benchmark whose items were also used to pick its thresholds, the score is a point on a selected frontier and the honest control is a benchmark in the same task family that the selection never saw.
