## What they wanted, and why
The circuit hypothesis says a capability is implemented by a small subnetwork — an attention-head/MLP computation graph — and almost a decade of mechanistic work has produced candidate circuits for induction, indirect-object identification, docstring completion, greater-than and compiled RASP programs. The authors' observation is that this literature has almost no **tests**: a circuit is reported together with a faithfulness number and a picture, and nothing forces the claimant to say what would have counted as failure. They want to turn the hypothesis into a falsifiable statistical statement and then run it against the field's own published circuits. The larger goal is the one our survey shares — making internals claims scoreable — and their chosen route is the statistician's: name the ideal properties, derive null hypotheses whose truth is a property of a *random* subnetwork, and report rejection or retention with a stated confidence level rather than a single benchmark number.

## What they studied
Six published circuits, each on its own small model and task, chosen because automatic-circuit-discovery papers already use them as benchmarks. Two are **synthetic**: circuits obtained by compiling a RASP program with Tracr, so the "ground truth" subnetwork is known by construction (sequence reversal `Tracr-R`, and computing the proportion of `x` tokens seen so far, `Tracr-P`). Four are **discovered** by researchers: induction heads in a 2-layer 8-head attention-only Transformer trained on OpenWebText, the indirect-object-identification circuit in GPT-2 small, the greater-than circuit in GPT-2 small as simplified by ACDC, and the docstring circuit in a 4-layer attention-only Transformer trained on natural language and Python. The unit of analysis is the **edge set of the computational graph** — edges between attention heads and MLPs, with input-embedding and output-logit nodes — and the intervention is activation patching over the edges the circuit omits.

## How they did it
They first fix an idealisation: a circuit should be **equivalent** to the model (same task performance), **localised** (knocking it out should destroy the task), and **minimal** (no removable edge). Each becomes a null hypothesis whose reference population is drawn from the *same model*, not from a hand-built control.

*Equivalence* is a sign test: the circuit and the model should beat each other about equally often, and the null is that the proportion of datapoints where the circuit scores higher lies within a tolerance of one half; the test statistic is a binomial count of wins, so the p-value is analytic. *Independence* (strict localisation) is the claim that after knocking the circuit out, the complement's performance is statistically independent of the model's; they measure dependence with HSIC — a kernel cross-covariance, hence sensitive to non-linear relationships — under a permutation test. *Minimality* needs a reference for "how much does removing a relevant edge change the score", which they manufacture by **inflating** the circuit with one randomly sampled extra path and then knocking out a uniformly chosen edge from that added path; edges whose removal barely moves the score relative to that reference are declared unnecessary, with a Bonferroni correction across the edges tested.

Then they relax: *sufficiency* asks whether the circuit is more faithful than a random circuit of the same size, and *partial necessity* asks whether knocking it out hurts more than knocking out a size-matched random circuit — both binomial tail tests over sampled random circuits, with the **size of the reference circuit treated as a knob** that dials the test's stringency from "not merely random" up toward the idealised claim. Random circuits are built by random walks from input to output nodes to reach a target edge count; the faithfulness metric is the L2 norm of the score difference; ablations run through TransformerLens, and the authors release a `circuitry` wrapper package that evaluates hundreds of circuits in minutes on one A5000.

## What counts as an explanation here
**A verdict, not a mechanism.** The paper does not try to say how induction works; it tries to say whether a claimed subnetwork *is* the thing it is claimed to be, and the explanation's form is a grid of hypothesis-test outcomes (reject / retain, per circuit per test) plus a null hypothesis with a stated reference distribution. It is deliberately **partial and conditional**: passing the idealised tests is offered as strong evidence for the circuit hypothesis on that task, failing one is evidence against the specific idealised property, and the flexible tests are explicitly framed as "how far along the axis toward the idealisation is this circuit" rather than as pass/fail. What it predicts is narrow and checkable — that a circuit passing the sufficiency test should be more faithful than almost any random subnetwork of its size, and that an edge it calls unnecessary should be removable at negligible cost.

## How they judged success
The judging is the paper. Per test: an explicit null, a reference distribution the user can redefine, a binomial or permutation test statistic, a confidence level, and a reported rejection decision — with the direction of the desirable outcome stated (reject for equivalence, independence and minimality; retain for the idealised independence claim). Controls are the two Tracr circuits, which must pass by construction and serve as **construct-validity checks on the tests themselves**; the paper says their alignment with the idealised properties "supports the validity of these tests". Two ablation schemes are compared (zero ablation and symmetric token replacement), which turns out to be the experiment that produces the paper's sharpest negative result. The flexible tests are calibrated by varying reference-circuit size and quantile, and the paper additionally plots the probability that a random circuit *contains* the canonical circuit, so that "as faithful as the circuit" can be compared with "contains the circuit" — the probability the circuit hypothesis itself predicts.

## What they found
The synthetic circuits behave as they should, and **no discovered circuit passes the equivalence test**: the proportion of datapoints on which the candidate circuit beats the model is 0.03 for induction, 0.07 for greater-than, 0.10 for docstring and 0.24 for IOI, so every real circuit is markedly worse than the model at its own task. On independence (HSIC p-values 0.000 for greater-than, 0.01 for IOI and docstring, 0.669 for induction, 1.000 for both Tracr circuits) only **induction** joins the synthetic circuits in retaining the null, i.e. knocking it out plausibly removes the task-relevant information. On minimality, **induction and the Tracr pair** pass; docstring passes the idealised test as well, but under the per-edge scores fewer than half of docstring's edges are significantly different from the reference distribution, so the verdict is explained by the reference edges being approximately zero rather than by the circuit being tight. Graphically, edges removed in order of their minimality score can go a long way before faithfulness drops: about **50% of the greater-than circuit's edges** and about **20% of IOI's** can be removed at no cost, and for IOI faithfulness does not move monotonically as more edges go — the paper attributes the wobble to negative-mover heads.

Partial necessity is where the methodology bites back. Against a reference circuit drawn from the **complement**, the knockdown effect is significant for every task; against a size-matched random circuit drawn from the **model**, it is not always. And when the random circuits are made larger, knocking out the *complete model* turns out to have **less** impact than knocking out the candidate circuit for greater-than and IOI — a pattern present under STR patching and absent under zero ablation, where significance instead declines monotonically as the reference size grows. The authors read this as the knockdown metric being an artefact of the validation dataset under STR, and conclude that knockdown alone cannot grade circuit quality. Sufficiency separates circuits that looked alike: IOI and greater-than beat random circuits at a fraction of the model's size while docstring and induction need a larger reference, and although induction and docstring look similar on that curve they differ on the contains-the-circuit curve, which is why induction is judged the better-aligned of the two.

## Limits and open problems they name
The equivalence and independence tests desire *retention* of the null, and the authors state plainly that they did not study the **Type II error** of those tests, so a retained null is not evidence of equivalence — only an absence of evidence against it. The flexible tests are sensitive to how circuit size is measured and require the null to be designed for the question, which makes them easy to game in either direction. The empirical study reuses the original papers' experimental configurations, while other work (and their own ablation comparison) shows circuits are not robust to changes of setup, so every verdict is conditional on one configuration. The tests are per-circuit and per-task; nothing here gives a base rate for how often circuits in general satisfy the idealisation.

## What we take from it
A **framing, a warning and a cheap repair**, and it lands squarely on the folder's most-repeated weakness.

(1) **The reference distribution is the deliverable, and "random subnetwork of the same size" is the weakest rung of a ladder the paper makes explicit.** Sufficiency, as they define it, is only the claim that the circuit beats a random draw — which is very close to what the folder's ablation baselines already establish, and the paper shows that this rung is easy to clear while the rung that matters (equivalent to the model) is cleared by nobody. Any write-up of ours that says "ablation of X hurts the task, so X implements it" is sitting on the easy rung and should say so; the ladder gives the vocabulary for saying how far up the claim actually goes.

(2) **A retained null is a non-result and must be labelled as one.** The independence test's desirable direction is *failure to reject*, and the paper admits it never measured how often that false pass occurs. Almost every "no effect" column we could print — a steering direction with no measurable effect, a probe whose ablation leaves behaviour unchanged — has the same shape, and the honest report is "we did not detect an effect at this power", with the power stated. This is the statistical face of the unmatched-null deficit `arxiv-2606.25092` prices behaviourally, and it is the check `arxiv-2505.23556` skipped by using a hundred-times-larger random feature set as its control.

(3) **The STR-versus-zero-ablation split is the entry's most portable finding and it is not about circuits at all:** under one reasonable patching scheme, knocking out an entire model damages the task *less* than knocking out the candidate subnetwork, because the corruption distribution carries the answer. Two habits follow for our own patching work — run the intervention under at least two ablation schemes and treat a scheme-dependent ordering as evidence about the scheme rather than about the component, and include a size-matched random-subnetwork reference at *several* sizes rather than one, since the paper's whole partial-necessity table is a function of that size.

(4) **Minimality is a free pruning pass and the numbers here are a prior on how much of a circuit is decoration**: half of greater-than and a fifth of IOI can go before faithfulness moves, and the per-edge procedure that finds them needs only one inflated circuit per edge. The equivalent move on our side is that any subnetwork, feature set or head set we hand over should arrive with its removals already performed.

(5) **The construct-validity habit is the one I would copy wholesale**: before trusting a test, run it on cases whose answer is known by construction (here, compiled programs), and report that the known cases behave. It is the same asymmetry `arxiv-2606.25092` names about positive controls, arriving from the testing side instead of the ablation side.
