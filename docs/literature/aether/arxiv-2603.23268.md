- **Kind:** paper

## What they wanted, and why
Circuit discovery has an established toolkit for capabilities — greedy pruning, attribution patching, edge pruning — but the authors argue that safety behaviours have been handled with bespoke heuristics: metrics hand-designed per phenomenon, ranked lists of "safety neurons" or "safety heads", greedy searches over head subsets. Those produce disjoint components rather than a complete functional subgraph, and they are expensive and unstable as a result. The paper's ambition is to make safety attribution use the same machinery as capability attribution: one objective, one optimiser, no scenario-specific score. A second goal follows directly from the first — if the resulting subgraph really is the mechanism, then fine-tuning that subgraph alone should be enough to change the behaviour, which turns the reading into a control knob.

## What they studied
Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Qwen3-8B. The unit of analysis is a **subgraph over a multi-granularity unit set**: single weight scalars, generalised neurons (any `σ(xᵀW)` structure, so attention and MLP both qualify), attention heads, or whole layers. Two scenarios are studied. Backdoors, injected by LoRA instruction tuning on 1,000 samples at a 10% poisoning rate, in three flavours: a phrase trigger producing refusal, a character-level trigger producing harmful compliance, and a semantic poetry-style trigger producing mislabelling. And safety alignment, where the target is the aligned model's own refusal behaviour on harmful instructions.

## How they did it
Each unit gets a learnable logit `z`, a sigmoid mask `m = sigmoid(z)`, and a straight-through binarised mask `m̂ = 1[m > η]`, applied multiplicatively to the unit's weight (for scalars) or its output (for neurons, heads, layers). The forward pass uses the hard mask and the backward pass routes gradients to the continuous one, so there is no train/test gap from post-hoc thresholding. Discovery minimises KL between the full model's output distribution and the masked graph's, plus an L1 penalty on the masks, over 100 samples from the in-domain dataset, 100 epochs. For both scenarios the paper runs **dual masks**: a dense "residual" graph (clean / unsafe) constrained to reproduce the model's normal behaviour and to neutralise the triggered or harmful behaviour, and a sparse "circuit" graph constrained to carry only the target behaviour, with an explicit overlap penalty keeping the two disjoint. Validation is two-sided — keep only the circuit and measure the behaviour, or keep only the complement and measure it — so faithfulness and necessity are separate readings. Finally SaCirT freezes everything outside the discovered circuit and fine-tunes only inside it on safety or helpfulness data, compared against LoRA and full-parameter baselines.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **subgraph with a parameterisation**: a set of units, at a chosen granularity, plus the claim that this set is both sufficient and necessary for the behaviour. No edges are described and no algorithm is characterised — the object is a set, evaluated by ablation, and its honesty comes entirely from the two-sided test. Explicitly partial: the authors select head- or neuron-level granularity and skip weights and layers for interpretability reasons, and nothing in the paper describes what the circuit computes, only that removing or keeping it moves a rate.

## How they judged success
Backdoor side: attack success rate on clean and poisoned inputs, in-domain and out-of-domain, ROUGE against the expected continuation, and 5-shot accuracy on GSM8K, MMLU and HellaSwag for general ability. Alignment side: ASR on in-domain harmful queries and on AgentHarm, judged by Llama-Guard-3-8B, plus the same three utility benchmarks via LM-Eval. Granularity and the straight-through estimator are ablated in a table (neuron, head, head+neuron, and no-STE arms in both scenarios). SaCirT is compared against LoRA on parameter count, utility and harmlessness.

## What they found
The headline sparsity numbers are striking. On Llama-3.1-8B, ablating a 0.42%-sparse circuit from the refusal-backdoored model drops in-domain ASR from 100% to 0.0% and out-of-domain ASR to 0.4%, while GSM8K moves from 72.5 to 72.0, MMLU 68.8 to 67.2 and HellaSwag 56.6 to 57.0. The complementary circuit alone reaches 100% ASR on triggered inputs and about 0% on clean ones at 98.3% sparsity on Llama and 93.2% on Qwen. For alignment on Llama, removing a circuit of 3.03% of heads and 0.79% of neurons drives in-domain ASR from 0.8% to 96.9% and OOD from 7.9% to 81.5%, while Qwen's utility holds (MMLU 77.1 to 74.9). The intersection between the dense and sparse graphs keeps 91-99% sparsity across all backdoor types, which is what the paper calls structural orthogonality. The STE ablation is the most informative row: without it the clean model still reports 0.0% ASR but GSM8K collapses from 72.0 to 0.0 and MMLU from 67.2 to 25.9, i.e. the backdoor appears "removed" because the model is broken. Head-only granularity for the backdoor task reaches 6.6% ASR against 0.0% for neuron-level, and head-only for alignment reaches 60.2% against 96.9%. SaCirT tunes about 1-2% of parameters on the backdoor task and 5.8% (Llama) / 5.8% (Qwen, from the 94.2% untuned figure) on the alignment task, matching or slightly trailing LoRA on utility while keeping AdvBench harmlessness at 93.8-99.2% against the baseline's 89.8-97.6%.

## Limits and open problems they name
The impact statement is candid that isolating a safety circuit is dual-use: the same optimiser could be pointed at removing safeguards. Methodologically the authors do not claim completeness — the granularity ablation shows the same behaviour attributed to materially different sets at different granularities, which they read as robustness and which reads equally as non-identifiability. Mask training uses only 100 samples. Everything is at 8B on two model families. The optimiser's convergence behaviour without the straight-through estimator is admitted to collapse outright rather than degrade, which suggests the objective is fragile and the reported sparsities depend on it working.

## What we take from it
A **method** and, more importantly, a **warning about what sparsity means**. The method: replacing heuristic ranking with a masked-graph objective is cheap (about 30 minutes on one A100 per the appendix) and gives a subgraph that can be tested two-sided; the dual-mask trick — constrain the complement to reproduce normal behaviour while the sparse graph carries the target behaviour — is a clean way to ask for a partition rather than a ranking, and it is the closest thing in this folder to a composition test. The warning is the one that should govern any masked-graph result we ever produce: **a mask optimiser reports success and failure in the same currency (a rate going to zero), and only a utility benchmark beside it distinguishes "we removed the behaviour" from "we removed the model."** The no-STE arm is the textbook instance — 0.0% ASR with GSM8K at 0.0% — and it exists because the authors could see a utility column. Second, the granularity table is the strongest evidence in this folder that a circuit's contents are a function of the unit alphabet: the same backdoor is 0.42% of neurons or 3.61% of heads, with different residual ASR, and the paper calls both correct. That is the same lesson as the threshold sweep in the modular-architecture entry, one level down. Mechanical rule for us: any masked or optimised subgraph our white-box model emits ships the complement arm, a utility benchmark on both arms, and the same discovery run repeated at two unit granularities — because a set that changes identity with the alphabet, and a success that is indistinguishable from collapse, are the two failure modes a rate-only readout cannot see.
