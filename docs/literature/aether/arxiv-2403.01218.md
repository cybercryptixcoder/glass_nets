# Inexact Unlearning Needs More Careful Evaluations to Avoid a False Sense of Privacy

- **Key:** arxiv-2403.01218
- **Authors / organisation:** Jamie Hayes, Ilia Shumailov, Eleni Triantafillou, Amr Khalifa, Nicolas Papernot; Google DeepMind
- **Date:** 2024-03-02 (v1), 2024-05-15 (v2)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.01218
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Inexact unlearning methods are proposed without formal removal guarantees, so their quality is usually argued from an experiment in which an adversary tries to tell whether a point was unlearned or was never seen. The authors suspect that this experiment has been run with adversaries that are far from optimal, and that the resulting pass rates say more about the attacker than about the model. Their aim is to build a stronger attacker, show that the field has been grading itself against a weak one, and characterise which examples survive the stronger test. The larger goal is an evaluation protocol that a privacy claim can stand on - the same problem the folder keeps meeting, in the removal-evaluation rather than the interpretability register.

## What they studied
Models: 256 ResNet-18 classifiers trained with SGD on random halves of the CIFAR-10 training set (25,000 examples each, over 80 epochs, learning rate annealed every 30, no data augmentation, about 73% test accuracy); and PaLM 2 S (Bison) fine-tuned on LIMA (1,000 instruction pairs) plus 300 hand-built examples in three subsets of 100 - random alphanumeric strings, fabricated PII, and false/poison answers - each confirmed memorised by greedy decoding before unlearning. Unit of analysis is the **example**: the object is a per-example membership score, not a neuron, direction or circuit. Ten thousand two hundred and forty unlearned ResNet models (256 base models times 40 random forget selections), forget set fixed at 200 randomly chosen examples of class 5 (with class 1 and class 9 as robustness checks). Arms: CF-k, EU-k, NegGrad, GradDesc, NegGrad+, SPARSITY (l-1 penalty), SCRUB; on the language side only NegGrad and GradDesc (10 epochs, SGD with momentum 0.9, batch 10, learning rates 1e-6 and 0.003 respectively), with SCRUB deliberately not adapted to generation.

## How they did it
They frame unlearning as a two-world hypothesis test: world one is a model that trained on the point and then unlearned it, world zero is a model that never saw it. They classify the previous literature's tests into *population* U-MIAs - one attacker trained across all examples, by shadow models, logistic regression on losses or probabilities, entropy thresholds, or a confidence threshold tuned on half the forget set - and *per-example* U-MIAs, where a dedicated decision rule is fitted for each example. Their contribution is U-LiRA: for every example they sample shadow datasets containing it, train a model, unlearn it, and separately retrain without it, giving two sets of rescaled logit likelihoods (one for the unlearned-model world, one for the never-seen world); a Gaussian is fitted to each, and the target example's own logit is scored by the ratio of the two densities. On vision this meant 5,120 shadow and 5,120 target models, split so that each of the 5,000 class-5 examples has 128 shadow models in each of the retain, forget and test conditions. For each example they compute a predicted membership probability before and after unlearning over 100 target models containing it, take the difference, and plot the empirical CDF over all 5,000. On PaLM 2 S, 20 fine-tune iterations with different data orderings give the in and out shadow sets, unlearning three subsets with two methods for 2,400 models total. Finally, they build a U-LiRA-aware variant of GradDesc that drops an example from the mini-batch once its log probability exceeds the shadow-out average, to test whether the attack can be defended by construction.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not an explanation of model internals - nothing inside the network is inspected. What the paper produces is an **attack as an instrument**, plus a taxonomy: a per-example likelihood-ratio test whose two fitted distributions are the operational definition of "the unlearned model and the retrained model are distinguishable". Understanding, in their framing, is knowing what the strongest usable test says about a given checkpoint, and the intended form is a formal adversary definition plus threat model so that a pass means something. It is explicitly partial: they show in their own appendix that a parametric test can fail, so the instrument is not claimed optimal either.

## How they judged success
Attack accuracy is the balanced accuracy of the binary decision rule over the forget set and an equally sized held-out set drawn from the same distribution (class 5 samples outside training). The scale is calibrated by two anchors: with no unlearning at all, both attacks exceed 85%, which is the ceiling a working removal should pull down to chance; and a perfect unlearned model would leave every example at a predicted membership probability of one half. The reference attack is the strongest of the population U-MIAs they implemented (Kurmanji et al.'s, which trains a logistic regression directly on losses of half the forget and test sets). Controls: class 1 and class 9 replications, a three-way forget/retain/test hypothesis test, and the U-LiRA-aware defence. Unlearning hyperparameters were grid-searched per method over epoch, learning-rate and batch-size grids, and failed runs were filtered out by each method's own success criterion.

## What they found
Without unlearning, both adversaries sit above 85%. With unlearning, the baseline falls below 60% for SCRUB and SPARSITY - the two strongest methods - while U-LiRA stays over 70% (abstract), i.e. the advantage over chance is two to three times larger under the per-example attack across all methods; ordering between methods is largely preserved, so a cheap attack ranks but does not measure. On the forget set, most examples do lose membership risk after unlearning, but the CDF never reaches all examples before crossing zero, so every method leaves a subset whose risk *rises*; CF-k barely moves most samples at the k they chose. On the retain set the picture is worse: NegGrad+ makes the attack better on nearly 70% of retained examples after unlearning, and several methods increase leakage for a substantial fraction of the retain set. On PaLM 2 S, U-LiRA stays at or above 80% except for the random-string forget set; both NegGrad and GradDesc fail to unlearn any subset by this measure. Random strings are easiest to learn and unlearn, and are unlearned most cleanly around epoch 2, after which the attack returns to near 100% - over-unlearning makes examples identifiable. The U-LiRA-aware defence ends at over 75% attack accuracy, which the authors call an improvement but not a solution. Their Gaussian-fit caveat is concrete: on a deliberately bimodal toy shadow distribution U-LiRA's Gaussian test scores about 58% where a kernel-density likelihood ratio reaches nearly 80%, and on a skewed real CIFAR-10 example the same swap moves 50.1% to 55.6%.

## Limits and open problems they name
Their own per-example attack is not optimal - the parametric Gaussian assumption can be defeated by reshaping the shadow distribution, and the toy example shows an arbitrary model can be re-represented to exploit it. The vision setting is fixed to one class, one forget-set size and a handful of methods, with GAN/naive attempts at per-example stopping criteria failing; the LLM side uses only two gradient-based methods and only a fine-tuned memorisation setting. They hypothesise that no single hyperparameter setting can terminate unlearning correctly for every example, and argue the field needs algorithmic guarantees and stated threat models rather than empirical attack scores. They stop short of producing such a guarantee.

## What we take from it
**Measure, by name:** balanced attack accuracy anchored at both ends - the no-edit ceiling and the chance floor - rather than a single number, because the same method moves from 59% to 72% between two attackers on identical weights and only the pair locates it. **Framing:** the "privacy onion" pattern, now shown for editing rather than training - protecting a targeted subset can make other examples more identifiable, so an edit has to be scored on units it did not touch as well as on the ones it did, and NegGrad+'s 70% retain-set degradation is the clean statement of that. **Warning:** a population-level average over examples is a weaker instrument than a per-example one, and our own model's predictions are about units, so the analogue of the per-example attack is per-unit scoring rather than a corpus-average probe accuracy; their three-way test (forget / retain / never-seen) is the template for a per-unit test with a matched-but-untreated control. **Warning, sharper:** their bimodal appendix is the strongest cautionary instance in the folder so far for parametric tests - an instrument whose distributional assumption the object can violate will under-report the effect, so any likelihood-ratio or Gaussian-fitted score we use needs a non-parametric side check. **Shortcut:** quarantining the difficulty in the instrument is legitimate - they do not fix unlearning, they show the grading is broken - which is the role our audit layer plays relative to the predictive model.
