# ConwayNet: a fixed Game-of-Life-style lateral inhibition wired into ordinary networks as a parameter-free regulator

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.10760
- **Authors / organisation:** Yair Cohen, Scott M. Gilmore (both listed with LinkedIn affiliation; a personal research address for the first author)
- **Date:** 2026-11-07
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.10760
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (HTML v1, main text plus Appendices A–N read; figures inspected via captions only, so every number quoted is a table cell or an explicit sentence)
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Interpretability is hard in ordinary networks because every scale of the computation is coupled to every other, and the authors want a mechanism that separates scales inside the representation rather than explaining it after the fact. Their foil is the cellular automaton: a single fixed local rule whose products can be read directly off the grid, programmed by hand into memory and signals. They do not propose replacing the network with an automaton — they keep the learned non-local maps and add to each layer a fixed, state-dependent, neighbour-reading rule that regulates the layer's output. The larger goal is a network whose activations have a coarse component that persists and a fine component that is renewed, so that some part of the representation is stable enough to be described without tracking every unit. They also claim several side benefits they then measure: input attribution that lies on the object, agreement across seeds, and robustness to noise.

## What they studied
Four small backbones, all with tanh activations: a 10-hidden-layer MLP on 28×28 grayscale images with each hidden map reshaped back to the grid (6.18M parameters on MNIST); a two-stage CNN (111k parameters on MNIST); a WRN-40-2 on CIFAR-10 (2.33M); and a 2-layer graph convolutional network on the pixel graph of MNIST (126k). Data: the MNIST family grayscale and colour, and CIFAR-10, with Gaussian, shot and impulse corruptions on a nine-level severity ladder. The unit of analysis is the layer's activation map treated as a field — space within a layer, depth as time, and independently trained seeds as an ensemble — with hidden units, classifier weight maps, saliency maps and the co-activation graph as the objects read.

## How they did it
Each layer computes its ordinary output z (affine, normalisation, tanh, hence bounded), and the rule reads the layer *input* x. Both are mapped to occupancies in [0,1]; a 3×3 kernel gives each unit the mean occupancy m of its eight Moore neighbours; two smooth logistic bumps of slope 15 turn m into a Birth gate (high near 5/8, the B5 set-point) and a Survival gate (high near 3/8, S3); the layer output blends the two gates weighted by the unit's own state, so an "off" unit takes Birth and an "on" unit takes Survival, then a normaliser. Nothing in the rule is learned; the same wiring is used on all four backbones, with resampling when the gate grid and output grid differ. The first comparison is against the identical network without the rule (plain), throughout. They then run a sweep of ten of the 2^18 outer-totalistic Life-like rules at ten layers and five seeds, select B5/S3, and from there build four measurement families: low-frequency power ratio (LFPR) of maps as a smoothness score; a logit lens that applies the trained final classifier to every hidden layer; a band-pass correlation depth per spatial-frequency band, combined into a "scale-separation index" (SSI) as the ratio of coarse to fine correlation depth; and a percolation analysis in which units are linked by positive activation correlation over 1,000 test images, strongest links added first, and the growth of the largest connected group is plotted against mean degree, with a 2D random geometric graph and an Erdős–Rényi graph as references. A separate control family replaces the birth–survival rule with rule-free alternatives that read the same input: a linear blend toward the neighbour mean of the input ("input blur", weight λ), the same blend computed from the output ("output blur"), a local average, a random-neighbour version (rule kept, grid destroyed), and a published difference-of-Gaussians surround filter. Two causal probes: noising object pixels versus equally many background pixels to see which the network relies on, and a gate-route cut in which the neighbourhood occupancy is computed from a detached copy of the layer input so no gradient flows through the rule while the forward pass is unchanged. Finally they repeat the "churn" protocol of Ly and Gong, training paired networks from initialisations differing by ε from 1e-1 down to 1e-8 and plotting the fraction of test images on which the two disagree.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not a description of individual units and not a dictionary — a *structural* explanation by imposed constraint. The claim is that the regulated network acquires a separation of scales: a coarse spatial component that survives many layers, a fine component that decorrelates within one, agreement across seeds at the coarse scales only, and saliency that tracks the object. The authors are explicit that this is a partial account ("stops short of producing the analogue of thermodynamic laws"): the analogue of thermodynamic laws — descriptions of the network at the large scale without tracking its units — is stated as the unattained goal. It is also explicitly a claim about a design, not a measurement of a given network: the paper's own controls show most of the coherence is a property of *reading the input*, and only a few properties are specific to the CA rule.

## How they judged success
LFPR at cutoff ρ = 0.25 averaged over classes for final hidden maps and classifier weights; compressed-size entropy of hidden maps relative to a random map; logit-lens accuracy at the first and last hidden layer; per-band correlation depth and the SSI (with censoring flagged when a band stays correlated at the largest lag); the percolation threshold ⟨k⟩_c at which half the units join one component, plus the largest single jump, the share of strongly correlated pairs within two pixels, isolate fraction and modularity; unit-wise correlation between seeds, split into trained versus untrained-init pairs to separate learned agreement from inherited agreement, plus the accuracy of a weight average of seeds; the riddling churn and its exponent γ_f from the Ly–Gong protocol; saliency log-ratio of mean true-class gradient magnitude on versus off an object mask (mask dilated by one pixel), corroborated by the object-versus-background noising test and by a finite-difference check of the surrogate gradient; accuracy under corruption at every severity with Δ over severities 1–5 and 1–9, five seeds, and 95% intervals that resample seed pairs and test images; and a matched-clean-accuracy variant of the MLP so the robustness comparison is not an accuracy artefact.

## What they found
**Everything is smooth, and that is the least specific result.** MLP activation-map LFPR rises from 0.09 ± 0.01 to 0.96 ± 0.02 and classifier-weight LFPR from 0.10 to 0.70; against ten rules, most reach activation LFPR 0.54–0.95, so smoothness is generic to input regulation. The authors themselves note Day & Night matches B5/S3 on both summary scores (0.95 vs 0.94 smooth, 0.37 vs 0.33 simple) while its trained maps saturate into a blob that fills the field rather than preserving the digit — a case where the scores agree and the pictures do not.
**Depth and ensemble coherence.** Final classifier read at the first hidden layer: 57.5 ± 4.0% with the rule, rising to 96.5 ± 0.3% at layer 10, against 9.6% plain. Coarsest band still correlated 0.92 ± 0.01 after nine layers while the finest decorrelates within one (C = −0.17 at lag 1); plain decorrelates at every band and lag (|C| < 0.08). SSI 18.2 ± 0.1 against 1.1 ± 0.1, but the ten-layer horizon censors it (ceiling 19); a 100-layer network measured over the same horizon gives 8.6, because its fine bands change more slowly (τ_fine 1.03 vs 0.50), and an earlier version's value of ≈29 used a 40-layer horizon and is not comparable. Cross-seed unit-wise correlation 0.96 at layer 1 and 0.82 at the last, against ≈0 for plain, while plain's *linear CKA* is still 0.89–0.97 — the same information in a different basis. Untrained seeds already agree at 0.95; trained agreement is 0.92 (active maps) and data-alignment falls from 0.73 untrained to 0.50 trained, so part of the coherence is inherited from the layout and the paper says so. A weight average of five plain MLPs lands at chance (10.1%) while the regulated average reaches 88.8%.
**Co-activation graph.** Half the units join the giant component at ⟨k⟩_c 4.2–6.0 against 1.7–2.1 plain; the largest single jump is 0.29–0.36 of units against 0.16–0.19; each unit has ~28 partners correlated above 0.5 against ~3; 68–70% of strongly correlated pairs are within two pixels against 2%. But the plain network's high isolate fraction (0.69) is mostly dead units: restricted to active units it falls to 0.24, equal to the regulated network's, and a rule-free local average of the output reproduces connectivity (isolates 0.25) and is more modular (0.88 vs 0.82). The authors also retract part of the plain network's apparent randomness: with inactive units removed its clustering is about six times a random graph and its active units have effective rank ≈11.
**Attribution.** On the CNN, saliency per pixel sits 4–14× higher on the object than off it; the log-ratio is +0.64 to +1.13 on grayscale and +0.58 to +0.98 on colour, with 83–100% of images favouring the object, against −0.13 to +0.47 plain, and plain favours the background on EMNIST in 94% of images. The causal test: noising as many object pixels as the digit has drops the regulated CNN to 60.5% while noising as many background pixels leaves it at 96.3%; plain is the reverse (74.4 / 67.0). The MLP does *not* pass: with the gate route detached its input gradient vanishes (4e-13 vs 4e-2), so its saliency is entirely the rule's own sensitivity, and noising the background costs it more (79.8%) than noising object pixels (85.3%).
**Noise.** Regulated CNN gains double digits under noise on every MNIST-family dataset and recovers roughly half to two-thirds of the accuracy noise costs, at a clean cost of about a point; WRN gains single digits; GCN gains double digits under Gaussian and impulse noise but *loses* under shot noise; at matched clean accuracy the shallow MLP wins on MNIST, FashionMNIST and KMNIST and is indistinguishable on EMNIST. Training trajectory matters more than the arm: the plain MLP's noise drop shrinks from about −13 to −4.5 points as it trains while the regulated one's grows (KMNIST −2.1 → −6.2), so the curves cross and a matched-accuracy comparison pits early plain against late regulated.
**Reproducibility.** With half the labels wrong, 25/25 plain pairs churn 2.3–3.1% of test images while 25/25 regulated pairs churn 0.03–0.14% (one-sided rank test p < 1e-14), at 94.7% vs 91.7% test accuracy; with clean labels the churn advantage persists several-fold while accuracy is slightly lower (98.4 vs 99.0).
**Controls that decide attribution.** At λ = 0.9 the input blur *exceeds* the CA rule on accuracy (0.981 vs 0.962), LFPR (0.967 vs 0.941), lens@1 (75.9 vs 57.5) and SSI (18.8 vs 18.2, both at the ceiling); the output blur smooths final maps (LFPR up to 0.94) with no depth consistency (lens@1 7–18) and no scale separation (SSI ≈ 1); shuffling the neighbourhood did not train (0.112 validation) and a random neighbourhood collapses (0.301). So smoothness, depth consistency and scale separation come from regulating the output by the input; only three properties survive as rule-specific — the CNN's object-localised saliency (+1.13 against +0.37 to +0.48 for the input blurs, +0.47 plain, +0.04 for the DoG filter which nonetheless recovers most of the noise gain at 86.7%), *learned* (as opposed to inherited) cross-seed agreement (0.11 untrained → 0.83 trained, whereas a blur strong enough to agree after training, 0.97, already agrees at 0.74 untrained), and an input-dependent gain S(m) − B(m) that is 8.5× larger on strokes than off them (0.262 vs 0.031) where the blur scales every unit by the same 1 − λ.
**A reported-number correction.** The SSI in an earlier version is explicitly disowned; a half-bin offset in the frequency masks is corrected and moves the value by at most 0.04.

## Limits and open problems they name
Training is harder: longer schedules, up to a few points of clean accuracy, and not every rule trains. Wiring is bespoke per architecture with no recipe. The rule is sensitive to input normalisation — other choices, colour backgrounds or inverted contrast can switch the effect off or reverse it. Benefits track the backbone: largest on shallow networks whose rule reads the image, smallest on the WRN, and some MLP coherence does not carry over to convolutional backbones, whose per-channel ordering differs across seeds so weight averaging fails. The form most of the coherence takes is not resolved by the ten-layer horizon (the coarse band is censored there). At vanishing contrast the layer is blind — a constant grey image gives zero gates, every first-layer unit at −1 and every image assigned to the same class with probability 1.00 — and the input gradient grows as 1/a, which the noise experiments never probe. And the separations of scales they do show are not the thermodynamic laws they set out to find.

## What we take from it
Three reusable instruments, one framing, two warnings.

*Instruments.* (1) **Reading a stack as a field with depth as time**, and scoring it per spatial-frequency band rather than as one number: the band-wise correlation depth turns "the representation is hierarchical" into a measurement, and it is the only diagnostic here that separated the winning rule from a wide field of rivals. The censoring rule matters — a coarse band still correlated at the largest available lag is a lower bound, and a horizon-capped index should be reported as such, as this paper does when it disowns the earlier value. (2) **Train/uninitialised decomposition of cross-seed agreement.** Correlating two trained networks is nearly meaningless without the same-seed-pair correlation at initialisation, because a constraint that imposes structure at init will show agreement that looks learned; here the two arms read 0.11→0.83 for the rule and 0.74→0.97 for its strongest rival. This is a cheap, one-line column that generalises to any "our intervention makes the model reproducible" claim, and it is the same discipline as the matched-random arm this folder keeps demanding. (3) **The escaped-gradient route as a measurable quantity.** Recomputing each weight gradient twice, once with the rule's path detached, gives the rule's own share of every layer's learning signal — below 0.2% in MLP layers 1–7, about 14% in layer 9, 0.02–4% in the WRN after training against 30–86% at initialisation. That is a way to ask what fraction of *training* goes through a chosen mechanism, which is a different question from the usual inference-time ablation and, to this folder, a new column.

*Framing.* A fixed, non-learnable constraint that cannot be optimised away is a clean experimental object: it converts "does the model use this structure" into "what does the model do when this structure is imposed", and the answer here is a scale separation plus a churn reduction of roughly thirtyfold under label noise. For a predictive white-box model, the more useful half is the counterfactual form — if the coarse component is genuinely a low-dimensional shared mean field (depth-average matches ensemble-average at r = 0.91 against 0.90 for two halves of the ensemble), then a coarse-only description should predict a subset of the network's behaviour, which is a testable claim we could run on ordinary networks without imposing anything, by asking how much of the output variance a coarse-band-only surrogate carries.

*Warnings.* The first is the strength of the paper's own controls and the weakness of the headline: four of the six things the abstract lists are reproduced, and in places exceeded, by a linear blur at λ = 0.9, which the authors report rather than bury. The lesson is a selection rule for us — when we attribute an effect to a mechanism, run the *same wiring with the mechanism replaced by a linear operator of equal read range*, because that arm answers the only question anyone cares about, and here it answers "not the rule" for most effects. The second is about saliency read as reliance: the MLP's maps look object-shaped and are pure rule sensitivity, with reliance pointing the other way (background 79.8% vs object 85.3%); the two are separated by one gradient-free test, noising matched pixel counts on versus off the mask, which costs nothing and should be a standing column in any attribution result we report. Relatedly, a surrogate gradient through fixed statistics changed the map magnitudes by 48–75% but its ranking barely (Spearman 0.95–0.97) and its localisation not at all — a useful calibration for how far a surrogate is trustworthy for *ordering* claims versus *size* claims.

The access conditions are worth recording: the code and figure-generating scripts are public under a noncommercial licence, and the method is subject to a pending patent application, so a method is available to study and to cite but not to build on commercially.
