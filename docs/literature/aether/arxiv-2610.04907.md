# Why Subliminal Learning Needs So Much Data: A Noisy Inverse View through Steering Vector Recovery

- **Key:** arxiv-2610.04907
- **Authors / organisation:** Luoyu Chen, Xiaoyu Ding, Weiqi Wang, Chenhan Zhang, Zhiyi Tian, Jianhuan Huang & Shui Yu; University of Technology Sydney, Xi'an Jiaotong University, Southeast University
- **Date:** 2026-10-04
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.04907
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

Subliminal learning — a student inheriting a teacher's behavioural trait from data that never mentions the trait — is usually shown with tens of thousands of carrier examples, and the field has treated that scale as the cost of the phenomenon. The authors ask where the data requirement actually comes from, and split the obvious answer into two competing hypotheses: either a small carrier set simply does not contain enough information about the trait (a coverage problem), or the trait is identifiable from far less data and the sampled one-hot targets are injecting noise that grows more harmful the longer you optimise (a noise problem). Their goal is to decide between these with a setting where the answer is readable rather than inferred: a "subliminal steering" distillation in which the teacher's trait is a single known residual-stream vector, so transfer can be scored as literal parameter recovery. The larger point is a warning about the folder's own instruments — if the thing being recovered is a direction, then the optimisation that recovers it is a regularised inverse problem, and the bias and variance of that inversion are the analysis.

## What they studied

Two instruction-tuned models, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (28 layers) and Gemma-2-9B-IT (42 layers), both of hidden width 3,584, with every pretrained parameter frozen. The unit of analysis is one vector: a teacher intervention ΔT added identically to the residual stream at every token position across a middle band of layers (layers 2 up to 26 on Qwen, 2 up to 40 on Gemma), and a student vector ΔS fitted in the same band. Five animal traits (cat, dog, eagle, lion, raven), each teacher built by minimising completion-only negative log-likelihood over 50 trait-eliciting question-answer pairs under a hard L2 bound (0.5 on Qwen, 1.5 on Gemma) so that trait acquisition stays in the local, approximately linear regime. The carriers are generated number sequences with no trait vocabulary.

## How they did it

The teacher is trained, then used to generate completions; each completion position becomes a training item consisting of a fixed prefix, a prefix token list and a sampled target token. The student is a second copy of the same base model with a zero-initialised intervention vector in the same layers, optimised on those items only. Two supervision variants are compared on *matched* prefixes: token-level (hard) negative log-likelihood on the sampled token, and full-distribution (soft) KL to the teacher's next-token distribution. Recovery is measured as the cosine between the student vector and the teacher vector, plus a norm-matched trait log-likelihood — every recovered vector is rescaled to the teacher's norm before its trait expression is scored, so direction and magnitude are not confounded.

Three analyses follow. First, a *local* comparison: the full-data mean gradient under each objective is computed at the same student state (the zero vector) and compared both to each other and to ΔT. Second, a derivation: the gradient of the soft objective at zero is shown to equal the carrier Fisher information matrix acting on ΔT, so the local descent direction is the trait direction distorted by the carrier task's curvature; the hard-label gradient is that same signal plus a zero-mean one-hot sampling error whose covariance is the Fisher matrix divided by the number of supervised tokens. Third, a correction experiment: the Fisher matrix is estimated by a Rademacher estimator on 30,000 clean carrier contexts with four resampled targets each, and the initial gradient is un-damped by a fractional inverse power of the damped Fisher; the (power, damping) pair is chosen per trait *with access to the true teacher vector*, which the authors say explicitly makes this a test of the predicted distortion rather than a teacher-free estimator.

The iterative arm follows the spectral dynamics the derivation predicts. Under a fixed-Fisher linear model, gradient descent from zero gives each Fisher eigendirection a recovery factor that rises with the eigenvalue, so a noiseless (soft) run restores steep components first and flat ones later, while hard-label runs apply the same inverse to a fixed error term and therefore amplify sampled-label noise in exactly the flat directions that need the most steps. Trajectories are projected into 64 equal-teacher-energy Fisher bins (500 soft Qwen sequences, 10,000 logged iterates; then hard runs with 500 to 16,000 independent carriers) and the per-bin normalised error is tracked against step count. A final experiment tests whether repeatedly re-sampling completions from a fixed pool of 500 prompts is as good as adding new prompts at a matched completion budget, and a spectral-location comparison across the two models relates where a trait's energy sits in the curvature spectrum to how hard it is to recover.

## What counts as an explanation here

A surrogate-plus-dynamics explanation, in closed form: an identified forward operator (the carrier Fisher matrix), a stated observation model for the learning gradient, and a parameterised trajectory with one scalar per eigendirection. It is not a description of features or circuits, and it is not meant to be complete — the paper says its linearisation must break down as the model Jacobian and curvature drift along the path. But it does what the folder keeps asking for: it *predicts* a quantity before it is measured (the single-step alignment ceiling, the ordering of recovery across curvature bins, the location of the optimal stopping depth as a function of dataset size, and the equivalence of resampling and new prompts), and four predictions are then checked on held-out traits and on a second model family.

## How they judged success

Main measures are cosine alignment between recovered and teacher vectors (single-step, and per Fisher bin at every logged iterate), the norm-matched target log-likelihood, and a normalised spectral error — a mean over the 64 bins of per-bin squared error divided by per-bin teacher energy. Controls and checks: hard and soft supervision on the same prefixes at the same student state; the Fisher estimated only on clean unsteered carriers so the teacher's own signal does not contaminate the curvature; damping added so that poorly estimated flat directions are suppressed; a fixed-Fisher model trajectory compared to observed recovery bin by bin (reported as a cosine between the predicted and the observed trajectory, and as a mild overestimation of the speed of the flattest bins); and a data-scale sweep in which the step of minimum error is read off per dataset size. There is no human rating and no benchmark: success is agreement between predicted curves and fitted curves.

## What they found

Hard and soft gradients at the initial state are nearly collinear and both align poorly with the teacher vector, so the local signal looks identical-ish and equally weak for the two objectives. Iteratively they diverge: soft KL recovers the teacher direction almost exactly from a few hundred carriers, while hard-label recovery plateaus well below even with tens of thousands of carriers. Damped Fisher inversion of the single-step hard gradient improves alignment substantially across all five traits, which the authors read as confirming that the low first-step alignment is a curvature transformation, not weakness or noise; the corrected estimate still lands below iterative recovery.

In the spectral test the fixed-Fisher model holds on ordering and most of the trajectory: at step 10,000 the flattest coarse bin is at 0.77 and the steepest at 0.99, and the observed vector has cosine 0.969 with the predicted trajectory. For hard supervision the optimal stopping depth grows monotonically with independent carriers — the min-error update goes 100, 190, 330, 560 and 880 as the number of carrier sequences doubles through 500, 1,000, 2,000, 4,000, 8,000 — and at 500 carriers the error bottoms out at 100 updates and then climbs to 2.756 by step 1,000 while the soft reference keeps improving. At a matched completion budget, re-sampling more completions from a fixed pool of 500 prompts is as effective as adding independent prompts. Gemma's teacher-vector energy sits at lower normalised curvature for all five traits than Qwen's, which the paper offers as the reason Gemma recovers worse: more of its trait is carried by slow, noise-sensitive eigendirections.

## Limits and open problems they name

The analysis is local: it linearises around the unsteered state and treats the Fisher matrix as constant along the trajectory, and the residual mismatch — notably the over-predicted speed of flat bins — is attributed to exactly that drift. Only one intervention family is studied: a single bounded vector shared across a layer band, which is what makes a 3,584-wide spectral analysis tractable in the first place. System-prompt-induced traits, and students trained by LoRA or full fine-tuning, may involve larger, higher-dimensional and more nonlinear changes, and whether the same noise-dominated inversion explains their data needs is left open. The paper also concedes that models of the finite-data covariance ignore within-sequence correlation between supervised tokens. No teacher-free version of the correction is offered — the hyperparameters are chosen against the known teacher — and no claim is made about how the recovered vector's *behaviour* diverges from the teacher's beyond the norm-matched first-token likelihood.

## What we take from it

A framing plus two measures. The framing: when our white-box model *fits* a direction to a network — probe weights, difference of means, a learned read-out — the carrier statistic is not contributing the direction, it is contributing a preconditioner, and the recovered direction is the target filtered through the Fisher geometry of the data we fitted on. That is a concrete alternative account of the folder's recurring "recovered directions disagree across fits, seeds and methods" results: disagreement can be curvature, measured and emendable, rather than randomness. The measures: (1) the Fisher-transformed alignment ceiling, computed as the cosine between the carrier-Fisher times the target and the target itself — cheap, and reportable beside any single-step recovery number, since if that ceiling is far below 1 the fit cannot in one step do better; and (2) the per-eigendirection recovery curve, which converts "this direction was recovered at cosine 0.8" into "this direction's steep components were recovered and its flat ones were not", i.e. a statement about which part of the target is missing. Also a warning that lands on our evaluation practice: more independent inputs and more optimisation steps are not interchangeable, and the optimum number of steps is not a property of the method but of the dataset size, so a fit that is early-stopped at a fixed step count has an unstated, data-dependent bias.
