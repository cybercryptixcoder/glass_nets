## What they wanted, and why
Natural-language verbalizers — activation oracles, NLAs, adapter-based inverters — are the read-out form this folder keeps returning to, and they have a known defect on both ends: the description can be generic enough to lose what the state specifically held, or fluent enough to assert a detail the state never carried. The authors point at the reason a question-conditioned verbalizer is hard to audit: readout and answering happen in one step, so when an answer is wrong nobody can say whether the information was missing from the activation or lost while answering. Their proposal is structural rather than a better prompt — make the intermediate text explicit and question-agnostic, so a reader can inspect what was recovered *before* anything is asked of it, and train the inverter to be faithful to that text rather than to a downstream answer. The goal is an activation read-out whose failures are attributable, which is the precondition for using one as evidence in our own write-ups.

## What they studied
Two donor models: Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct and Mistral-Small-24B-Instruct-2501, with a single last-token hidden state at a chosen layer as the object being verbalized. Inverters span four decoder sizes — Qwen3-0.6B, Qwen3-4B, Llama-3.1-8B as self-decoder, and Mistral-Small-24B — so the same activation is decoded by a model smaller than, equal to, and larger than the donor. Six English text families (Wikipedia, AG News, peS2o scientific text, SST-2 affect, DAIR Emotion, TweetEval, LMSYS-Chat-1M user turns, LatentQA control prompts) truncated to 64 tokens, each paired with one gist question and one detail question written by Qwen3-14B; 165,760 train / 2,172 validation / 1,560 test texts, validation and test balanced across families. Two out-of-distribution sets of 250 items each: harmful requests pooled from JBB-Behaviors, AdvBench, HarmBench, StrongREJECT and Do-Not-Answer, and one-sentence NBME clinical summaries.

## How they did it
Stage I is an inverter: a Q-Former-style cross-attention adapter projects the donor hidden state into 8 context slots read by 64 learnable queries across L cross-attention layers, producing a soft-token prefix in the decoder's embedding space; the decoder is a LoRA-adapted language model that generates a reconstruction of the source text under a fixed instruction ("What does this representation encode?"). It is first trained by ordinary autoregressive cross-entropy on the source text — question-agnostic by construction — and then optimised by DPO from that checkpoint, with the frozen SFT inverter (decoder *and* adapter) as the reference policy. Preference pairs are built by sampling eight candidate reconstructions per input and scoring them with a three-part reward: a gist score and a detail score from an interpreter answering the paired questions from the reconstruction alone, judged against the source by a Qwen3-32B judge, plus an anchor F1 over proper nouns and numbers, which is the only component that is rule-based rather than judged. Detected refusals get zero. Default weights are 0.35 gist / 0.35 detail / 0.30 anchor. Stage II keeps a separate frozen QA model that answers questions from the inverted text and never sees the source or the activation. Evaluation reports gist, detail and overall (their mean) recovery, an answer rate, Anchor F1, and two collapse rates: within-output repetition (fraction of outputs with a repeated 4-gram covering at least half the output) and cross-output identity. Every method is re-run on the same data under one protocol at its validation-selected layer, and the DPO checkpoint is chosen by validation, not taken from the last round.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **replacement read-out**: a piece of natural language generated from one activation, which a separate model then answers questions from. It is meant to be partial and inspectable rather than complete — the paper's claim is not that the text is the state but that anything the text supports is recoverable from the state, and anything missing from the text is a measured limit either of the inverter or of the state, distinguishable in a way a one-step verbalizer's answer is not. The DPO reward also makes the read-out's *failure mode* a design target: the anchor term is there so that the reconstruction can be scored lexically as well as semantically.

## How they judged success
Gist and detail recovery, macro-averaged over the six families, are the headline metrics; overall is their mean. The baselines are the right ones and are all re-run: Patchscopes and SelfIE (training-free), and AO, LatentQA and UAV (training-based, question-conditioned), each at its own best layer. Two controls carry the argument. First, a **chosen-only SFT control** — fine-tuning the same inverter on the *preferred* reconstructions from the DPO pairs — which isolates preference *contrast* from training on higher-reward samples. Second, a **five-seed paired stability check**, computing the DPO−SFT delta within each seed and reporting the mean and the paired SD, so the gain is not a lucky run. A cross-output collapse rate is monitored to test whether the inverter has learned a generic template instead of an input-specific mapping, and the layer sweep is reported per family because the best layer differs by source.

## What they found
Supervised reconstruction alone loses to question-conditioned baselines: on Llama-3.1-8B activations with a cross-model Qwen3-4B inverter the SFT overall score is 0.389, below UAV at 0.439 and LatentQA at 0.475. DPO closes and reverses that: 0.472 after one round, 0.624 at the validation-selected round, against the strongest baseline UAV at 0.492 and the donor's own self-decoding AVPO at 0.608 — i.e. a 4B external decoder beats a donor-matched verbalizer. On Mistral-Small-24B, 0.411 → 0.638 against 0.516. Even Qwen3-0.6B reaches 0.520 on the 24B donor, above AO at 0.496 and LatentQA at 0.516. The improvement is preference contrast rather than sample selection: chosen-only SFT moves gist from 0.254 to 0.257 and detail from 0.335 to 0.344, while a single DPO round moves them to 0.363 and 0.422 (gist-only reward) with the anchor-inclusive default at 0.334 / 0.403 plus Anchor F1 0.228. Over five seeds the paired gain is +0.089 gist (SD 0.010) and +0.070 detail (SD 0.009). The reward ablation shows a genuine trade: QA-based rewards *lower* Anchor F1 (0.232 → 0.201 for gist-only), so the anchor term is a regulariser against losing names and numbers. Depth helps in general but the family matters — affect saturates by middle layers while Wikipedia remains hardest at all layers and is the one case where DPO hurts detail at the last layer, with about 13.4% of post-DPO outputs collapsing and accounting for roughly 70% of that decrease. Iterative DPO keeps raising recovery while raising within-output repetition and length, and cross-output collapse stays below 1% throughout. Out of distribution, the picture splits cleanly: on harmful requests the r4 inverter is best on intent (0.77) and category (0.78), and on clinical summaries it recovers the key finding about three times as often as QA-trained baselines (0.11–0.12 against at most 0.04) — but it recovers the patient's *name* less often, while **fabricating names at 0.10 against 0.87–0.99 for the baselines**, a conservative failure mode the authors name explicitly. DPO also trades demographic accuracy for semantic recovery (age accuracy falls from 0.49 to 0.27).

## Limits and open problems they name
Only last-token single-layer activations are verbalised, with token-level and multi-layer read-outs left to future work; texts are capped at 64 tokens, so nothing is said about long-context states. The reward is largely judge-based: Qwen3-32B is simultaneously interpreter, judge and reward source, and the paper does not report how much of the measured gain moves with the judge — the anchor F1 is the only rule-based check on the lexical side. Iterative DPO degrades generation stability (longer outputs, more repetition, lower answer rate), which is why checkpoints are validation-selected, and the paper states the trade-off but offers no reward that fixes it. Wikipedia detail recovery at the final Llama-3.1-8B layer is a real regression, and the authors attribute it partly to weaker detail supervision for that family (22.6% of its preference pairs lacking the detail signal). Larger-decoder behaviour is described as sensitive to hyperparameters with a systematic study deferred.

## What we take from it
A **method** that fits our read-out work exactly: separate *recovery* from *answering*, and optimise the recovery step against a lexical rule-based check as well as judged semantic scores. If our verbalized read-outs were trained with DPO against (gist, detail, anchor F1), the anchor term is what would stop a fluent summary from drifting off the names and numbers that make it falsifiable — and the ablation shows that without it the judged rewards actively *reduce* anchor fidelity. A **measure** we should adopt regardless of method: **within-output repetition** and **cross-output identity** rates beside every generation-quality score, because the best-scoring checkpoint in this paper is also the most repetitive one, and cross-output identity is the cheapest test for whether a read-out has learned a template rather than an input-specific map. A **control** that is reusable and rarely run: the chosen-only SFT arm, which prices preference *contrast* separately from training on reward-selected data — the same discipline our own DPO-shaped fits (e.g. distillation of a scaffold into a direction) owe. And a **framing** worth carrying into any audit claim: the honest comparison is not "which read-out scores higher" but "which read-out fails by omission rather than fabrication", since 0.10 vs 0.87 name-fabrication is a bigger practical difference than eleven points of gist recovery.
