## What they wanted, and why

Multi-head self-attention is the place where the field's decomposition tools have never reached parity with the MLP: a head can be individually legible (an induction head, a successor head), but most heads attend diffusely, and the field cannot say whether that is because the head is genuinely doing something diffuse or because several distinct interactions are packed into it. The authors take the MLP superposition hypothesis and assert its attention analogue — that one attention unit (a specific read-between-two-token-positions, write-to-one-direction computation) can be spread across several heads while a single head can hold several units — and then build the attention-side instrument that the analogy implies. The larger goal is circuit tracing: attribution graphs and path patching become misleading when a feature is computed collectively, because a per-head attribution then distributes the cause across heads that each contribute only part of it. The paper is explicitly a first attempt in that direction, not a finished decomposition.

## What they studied

Two target models, all layers of each: Pythia-160M (12 heads per layer, head dimension 64) and Llama-3.1-8B (32 heads, head dimension 128), trained on 800M tokens of SlimPajama at context 256 and 1,024 respectively. The unit of analysis is neither the head nor the residual-stream direction but the *attention unit* — a read from certain token positions through a query and a key, producing a scalar activation, written to a single residual-stream direction. Each layer gets thousands of such units: 6,000 Lorsa heads versus 96 real ones in Pythia, 32,000 versus 128 in Llama, i.e. 500-1,000x more, of which only K = 12 (Pythia) or 32 (Llama) are active per token.

## How they did it

Lorsa is a replacement model for one attention layer, trained by MSE to reproduce that layer's real output. Each Lorsa head is an attention head with a normal query and key but a value and output that are one-dimensional, so its activation z is a scalar obtained by a softmax over the real multi-dimensional QK scores, weighted-summing a scalar read of each previous token. The output is computed for all H candidate heads at a token and then only the top-K by z are summed — the sparsity mechanism is a TopK gate over heads rather than an L1 penalty or a ReLU. Two design decisions are load-bearing and were chosen against their own intuitions: the QK dimension is kept at the *full* head width (shrinking it degrades reconstruction badly, and especially below the original head dimension), and query/key weights are *shared* across groups of Lorsa heads, so each head costs 4·d_model parameters and a QK-sharing group looks structurally like one real head whose OV dimensions have been split apart. The shared QK can be re-initialised from the real head or fixed to it; fixing is worse (FVU 12.4% vs 11.2% trainable and 11.3% random), which they read as evidence that Lorsa learns QK structure the original head did not carry.

Interpretation rests on three views per head. The top-16 activating token contexts from 100M held-out activations. The *z pattern* — the exact decomposition of z into per-previous-token contributions A·v, which is the attention equivalent of direct feature attribution for an attention-output SAE but with a single 1D OV path and no aggregation across heads. And the oblique projection of the head's output onto the basis formed by the real heads' outputs, which yields a per-head share and hence an effective number of source heads (the minimum set explaining 90%). Searches for specific functions use path patching to ablate a Lorsa head and let influence flow only through residual connections and MLPs, plus an attribution score O_h·∇L against the correct-token logit.

## What counts as an explanation here

A replacement model in the transcoder tradition, carrying a *dictionary* of thousands of atomic attention units per layer, each with a name, an activation site, a read pattern and one write direction. It is deliberately partial: reconstruction error is reported as a first-class quantity (fraction of variance unexplained), and the authors say outright they did not achieve complete independence between units. Predictive content is narrow and structural — a head's z pattern should name exactly which earlier tokens produced it, and a QK-sharing group should behave like a real head with its OV circuit split — but it is checkable per unit.

## How they judged success

Four instruments. Reconstruction: FVU per layer against a matched-size attention SAE on the same layer, plus a joint L(N,K) scaling law in parameter count and sparsity compared with TopK SAEs. Interpretability: automated interpretability in a two-phase explain-then-simulate protocol scored by GPT-4o, compared head-for-head against SAE features of the same size, evaluated with t-tests at α = 0.05 across 24 layer-wise comparisons, with score distributions over 100 heads/features per group. Rediscovery: path-patching attribution (Table 6's per-head contribution matrix) to confirm that Lorsa heads matching known circuits land on the heads that the literature says compute them, and that the Lorsa equivalents are found at all. Composition: the oblique-projection census of how many real heads each unit draws on. Ablations on QK dimension, binding ratio, QK initialisation, ReLU-vs-no-ReLU, RoPE and GQA adaptation.

## What they found

Criteria for cleanliness shift in the predicted direction: Lorsa heads are finer versions of known circuits — an induction head that fires only for "you", a successor head, a copy-suppression head, a sink head — and the arithmetic heads in Llama are the clearest new find, each fetching one operand under a handful of unrelated heuristics (operand in a numeric range, operand mod 10 in a narrow set, operand near a value), so that "36+62=" is served by three heads per operand, each contributing a partial constraint, and the heuristics match what SAE features on the same model show. Automated interpretability scores are statistically comparable to SAE features (6 wins, 3 losses, 15 ties across 24 comparisons), and both degrade in deeper layers. The superposition census is the paper's most consequential number: roughly half the units are attributable to a single real head, a quarter to two, an eighth to three, and an eighth to more than three — so the phenomenon is real but the paper's own headline claim is that at least half of the extracted units could have been found by head-level analysis alone. Units spread across real heads are the ones that head-level circuit tracing gets wrong, and the paper's own example is an induction unit split almost evenly across two real induction heads. Error analysis shows Lorsa's residual correlates with a same-size attention SAE's residual per layer, per token and in direction, taken as evidence of a shared dark matter for sparse dictionary learning on attention.

## Limits and open problems they name

The QK circuits are shared, not independent, which the authors flag as the main obstacle to calling the units independent — and they concede that some units may genuinely need multi-dimensional QK circuits, so full low-rank separation may be the wrong target. Both methods leave a majority of units inactive even after hyperparameter search, which they contrast with residual-stream SAEs where dead features are rare. Cross-layer superposition — the same interaction performed in several layers — is out of scope. Early attempts to find Q/K composition between Lorsa heads failed. The QK sharing means a z pattern is an artefact of all three of Q, K and V together, not a clean read of the query.

## What we take from it

- **The unit has to be able to be split or shared, and the census has to be published.** The transferable object is the census in Figure 11: for every extracted unit, how many of the original units it draws on. Our white-box model currently attributes per head; if a behaviour is computed by two heads at ~50/50, a per-head attribution silently halves it or double-counts it. Mechanical rule: every unit we publish carries the number of source units it is composed from, and any claim about a head's contribution is qualified when that head's output is only partly owned by one extracted unit.
- **A replacement model that reports FVU, whose residual correlates with an independent decomposition's residual, tells us the gap is in the method class rather than the method.** That is the cheapest available argument that a residual is a real object rather than a tuning failure, and it is a control our model should run: compare our residual against a differently-parameterised decomposition of the same layer rather than only against itself.
- **The second mechanism (QK sharing) is the interesting one.** Holding the QK circuit fixed while splitting the OV circuit into many sparse 1D outputs is a general recipe: fix the part you do not trust yourself to learn, decompose the part you do, and then check that the fixed part is not simply being copied (they check, via QK init ablation, that the learned QK differs from the real one — fixed QK is worse by 1.2 points of FVU).
- **A warning about interpretability evals that compares a decomposition to a decomposition.** Automated-interpretability parity with SAE features is a parity of *scores from the same rater on the same protocol*, not of correctness, and the paper's own case studies — the arithmetic heads where two views (operand plot and z pattern) sometimes disagree on whether a head is about one operand or a different one — show the score is the weaker evidence.
- **A shortcut for us:** path patching a single scalar-output head with an O·∇L score is a far cheaper attribution per unit than per-head ablation, and the head's shared QK means the expensive part of the computation is amortised across the whole group.
