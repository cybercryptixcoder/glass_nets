# From Local Prediction Error to Final Outputs: A Theory of Expert Sharing in Multi-Step Reasoning

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.17879

- **Date:** 2026-11-13
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.17879

## What they wanted, and why
A mixture-of-experts network can reuse one expert block across several layers or several reasoning steps, which pools training signal from different contexts and usually lowers error on each individual call. The authors' point is that this local number is the wrong thing to optimise: a multi-step system is graded on where it ends up, and a per-call improvement can be produced entirely by a bias that the later computation magnifies. They want an exact statement of when a local gain survives composition, and they want it in a form that survives being lifted from textbook linear regression to a routed, nonlinear Transformer with a hard router. The larger goal is the one this folder cares about: an account of a composed computation that predicts the effect of an intervention (tying, merging, forcing a route) on the *output*, not on an intermediate. As a second, separate question they ask what evidence would justify the everyday interpretability move of saying that an expert *implements* an operation because the router keeps sending that kind of input to it.

## What they studied
Two units, deliberately kept apart. (1) **Roles** — a role is a call environment plus a target behaviour: the same expert function inserted at layer 1 and at layer 2 defines two different roles because the inputs and surrounding computation differ, even when the task operation is identical. The estimators are role-wise linear maps fit on fixed orthogonal designs; the comparison is the average excess prediction risk of separate fitting, full sharing, and partial pooling. (2) **Experts in a trained model** — four-layer, width-256 causal Transformers trained to compose four operations over the integers mod 31, at horizons 4 and 8, trained with teacher forcing and evaluated free-running; cross-depth expert tying versus layer-local experts, three seeds in the main comparison, ten seeds in a balanced follow-up, four data budgets (64, 96, 128, 192 training traces). Evaluation banks are 32,768 independently sampled held-out-family traces each.

## How they did it
The theory starts from a specified linear model: M equally weighted roles, each with its own coefficient matrix, designs held fixed with orthogonal columns, centred noise with covariance σ²I, and a test input with identity second moment. Under those assumptions the expected excess prediction risk has a closed form for any fixed pooling weight λ between separate and shared, so the sharing decision becomes a comparison between the dispersion of the true role targets and a removable-variance term that grows with the number of coefficients, the noise level and inverse sample size; the optimal λ is a ratio of those two quantities. The same algebra gives an exact partition objective — the risk of any grouping of roles is the within-group dispersion plus a per-group penalty — and therefore an exact merge rule: merge two groups iff the weighted distance between their centroids is smaller than the variance saved by deleting one fitted expert. They then put a common *fixed* linear map between the role coefficients and the terminal error, with a free pooling matrix, and obtain the terminal risk as a quadratic in the pooling weight whose coefficients are the alignment between the original noise and the part pooling removes, and a bias-plus-noise term. To lift this off the linear case they write a routed program as a sequence of events, split each event's error into an *implementation* term (fitted vs target map, same instructions) and a *routing* term (actual vs aligned plan, same state), propagate both through a Lipschitz envelope, and give simultaneous high-probability bounds over finite expert classes with an effective sample size for the shared model. Finally, the causal half: they define an intervention on the route while holding the pre-action context fixed, define its defect as the distance between the forced effect and a candidate operator's prediction, and construct a counterexample model. The experiments reproduce the linear frontier by Monte Carlo, realise the reversal, and then measure one-step and free-running error on the trained models with paired, simultaneous intervals.

## What counts as an explanation here
Three artefacts, and the authors are explicit that they are different instruments. (1) An **exact risk frontier**: a closed-form criterion (dispersion below removable variance) plus an oracle pooling weight and an exact per-merge comparison — predictive, in the sense that it names the direction of the effect of a specific structural edit on the output, but conditional on a specified linear model. (2) A **decomposition and bound** for general routed programs: terminal utility error bounded by propagated implementation error, propagated routing error, and a per-expert-group term carrying reuse bias plus a finite-class deviation, which orders two sharing schemes when the difference is positive. (3) A **causal-abstraction test** for whether an expert implements a named operation: intervene on the route at a fixed pre-action state, compare the observed forced effect with the candidate operator's prediction, and report coverage. Nothing here is a full account of a model; it is a set of sufficient criteria and one refutation.

## How they judged success
For the theory, exactness: the risk formulas are stated as identities under named assumptions and checked by Monte Carlo against an analytic reference rather than by a significance test, and the appendix audits each core result's assumptions and says which extensions hold when they fail. For the experiments: paired differences with simultaneous intervals over a declared comparison family (18 primary comparisons at a two-sided radius of 2.0039 percentage points from 36 tails at 32,768 independent traces per checkpoint; the balanced follow-up uses a 40-contrast family over 240 endpoints with radius 2.3657). Two endpoints are always reported separately — teacher-forced one-step error on reference prefixes, and free-running terminal or complete-trace error on the model's own predictions — with the trace, not the token, as the independent unit. For attribution: label agreement against the route the model actually took, forced-action fidelity (average indicator that forcing a route reproduces the declared operation's output), cross-action fidelity excluding the requested action, a uniform-state and uniformly-forced collapsed-control reference, single-layer versus four-layer compound interventions, and held-out-transition fidelity to test whether a verified compound action generalises beyond the transitions it was trained on. A coverage lower bound is proved for randomised route audits: no estimator can beat a dependence on the product of sample count and per-action forcing propensity.

## What they found
The reversal is real and exact. With three roles, unit noise variance, target coefficients (1/2, −1, 1/2) and a fixed propagation (1, −2, 1), uniform full pooling cuts local average risk from 1 to 5/6 and raises terminal risk from 6 to 9 — because the pooled equal-coefficient component is annihilated by the propagation map while the target is not, leaving a squared terminal bias of 9; the same example's terminal risk is 6 − 12λ + 15λ², whose interior minimum is better than either endpoint. The clean condition under which no such reversal can happen is narrow and worth stating precisely: for orthogonal full pooling with centred isotropic noise, *every* strict local improvement survives to the terminal output for every target exactly when the propagation scales squared lengths by one common positive factor on the discarded contrast space. In general the direction of the pooling effect is governed by an alignment term between the retained noise and the removed component, which can be negative when pooling destroys a cancellation the separate model was enjoying. In the trained models the two endpoints disagree in exactly the predicted way: at 64 training traces, tying reduces teacher-forced step error by 5.18, 5.16 and 6.49 points (lower bounds 2.79, 2.77 and 4.09) while the terminal difference is −0.04, −0.01 and −0.14 points with every interval spanning zero; in the ten-seed follow-up at 96 traces the local contrast splits (six seeds favour tying, four favour local). All twelve architecture comparisons in the 18-comparison family cross zero, while all six supervision comparisons favour their candidate. On attribution the results are blunt: a route-supervised model with perfect label agreement at 64 traces has forced-action fidelity 0.3713 ± 0.0267; intervention-supervised models reach compound fidelity 1.0 on the four-layer action but single-layer fidelity of only 0.3274 ± 0.0247 averaged over layers; and intervention-supervised models trained with 16 state–action transitions withheld keep seen-transition fidelity 1.0 while reaching 0.0021 ± 0.0037 on the withheld transitions, with the gap reproducing at moduli 97 and 251 (withheld fidelity at most 0.00647 against seen fidelity at least 0.97763 across eighteen checkpoints, and all nine full-coverage controls at 1.0). Expert-replacement diagnostics find positive distance-to-terminal-harm associations in many accurate checkpoints, weakening or flipping sign in some lower-budget settings.

## Limits and open problems they name
The overview does not carry a standalone limitations section; the limitations are distributed, and the authors are unusually explicit that most results are *sufficient* rather than necessary (the table in Appendix Q marks each row accordingly). Named: the exact frontier needs a specified linear model with fixed designs and independent role noise, and the merge criterion can mispredict when a global equal-variance proxy is applied outside its assumptions (four sign discrepancies in a general-design table, recovered by the covariance-aware reference). The terminal ordering result does not bound discrete answer accuracy. The paired audits are guaranteed only when candidates are fixed before the independent data is seen, four forced actions averaged within a context and three seeds do not increase the independent sample count, and the low-data regime leaves both tied and local models near chance at the endpoint, so the null result is partly a statement about power. Causal attribution needs a sufficient pre-action macro state and supported actions; a rarely forced action cannot be audited precisely no matter how large the total sample is, and the held-out-transition result is described as consistent with memorisation without uniquely identifying the internal implementation. Compute was only nominally matched across budgets and parameters differ with vocabulary.

## What we take from it
A **warning with a formula**, which is the most useful kind. Three things are directly portable. (1) **Grade the account at the endpoint the system is judged on, and print the local score only as a diagnostic.** The mechanical rule: every per-unit or per-component improvement our account reports is accompanied by the same comparison at the final output, on free-running trajectories rather than teacher-forced prefixes, with an interval from the same family of comparisons; if the local gain is resolved and the terminal one is not, the finding is "the local metric does not see the effect" and the local gain is not quoted as evidence of capability. (2) **A local-to-final reversal is predictable in closed form when there is a linear propagation, and the diagnostic is the overlap between what the edit removes and what the downstream map amplifies.** Before crediting any edit (ablating a head, merging a component, steering), compute the projection of the edit onto the directions the downstream computation amplifies and onto the target, and report the alignment term and the bias term separately; an edit that removes a component the target needs is a local gain and a terminal loss and this is arithmetic, not noise. This is the sibling of run 574's ablation-coverage rule and of run 573's restoration-denominator rule: three papers, three denominators — patched tokens, in-graph units, and now the propagation map — and in every case the wrong denominator makes a confound look like an explanation. (3) **A verified action is not an ascribed unit, and coverage is the free variable that decides.** Their separation of compound (four-layer) fidelity 1.0 from single-layer fidelity 0.33, and of seen-transition fidelity 1.0 from withheld-transition fidelity 0.002, is the sharpest statement the folder has of a distinction it keeps meeting: a probe, a patch or a steering vector can be shown to work at a scope and still carry no claim about the individual unit it is named after; and a verification set that overlaps the discovery set cannot tell memorisation from mechanism. Mechanically: our accounts report, for every attributed unit, the intervention at the compound scope and at the single-unit scope separately, plus a held-out-transition (or held-out-input) replication, and a failed held-out replication downgrades the claim to "works on the transitions it was fitted to" rather than being smoothed into an average. **A shortcut to steal:** their effective-sample-size formula for a pooled estimator means our own pooled statistics should report the number of independent units *after* pooling, not the raw count — a headline N can be inflated by exactly this factor.
