# Playing Dumb: How a Chess Transformer Simulates Users Across Skill Levels

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.03439
- **Authors / organisation:** Difan Jiao, George Eilender, Zhenwei Tang, Ashton Anderson (Department of Computer Science, University of Toronto)
- **Date:** 2026-11-02
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03439
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (arXiv HTML v1, ~74k clean chars, 5 figures, 3 tables, Appendices A–D; prose, all quoted table cells and every inline formula numeral intact; figures 1–14 read via captions only)
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
A model conditioned to act like a novice may know less, or may use what it knows differently. The difference matters wherever behaviour is read as evidence about knowledge: a monitor that judges capability from output will underestimate a model that can be made to underperform; an educational simulation that only reproduces novice errors has not shown it represents the student's knowledge state. Natural language makes the two hypotheses hard to separate, because proficiency and domain knowledge cannot be measured independently. The paper therefore asks the question in chess, where player strength is a number, move quality has an engine reference, and individual concepts such as a threat to one piece are decidable from the board. The authors' aim is to establish a general recipe — measure encoding with probes, then measure *use* with interventions that can only succeed if the information was already there.

## What they studied
Maia-2, a skill-conditioned transformer that predicts human chess moves for 11 Elo categories (from below 1100 to 2000 and above), with a ResNet position encoder, two Transformer blocks whose attention queries receive additive skill embeddings, and a single 1024-dimensional residual stream feeding a shared linear policy head; the head holds 8.2% of parameters. Skill, board, and weights are held fixed and only the rating category varies, so every comparison is the same position under different conditioning. The unit of analysis is the average-pooled residual stream after each of the two blocks, read by linear probes along the concepts axis (172 oracle concepts), decoded by a retrained head, and edited by SAE feature amplification in block 2.

## How they did it
Ground truth comes from an **oracle**: 172 binary chess concepts with deterministic labels computed from the board — 16 king/queen-safety, 7 fork and 10 pin patterns, 23 positional-structure concepts, and 128 per-square attack/defence contacts (one concept per square for threats to the opponent, one set for threats to the mover). Positions are drawn from a **Blundered Transitional Dataset**: from Lichess games with engine evaluations, they keep positions where Maia-2's prediction moves monotonically from a suboptimal move at low skill to the engine's best move at high skill and never reverts (2.28M candidates), then keep only the 1,147,280 where the lowest-skill pick is a blunder of more than 10 win-probability points (70/10/20 split). **Encoding** is measured by ℓ1 logistic probes fit per concept, per block, per skill level on the fixed positions (up to 5,000 positives and 5,000 negatives, class-truncated, seed 42, regulariser chosen on validation), then applied without refitting from every source skill to every target skill to build an 11×11 transfer matrix. **Use** is tested by two interventions, both of which can only recover information already present. Head SFT freezes the backbone and fine-tunes only the policy head for each of the 71 concepts with at least 500 qualifying positions, using Maia-2's own highest-skill prediction as the target for that concept's positions; a high-skill-only variant trains and early-stops each head on the top skill condition alone (each example repeated ten times), so no lower-skill state is ever used as training input. SAE steering trains JumpReLU dictionaries on each block's mean-pooled FFN contribution (selected by best feature AUC over a separate 93-concept calibration set; 16,384 features), ranks features per concept by ROC-AUC against the oracle label, and at inference amplifies the top-k features by a factor α before decoding and reinjecting the block-2 FFN contribution; k and α are grid-searched per concept and skill condition on training positions, and the random-feature variant runs the identical procedure with uniformly sampled features. Success is scored as best-move accuracy in five 200-Elo bands, plus the mean transition point TP (the first skill level from which a position stays correct), CP loss, win-rate loss, blunder rate, and an *ignore rate* — the share of moves that do not even address the target concept (relocate or defend the threatened piece).

## What counts as an explanation here
A dissociation, not a mechanism. The claim is that a fixed model's conditioning changes which retained concepts reach the decision, established by the pair (probe accuracy flat) × (decisions recoverable from the same states). Explanations are partial by design: concept encoding is operationalised as decodability by a linear probe, and the authors say so — their probe is a lower bound on what is encoded, and nothing here locates where the skill conditioning acts inside the blocks. It predicts one thing strongly: performance under a conditioning context should be treated as a lower bound on what the model can do, and a restricted intervention should be able to raise it.

## How they judged success
Concept encoding: probe test accuracy across 11 skill levels with the per-concept standard deviation across levels as the stability statistic, cross-skill transfer accuracy, and the off-diagonal gap with bootstrap CIs. Recovery: best-move accuracy on held-out concept-filtered positions against the frozen baseline; corner rates for best move, CP loss and win-rate loss; skip rate; ignore rate; the two gap statistics (STD of the five band means; TP). Controls: random-feature steering at the same k and α, cross-concept head application (each concept's head scored on every other concept's test positions), a high-skill-only training arm, and — for steering, which does modify representations — the pre-steering probes re-applied to the steered states with a **paired two-one-sided equivalence test** against a ±1-point margin. Engine reference from Lichess server evaluations where available, otherwise Stockfish at depth 20, with a logistic centipawn→win-rate map.

## What they found
Encoding is flat while behaviour moves. Mean probe accuracy is 96.0% after block 1 and 95.9% after block 2, with a mean per-concept standard deviation across the 11 skill levels of only 0.57 points at both blocks, and the same flatness holds within each of the four concept categories. Transfer is nearly total: mean off-diagonal accuracy 95.6% and 95.4% against matched 96.0% and 95.9%, gaps of 0.40 and 0.43 points (CIs 0.34–0.47 and 0.36–0.50); the gap grows smoothly with skill distance, and the extreme cells are the informative ones — highest-skill probes still read 93.2% and 92.8% on lowest-skill states, and lowest-skill probes 94.7% and 94.3% on highest-skill states, so conditioning shifts the representation slightly but leaves one linear readout usable across the whole range.

Head SFT recovers nearly all of the behavioural gap while the representations it reads are frozen. Best-move accuracy in the lowest band (<1200) goes 0.144 → 0.828, the 1200–1399 band 0.443 → 0.854, 1400–1599 0.621 → 0.870, 1600–1799 0.779 → 0.883, and the top band 0.894 → 0.912, so the gain is not bought by trading high-skill performance. The gap statistics collapse: STD 0.295 → 0.031 and TP 4.24 → 1.32, against 4.02 for random steering. Chess quality moves the same way below each position's baseline transition point: ignore rate 93.7% → 4.1%, blunder rate 52.5% → 8.0%, win-rate loss 0.150 → 0.026, CP loss 225.3 → 61.8. The two obvious deflations are tested and do not explain it. A head trained on one concept does **not** improve positions governed by others — other-concept heads sit at or below the frozen baseline from the second band onward — so the recovery is concept-specific rather than a uniform stronger policy. And a head trained and early-stopped only on highest-skill states reaches 0.800 in the lowest band, within 2.9 points of the all-skill head in every band, so the mapping transfers downward and does not need supervised exposure to low-skill states.

SAE feature steering recovers part of the gap without retraining anything. In the lowest band best-move accuracy goes 0.144 → 0.427 against 0.163 for random features (TP 4.24 → 3.42 against 4.02), ignore rate 93.7% → 48.6%, blunder rate 52.5% → 32.1%, win-rate loss 0.150 → 0.094, CP loss 225.3 → 161.1. Because steering edits activations, the probes trained before steering are re-applied to the steered states: averaged within concept the mean change is +0.86 points (90% CI 0.73–0.98), inside the ±1-point margin under a paired two-one-sided test (p = 0.031), so the gain in use did not come with a measurable change in decodable encoding. The authors are explicit that the two intervention sites are complementary evidence, that success at the policy head says nothing about where Maia-2 naturally implements skill adaptation (the Elo embeddings enter every block's queries, not the head), and that they have not shown H2 to be the only mechanism.

## Limits and open problems they name
The oracle is static and atomic — it cannot see multi-move planning, search, or long-term positional judgement, which is exactly the knowledge the authors say a chess study cannot address. Encoding is measured as linear decodability, so "unchanged encoding" means "unchanged to an ℓ1 probe", not "unchanged representation". Only 71 of 172 concepts had enough qualifying positions for the intervention work. The recoverability test is not a location claim about the model's own mechanism. Whether LLMs behave the same way is untested and harder to test, because prompting a proficiency level does not validate that the adaptation happened, and an independent knowledge measure would be needed; the paper also states plainly that nothing here shows weaker output reflects deliberate concealment.

## What we take from it
Method, and a battery design we should copy. (1) **Encoding versus use is a two-number claim** — decodability under a fixed readout, and recoverability from the same states by an intervention with a frozen trunk — and either alone is misleading: a probe that is right while behaviour is wrong looks like a redundant readout, and recoverability alone looks like an artefact of the new head. (2) **The strongest single control is the high-skill-only arm**: when the intervenable map is learned only on the *other* condition's states and still works on the target states, "the new component was fit to the site it is tested on" is dead, at the cost of one extra training run. (3) **Cross-concept application is the specificity control**, and it can come out negative — other-concept heads performed *below* the frozen baseline, which is a stronger statement than the usual "no gain". (4) **An equivalence test, not a null**, is the right way to report "the representation did not change": the steering arm still moved probe accuracy by +0.86 points and says so, with a margin declared in advance. (5) There is a measurability precondition worth importing whole: the clean statement required a fixed model, a *quantitative* conditioning variable we can sweep, and a deterministic checker for the concept — which is why the same design in LLMs is still open, and why our own probes should prefer domains with an oracle.
