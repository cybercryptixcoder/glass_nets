## What they wanted, and why
Whether a model knows a fact and whether it can use that fact are different questions, and phonology in Chinese offers an unusually sharp test because the three components of a Mandarin syllable — initial, final, tone — can be varied one at a time while everything else is held fixed. The authors want to know how much phonological knowledge text-only and audio-trained models actually hold, where each component is linearly readable inside the network, and whether that readable information can be moved to change what the model outputs. The motivation is partly linguistic (does a logographic writing system, which lacks systematic spelling-to-sound rules, force the model to store sound some other way?) and partly methodological: they argue that behaviour, probing and causal intervention each answer a different question, and that a study using only one of the three will misdescribe the knowledge. A larger purpose is to establish whether "encoded but not usable" is a real, measurable regime rather than a slogan, using a domain where the encodings are discrete and the labels are not in dispute.

## What they studied
The unit of analysis is the representation of a single Chinese character at the position where it appears at the end of a short prompt, at the input embedding and after every transformer layer — probed for three categorical labels (initial, final, tone) and intervened on with a residual-stream direction. Eighteen models are behaviourally evaluated: fifteen Qwen base checkpoints across Qwen2.5 (0.5B–32B), Qwen3 and Qwen3.5, plus Qwen2.5-Omni-7B and DeepSeek-V4-Flash as a frontier reference. The representational work centres on the Qwen2.5 base family (0.5B/1.5B/3B/7B) for a clean size axis, with Qwen2.5-Omni-7B added as the audio-bearing comparator, and steering is run on Qwen2.5-7B-base and Qwen2.5-Omni-7B using directions extracted from each model's own hidden states.

## How they did it
Three linked experiments on one shared asset. **The benchmark first:** PinyinBench is built from the 3,500 most common characters plus a 3,500–7,000 frequency band, giving 12,600 homophone-judgment minimal pairs, 5,400 feature-judgment pairs and 5,000 polyphone pairs, 38,183 in total. Each item is a sentence asserting a phonological relation between a target and a candidate, with one candidate that satisfies it and one distractor that violates it; the seven distractor conditions in homophone judgment vary which of the three dimensions matches and which differs (all three differ; exactly one matches; exactly one differs), so each dimension can be isolated. Pronunciation is obtained with pypinyin and converted to IPA with Dragonmapper, with initials and finals split by reverse-deriving each initial's longest common IPA prefix. **PROBING:** 2,300 monophonic characters with at least 20 examples per class, extracted at the character's position under four prompt templates (bare, "pronunciation of this character", "this character is", "meaning of this character"); representations are standardized and reduced to 32 dimensions by PCA fitted on the training fold only, then an L2-regularized multinomial logistic regression is trained at every layer for every dimension and template, evaluated by five-fold stratified CV in macro-F1 and compared against a fixed-permutation shuffled-label probe, with the difference reported as selectivity. **STEERING:** for a transition such as initial *j* to *l*, the direction is the mean difference of hidden states between minimal pairs that differ only in that dimension and match on the other two, averaged over three templates and injected at the source character's position as h + αv with α swept from 0 to 10, at five sites (embedding, layers 8/13/20/24) over 82 transitions (40 initial, 30 final, 12 tone). The effect is scored as the next-token probability mass on single-token hanzi in the target class minus that of the source class, and both the peak advantage over the α sweep and its increase from α = 0 are reported, because the response collapses under over-injection. Two controls are added that the headline depends on: a held-out set of same-class characters never used to build the direction, and a prompt-cue control that removes the source character from the prompt (so the direction is injected at a different position).

## What counts as an explanation here
Three partial explanations stacked, none of them a mechanism. Behaviour gives a difficulty ranking across the three dimensions. The probe gives an *availability* claim — this label is linearly recoverable from this layer of this model — made disciplined by the shuffled-label control, so the claim is about information present rather than about a classifier's capacity. Steering gives a *causal-access* claim — this direction, added here, moves output probability mass toward that class — with the held-out test guarding against the trivial reading that the direction memorised its construction set. The paper deliberately does not claim a circuit, and it explicitly refuses the stronger phonological reading of its own asymmetry (it notes the segmental/tonal split resembles autosegmental theory but says the results do not establish that the models encode such a structure).

## How they judged success
Behaviour: zero-shot accuracy against 50% chance, with two scoring protocols kept separate (sentence log-probability comparison for base models, binary-choice letter scoring for Omni and DeepSeek-V4) and reported per distractor condition rather than pooled; plus a joint logistic regression of correctness on standardized frequency gap and phonological distance (DIMSIM, with PanPhon as a tone-blind cross-check), with standard errors clustered on the target pronunciation because 2,690 target characters collapse to about 709 pronunciations. Probing: macro-F1 and selectivity (true minus shuffled-label F1), five-fold CV, per layer and per template. Steering: peak target-minus-source class mass over the α sweep, its gain over α = 0, the fraction of initial transitions that flip, the relative perturbation norm at the flip point, and a collapse rate for the decline after the peak. Controls: a prompt-cue ablation, a held-out-character test, and (in an appendix) a re-extraction from a single template to check the direction is invariant to the template used to build it.

## What they found
The difficulty ordering is consistent and large. When target and distractor differ in all three dimensions, Qwen2.5 base models score 56.3%–78.7% and DeepSeek-V4 98.4%; when they differ **only in tone**, Qwen2.5 base models sit at 51.1%–55.7% — near chance even at 32B — and DeepSeek-V4 falls to 72.7%. The initial- and final-mismatch conditions sit between those extremes (roughly 53.6%–67.7% for 7B, and above 90% for V4). Phonological distance and lexical frequency both matter and are nearly uncorrelated (r = −0.015): accuracy rises monotonically with DIMSIM distance at matched frequency and falls with the frequency gap; every frequency coefficient is negative and every distance coefficient positive, with distance worth about +1.6 to +5.5 percentage points per standard deviation and frequency about −17 to −24 points per SD, the distance effect surviving task fixed effects in twelve of fifteen models. A third task shows the same split inside polyphone disambiguation: rejecting a reading the character does not have is learnable and reaches about 95% for the largest models, while choosing the correct reading over a homophone of the character's *other* valid reading stays near chance for every model including DeepSeek-V4. Probing inverts the ordering. Tone is already strongly decodable at the input embedding and improves only modestly with depth, while initials and finals start substantially lower and rise sharply, peaking in deeper layers; the reported selectivity is 0.482 for tone against 0.708 for both initials and finals, and the tone margin is the smallest despite its far smaller label inventory (four classes). Across 0.5B–7B the peak macro-F1 moves from 0.258 to 0.736 for initials, 0.169 to 0.667 for finals and 0.487 to 0.695 for tone, with selectivity rising in step so the segmental gain is not the probe fitting arbitrary labels. Steering tracks behaviour rather than probing: mean peak target-minus-source advantage is 0.52 for initials, 0.40 for finals and 0.16 for tone at the embedding, and the ordering holds at every site (roughly 0.54/0.41/0.14 at L8 and 0.46/0.38/0.15 at L20), with 76–85% of the target-class characters that rise at L8 never having been in the construction set. An explicit-tone prompt raises the tone advantage to 0.25 and its class mass to 0.41 from a 0.25 baseline, while removing the source-character cue turns every effect negative — so the cue is necessary and, given the cue, tone remains the smallest effect. The audio-trained model complicates the story in exactly one direction: Omni-7B probes better than the text-only 7B on all three dimensions at both the embedding and its peak layer, including tone, but steers no better — its initial and final advantages are in fact lower than the base model's — and stays the weakest dimension on tone behaviourally. One more structural result is stated in passing and holds at the top of the model stack: all three dimensions **reverse sign at layer 24**, the last site.

## Limits and open problems they name
The behavioural hierarchy spans seventeen models, but probing and steering are centred on one family, and steering covers five depths in two models. Steering is scored through class probability mass under elicitation prompts, not free generation, so the held-out result is a statement about how the class spreads rather than about why individual characters rise. Tone results concern isolated-character lexical tone only — no neutral tone, no tone sandhi, no context-conditioned reading selection, which their own third task shows is a separate and much harder problem. The Omni comparison probes the text backbone only, so the gain is attributed to multimodal training as a whole rather than to speech input, and since the models' training data is unknown the paper characterises representation and use rather than origin. The paper also does not explain the direction of the encoding/accessibility dissociation; it offers the possibility that recognising a character's tone is easier than comparing tones across two characters, and leaves it untested.

## What we take from it
A **measure** and a **framing**, both directly usable. The measure is selectivity beside every probe number, computed the same way in every cell: a probe's raw macro-F1 is not comparable across dimensions with different class counts (four tone classes against 22 initials and 31 finals make the raw numbers meaningless as a ranking), and the paper's own ordering claim rests on the selectivity column, so any readout we report should carry its shuffled-label counterpart and we should never compare two probes' raw accuracies when their label inventories differ. The framing is the three-column table — behaviour, decodability, steerability — reported for the same labels, the same characters and the same model, which turns "the model knows X but does not use it" from an assertion into a pattern with a specific shape, and the shape that appears here is worth keeping as a hypothesis to test rather than a law: the property that is **already readable at the input embedding** is the one that is hardest to steer. That is a cheap, checkable prediction for our own battery — order candidate features by the layer at which a probe first succeeds and expect the shallow ones to be the worst handles — and it comes with a ready-made confound check in the paper's own prompt-cue ablation: an intervention whose effect vanishes when the prompt no longer names the source has been riding the prompt, not the representation. The peak-advantage and peak-gain pair and the flip-cost and collapse columns are a reporting convention we can adopt wholesale, since they price an intervention as an α-curve with a peak, a gain and an over-injection penalty rather than as a single number.
