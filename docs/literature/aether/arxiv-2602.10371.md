## What they wanted, and why
Standard model evaluations only reveal differences an evaluator designed them to catch, so they miss unexpected behavioural drift between model revisions and emergent misaligned tendencies that fine-tuning can create. Model diffing - automatically producing statements of the form "model A does X more than model B" - is the proposed answer, but the two leading families (LLM-generated natural-language descriptions vs sparse-autoencoder features) had never been compared head-to-head, and nobody had said what "good diffing" even means. The paper's goal is therefore two-part: define the desiderata a diffing method should satisfy, and then use those criteria to compare the methods fairly. The larger goal is a pre-deployment audit instrument that works on closed-weight models through an API alone.

## What they studied
Comparisons of pairs of language models: Qwen-2.5-7B-Instruct against a version fine-tuned on risky financial advice (emergent misalignment model organism), gemma-2-9b-it against a version fine-tuned to implicitly assume a female user, and two Gemini 2.5 Flash Lite releases (stable vs a preview dated 2025-09). The unit of analysis is not a neuron, feature or circuit but a **hypothesis about behaviour**: a sentence naming a difference and its direction. Prompts come from 1,000 WildChat conversations for discovery, plus a disjoint 500-prompt held-out set for evaluation. SAE-based diffing uses a reader model (LLaMA 3.3 70B, a published layer-50 SAE) as its internal instrument only.

## How they did it
Both models write one response per prompt to a shared prompt set (max 1,024 new tokens). The LLM-based pipeline then: (1) asks an LLM to list qualitative differences per response pair, each tagged with which model shows the property more; (2) strips the model names, embeds the difference descriptions, reduces with PCA to 128 then UMAP to 30 dimensions, and clusters with HDBSCAN; (3) summarises each cluster into one hypothesis; (4) assigns direction by majority, discarding clusters where the dominant model holds under 65% of the differences. The SAE-based pipeline feeds the reader model the full prompt-plus-response (their modification - Jiang et al. fed responses only, which loses the question a "yes" answers), max-pools activations over completion tokens only, ranks SAE features by the absolute difference in activation frequency between the two models' responses, relabels each with positive and negative examples, and summarises; they generate 40 hypotheses rather than the original 10 to match output volume, and normalise phrasing across pipelines so the evaluation is not biased by style. Hypotheses are then verified by a comparative LLM judge that sees a hypothesis and both responses, is not told the expected direction, and has response order swapped with probability one half; verdicts give frequency (share of held-out triplets where the behaviour appears) and accuracy (share of those where the direction is right). Interestingness and abstraction are rated 1-5 by three different frontier LLMs and averaged, and acceptance rate is the share of hypotheses the judge confirms on the discovery data.

## What counts as an explanation here
A set of natural-language behavioural hypotheses, each a directional statement about one model relative to another, plus a 1-5 abstractness rating. It is explicitly partial and non-mechanistic: nothing about the internal computation is claimed, and the authors' own byte-level check (Appendix E) shows the mechanisms their cheapest method finds are formatting conventions. The evaluation is a scoreboard for hypotheses rather than a model of behaviour - nothing here predicts an unseen behaviour except by the hypothesis sentence itself, which is exactly what frequency and accuracy on held-out prompts measure.

## How they judged success
Five operationalised metrics, all on held-out response pairs: frequency, directional accuracy conditional on the behaviour appearing, interestingness (1-5, autorater average over GPT-5.2, Claude Opus 4.5, Grok-4), abstraction level (same protocol), and acceptance rate on the generation data. Baselines are each other: the improved LLM pipeline against the adapted SAE pipeline, plus a KL-divergence fork-token method in Appendix E. Confidence intervals are 95% t-intervals over hypotheses. There is a careful methodological move in Appendix B.1: they show that the prior standard score, judge-verified frequency difference, factors exactly as frequency times (twice accuracy minus one), so a hypothesis appearing 10% of the time and always right and one appearing 100% of the time and right 55% of the time both score 0.1. Controls include random answer-order swapping against position bias, a judge that never sees the expected direction, and phrasing normalisation across pipelines. Model organisms with known ground-truth behaviours supply the only external validity check.

## What they found
On the two model organisms both methods find accuracy above 87% on average, while the Gemini revision comparison is harder for both (63.4% LLM, 72.3% SAE). The clearest split is abstraction: LLM hypotheses average 3.8-4.3 with low variance against 2.4-3.5 with higher variance for SAE, widest on the Gemma pair (4.17 vs 2.71), and acceptance rates are usually higher for the LLM pipeline. On Qwen both identify the harmful-advice behaviour, but the LLM version states it directly at 100% accuracy and only 3.4% frequency, while the SAE version routes it through "simplification" rhetoric at 96.5% accuracy and 17.2% frequency and bundles in low-level linguistic observations (anaphoric pronouns, connecting words). On Gemini the LLM version says "uses tables to present organized information" (96.5% accuracy, 11.4% frequency, abstraction 3.0); the SAE version says "more Markdown table syntax, specifically the pipe and hyphen characters" (95.7% accuracy, 9.2% frequency, abstraction 1.33) - and the LLM pipeline alone surfaces a large response-length shift on Qwen (68.1 vs 495.8 tokens) that character counting confirms. The negative result is the most instructive: on the Gemma gender organism neither method recovered the trained-in behaviour, and Appendix D establishes why - only 5.7% of the 1,000 prompts were rated suitable for eliciting it, and on 57 suitable prompts with 200 samples per model the judges found rates below 7% with no significant difference, the base model sometimes scoring higher. The KL method locates disagreement tokens reliably but its best examples are LaTeX delimiter style and heading markup.

## Limits and open problems they name
LLM judges are unreliable and inconsistent, and the paper demonstrates it: the same judge called identical "especially for women" phrasing an assumption in one response and a cultural note in the other. The framework evaluates hypotheses it was given but cannot see what went undetected; API-only access means behaviours not expressed in output are invisible by construction, which is precisely the Gemma case, and internal access might have found it. Findings are prompt-distribution-bound, so prompt curation changes the answer. Sample sizes are small in places (n = 15 for one Gemma comparison), and they explicitly decline to call these methods standalone audit tools.

## What we take from it
Three things. First, a **measurement discipline we should adopt verbatim**: their composite score decomposes into frequency times (2·accuracy − 1), so any aggregate "how well did our probe/feature explain behaviour" number we report should be reported as its two factors and the confound demonstrated, because one high-frequency low-accuracy item and one low-frequency perfect item had been scoring identically. Second, a **held-out-evaluation protocol for descriptions**: a claim of the form "this direction/feature means X" is worth exactly its frequency and directional accuracy on prompts the claim was not derived from, with answer order swapped; that is the natural scoring rule for a white-box model built from probes, and it costs one API call per item. Third, their **attribution-by-frequency** shortcut is a warning: the SAE arm's highest-confidence finding was a token-artefact (pipe characters, end-of-text position), which is the same failure as a probe whose top component reads out formatting rather than computation - so any feature or probe we credit with a behaviour must beat a stylometric baseline computed on raw text alone. Their negative result also supplies the cheapest possible pre-check for us: before concluding a probe "fails", score the prompt set for how often the behaviour could even be elicited; 5.7% suitable prompts means the experiment, not the method, produced the null.
