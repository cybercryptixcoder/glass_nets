# Stable and Steerable Sparse Autoencoders with Weight Regularization

- **Key:** arxiv-2603.04198
- **Authors / organisation:** Piotr Jedryszek; Oliver M. Crook. Department of Biology, University of Oxford (Jedryszek); Kavli Institute for Nanoscience Discovery and Department of Chemistry, University of Oxford (Crook).
- **Date:** 2026-06-16 (v2; v1 2026-03-04)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.04198
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text

## What they wanted, and why

The paper starts from the reproducibility complaint that has become standard in this literature: an SAE trained on the same activations twice learns substantially different dictionaries, so a "feature" recovered in one run is not a stable object across runs. The authors' framing is that activation sparsity alone under-determines the solution, and they ask whether the cheapest classical answer — an explicit penalty on the weights themselves — buys anything. They pitch this against methods that add structure (mutual feature regularization across parallel runs, attribution-guided distillation across retraining cycles) and against the discouraging result that SAE-based probes track strong raw-activation baselines only patchily. Their three questions are cross-seed consistency, downstream quality (interpretability and steering), and how the penalty interacts with ordinary design choices such as tied initialization, unit-norm decoders, and which sparsity mechanism is used. The larger goal is a reliably reusable dictionary, not a better reconstruction number.

## What they studied

Two models at very different scales, plus three latent units. On MNIST they train SAEs on flattened images (784 inputs, 1,568 latents, i.e. 2× overcomplete) under an L1 activation penalty, used as a toy for intuition. On language they train SAEs on layer-3 residual-stream activations of Pythia-70M-deduped through SAEBench, sweeping three architectures (TopK, BatchTopK, Matryoshka), sparsity k in {40, 80, 150, 320} and six weight-penalty strengths from 0 to 1e-4, always with tied initialization and unit-norm decoder columns. The unit of analysis is the dictionary entry (a decoder direction paired with an encoder row), and the object being measured across runs is the correspondence between entries from differently seeded fits.

## How they did it

The training objective is reconstruction error plus the usual sparsity term plus an L1 or L2 penalty applied jointly to the encoder and decoder matrices; for TopK models the sparsity term is dropped because the encoder enforces the constraint itself. Two implementation details carry much of the result and the authors flag one arithmetic consequence explicitly: a unit-norm constraint on decoder columns makes the decoder's L2 norm constant, so the same penalty coefficient does different work depending on whether that constraint is on — with normalization in place, the weight penalty is effectively an encoder penalty. Cross-seed consistency is measured the way the cited seed-variability paper does it: absolute cosine similarity between the two decoder feature matrices, Hungarian assignment for a one-to-one pairing, then three numbers — mean best-match cosine, the fraction paired above 0.7, and a "shared" count that is stricter in that it demands the encoder-side and decoder-side matchings agree and both similarities exceed 0.7. Entries are filtered as "alive" by requiring encoder–decoder cosine above 0.1, a filter the authors justify from a strongly bimodal distribution. On MNIST they compare untied versus tied initialization crossed with no penalty, L1 and L2 across three seeds. On Pythia they first check SAEBench metrics (sparse probing, spurious-correlation removal at 10, targeted probe perturbation at 10, cross-entropy recovery), then run cross-seed comparisons for TopK only. The downstream arm samples 300 alive features per SAE, has a judge model write explanations for each, keeps those with valid explanations, and steers each against three prompts by adding a scaled decoder direction to the residual stream during generation with fixed strength; a second judge scores whether the steered output relates differently to the feature's concept on a 1–5 scale, and scores of 4 or above count as success. They also compute the mean absolute pairwise cosine of decoder columns, reported against the expectation for random unit vectors at that width.

## What counts as an explanation here

A weight-level account of the dictionary: which entries survive training under a given penalty, and how far the surviving entries agree across independent fits. The explanation is not a text description of what a feature means — it is a claim about the geometry of the dictionary (alignment of each entry's own encoder row with its own decoder column; orthogonality among columns) plus a behavioural reading of what happens when one entry's direction is added to the residual stream. The authors explicitly refuse to treat a high auto-interpretability caption as evidence of quality on its own, and their most interesting claim is a dissociation: the penalty makes captions and controllability agree better without making the captions better.

## How they judged success

Three families of metric, none of them a single headline. Reproducibility: mean best-match cosine, fraction paired above 0.7, and the strict shared-feature fraction, computed on all entries and on alive entries separately, over three seeds. Quality: the four SAEBench metrics, with regularized configurations identified as Pareto-optimal or dominated rather than ranked by a sum. Behaviour: judge-scored steering success on a 1–5 scale with 4 as the threshold, reported as a sample-level rate with a chi-square test; the Spearman correlation between per-feature auto-interpretability score and per-feature steering success, with a slope and standard error and a test of the change in slope across conditions; and a check that the auto-interpretability score distribution itself is unchanged. Controls are within-method comparisons (penalty on versus off; tied versus untied initialization; with and without decoder normalization), with a random-vector cosine baseline supplied for the orthogonality numbers and across-seed standard deviations printed for them.

## What they found

On MNIST, without tuning, an L2 penalty produces a bimodal distribution of encoder–decoder alignment: a small set of strongly aligned entries that visually correspond to clean strokes and curves, and a large rest. Under the language-model recipe (tied init, unit-norm decoder), the shared-feature fraction jumps from 1.74% with no penalty to 22.50% alive-shared for L2, and to 14.10% for L1; untied initialization gives roughly 0% shared in every arm. So the penalty only pays when it is combined with the other two design choices, which is the paper's most reproducible interaction.

On Pythia-70M TopK, comparing no penalty with the strongest L2 arm at four sparsity levels: strict shared fraction among alive entries goes from under 2% to about 35%, mean best-match cosine from at most 0.32 to about 0.7, and the fraction of decoder entries above 0.7 cosine from under 10% to about 50%. The median mask is that this is bought with dictionary death — the L2 arms collapse the majority of latents to zero norm, and at k=40 the alive pool falls from 16,384 to 1,699 entries, still more than three times the 512-dimensional residual stream. Steering at k=40 rises from 6.3% success (57 of 900 samples) to 13.0% (72 of 555), chi-square 17.92, p below 1e-4 — but only 185 of the 300 sampled L2 features produced a usable explanation at all, against all 300 unregularized ones, and the authors state that the improvement is conditional on explained features and that the 115 invalid explanations are themselves a finding.

The interpretability–steering link strengthens: Spearman 0.060 (p = 0.075) without regularization against 0.144 (p = 7e-4) with it, with the success-probability slope rising from 0.11 (SE 0.06) to 0.40 (SE 0.12), a significant change (Z = 2.21, p = 0.027), while the auto-interpretability score distributions stay similar. On orthogonality, the honest reading is that their initial hypothesis mostly fails at the sparsity they steered at: over all entries L2 lowers mean pairwise decoder cosine (0.043 to 0.036 at k=40), but restricted to alive entries k=40 is essentially identical (0.043 vs 0.042); only at k of 80 and above do surviving entries become more orthogonal than the unregularized dictionary (0.028 vs 0.035 at k=320), and there the unregularized alive set drifts toward the random-vector baseline of about 0.035. Their attribution for the steering gain is therefore pruning of interfering directions, not disentanglement.

## Limits and open problems they name

Everything on language is Pythia-70M-deduped, so scaling is untested; cross-seed and steering analyses are TopK only; steering is one judge and one sparsity level. The largest gap they name is the one their own discussion makes urgent: they suspect L2 is largely performing implicit effective-dictionary-size selection, and they did not run the comparison that would settle it — a smaller unregularized dictionary matched on alive-feature count. They also note the high death rate rules out applications needing coverage, propose diagnostics to separate useful from pathological death, and suggest annealing schedules and combination with attribution-guided distillation.

## What we take from it

Three things. First, a measurement discipline for cross-seed stability that our account should copy whole: report the strict shared count alongside the looser paired fraction and the mean best-match cosine, and always print the alive-entry version next to the all-entry version, because the two denominators move in opposite directions under exactly the interventions that are supposed to help (here the alive fraction of the dictionary collapses while the alive-shared percentage rises sixfold — 4.91% shared of all entries on the untied L2 arm against 22.50% shared among alive entries on the tied arm is a direct example of the same table supporting two very different sentences). Second, an outcome-measure pairing that the folder has been missing: a stability claim scored against a *behavioural* read-out (steering success under a judged criterion) rather than against interpretability captions, with the two distributions printed to show that the caption score did not move. The pattern "stability up, captions flat, controllability up" is the cleanest case in this folder of a stability gain that is functional rather than cosmetic. Third, a caution we should keep permanently: their own preferred explanation is a confound they could not exclude — if the penalty's benefit is mostly capacity selection, then a leaner dictionary would suffice, and any of our claims that a training-time intervention improves unit identity must be compared against a capacity-matched baseline before it is called an improvement. On the gather side: an L2 penalty jointly on encoder and decoder is a trivially cheap knob worth including in any sweep of our own, and the tied-init-plus-unit-norm combination is a prerequisite rather than a competitor.
