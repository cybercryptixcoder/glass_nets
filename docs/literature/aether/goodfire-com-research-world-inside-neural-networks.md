## What they wanted, and why
The post is an argument for a research programme rather than a report of a study. Its premise is that concepts inside a network are not sets of straight directions but low-dimensional curved surfaces — a closed loop for months and weekdays, a smooth curve for years and for characters in a sequence — and that the field's dominant instrument, the sparse dictionary of directions, is therefore the wrong shape. The larger goal is stated in stages: understand a model's ontology (how it carves up the world), then the algorithms that operate over that ontology, then the behaviour those algorithms produce. They connect this to the wider interpretability ambition of knowing when a model will fail, auditing it, and designing safer models, and they close with the claim that this programme is the scientific opportunity of the era and is feasible with research agents doing the empirical collection.

## What they studied
Two demonstrations and no systematic study. The first is a small image-action world model trained on the classic mountain-car environment: it predicts the next frame given the current frame and a random left/right action, and the analysis looks at the image encoder's embedding vectors across car positions. The second is a language model's activation space examined with an unsupervised manifold-finding pipeline the authors say will be described in later work; the object recovered is a region populated by words sharing an ending sound, ordered from perfect to poor rhyme. The unit of analysis in both cases is a manifold in activation space plus a location along it, fitted to embeddings; no layer-by-layer sweep, no neuron or head analysis, and the LMs examined are not named in the post.

## How they did it
For the world model they trained the encoder on images of the car at different positions within the simulation, plotted the embeddings, and observed a one-dimensional string-like arrangement where nearby positions sit near one another. They then fitted a smooth one-dimensional curve to that string and used it as a path for intervention: hidden activations were edited to move a point along the fitted manifold, and the model's predicted frame was inspected to see whether the car had moved correspondingly. The contrast case is the standard steering construction — a vector from activation difference between two conditions — and here specifically a straight path between the two endpoints of the string. They report what happens when the intervention follows that straight line rather than the curve: output becomes garbled at points away from the data, and some of these paths pass through a different valid activation, so the predicted car appears at that other location instead. For the language-model side they run an unsupervised pipeline over activations, obtain a curved surface, then take the SAE features that contribute to reconstructing it and read their automated interpretability labels to ask whether any of them names the property the surface is organised by.

## What counts as an explanation here
A geometric ontology: an account that says which curved surface a concept occupies and how position along it corresponds to a quantity in the world. It is explicitly partial — the post says the geometry is the data structure, and that computation over it is a separate and unfinished half — and it is meant to be predictive in the intervention sense: steer along the manifold and the model's output should change in the corresponding direction, whereas steer off it and the model should break. No dictionary of features, no circuit, no surrogate model; the geometry replaces the unit of analysis rather than supplementing it.

## How they judged success
Nothing quantitative. The checks are demonstrations: whether the steered prediction shows the car moving up and down the hill, whether a linear interpolation visibly corrupts the output or relocates the car, and whether the automated labels on SAE features contributing to the rhyme manifold mention rhyme. There are no baselines, ablations, held-out sets, agreement statistics or human ratings, and the post does not present the unsupervised pipeline in enough detail to be reproduced from it; the authors point to forthcoming work for the method. The best evidence in the piece is the failure case, because a straight path that produces incoherent output is at least a falsifiable observation, and they report it as an observation, not a measurement.

## What they found
Two claims. First, a scalar spatial quantity can sit on a curve rather than on a line — the encoder's position representation is string-like, and moving along a fitted curve changes the predicted frame's car position smoothly — while the straight-line version of the same edit lands in unoccupied regions where the model produces garbage, or crosses into another valid state and teleports the car. Second, the SAE features that reconstruct the rhyme manifold describe letter-level fragments (words beginning with particular syllables, the token appearing in titles, morphological prefixes) and none of them names the phonological property that orders that part of the space; the authors present this as the general "shattering" failure, where a dictionary fragments one coherent structure into many apparently unrelated pieces. Both findings are presented as legible illustrations; the only numbers in the post are in the list of feature indices and their labels.

## Limits and open problems they name
The post is candid that the unsupervised pipeline is not described here and that the steering-versus-manifold comparison is continued in the next installment. It concedes that SAEs remain valuable for scale and unsupervised coverage even while arguing they are insufficient. It does not address how a manifold should be validated, how many manifolds a layer contains, how geometry composes across layers, or whether a fitted curve is the computation or merely a shadow of it — the causal question that its own mountain-car demo is designed to answer is answered only for a toy world model. There is also a framing risk the authors do not raise: the evidence they cite for curved structure is a mixture of toy models, small LMs and single domains, so "abundant and undeniable" is a rhetorical summary of a heterogeneous literature rather than a measurement.

## What we take from it
A framing, a warning and a method — no measure. The framing is the three-level decomposition (representation, computation, behaviour) with geometry as the data structure that computation consumes, which is a clean way to state why our predictive model needs an ontology before it needs a simulator: a surrogate that predicts behaviour without naming the structures behaviour runs on can be accurate and unreadable. The warning is sharp and directly usable: an intervention that moves along a straight line between two points in activation space is not a small perturbation of the concept, it is a jump into space the model may never occupy, and the observed failure (incoherent output, or a silent arrival at a different valid state) is exactly the "voids" problem the folder has met from the other side in manifold-constrained steering. The method is the diagnostic: fit a curve to the set of activations that vary with a known quantity, intervene along the curve and along the straight line, and report both outcomes — if the line fails and the curve works, the geometry is at least a good enough description of the occupied set to steer within. What the entry cannot supply is a validated manifold: the post never says how to test that a curve is the right curve, which is precisely the question our own decomposition needs answered, and the folder's `goodfire-com-research-can-saes-capture-neural-geometry` is the entry to trust for the operational definition of capture. For the specific claim that SAE features miss the organising property of a manifold, the post gives one example and no count; treat it as a hypothesis to check on our own activations, not as a result.
