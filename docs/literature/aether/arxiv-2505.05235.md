# Advancing Neural Network Verification through Hierarchical Safety Abstract Interpretation

- **Key:** arxiv-2505.05235
- **Authors / organisation:** Luca Marzari, Isabella Mastroeni, Alessandro Farinelli; Department of Computer Science, University of Verona, Italy (single affiliation on the byline)
- **Date:** 2025-05-08 (v1, the only version listed)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.05235
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text — arXiv HTML (abstract, §1–§7, acknowledgements, references, appendices 7–10)
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
A formal verifier answers one bit: safe, or not safe. The authors argue that this encoding throws away most of the information a deployment decision needs, because it cannot distinguish an error that is mildly wrong from one that is catastrophic, and it forces the analyst into either an over-strict property (nearly everything fails) or an over-permissive one (nearly everything passes). Their motivating example is a road-sign classifier: mistaking a yield sign for a stop sign is a misclassification, but the vehicle is still reacting to a critical safety sign, and a binary verifier reports only "not robust". They want the verification question itself to carry a hierarchy of tolerable outcomes, so the tool can answer with a *level* of safety instead of a bit. The larger goal is richer feedback at no extra verification cost — an account that says not just that something failed but which of several graded failures occurred.

## What they studied
Reachability-based verifiers over feed-forward networks, where an input box is propagated through abstract semantics and the output is a tuple of intervals. The unit of analysis is the output tuple plus a *class abstraction*: a lattice of output categories supplied by the analyst, plus a widening operator on the input box. Two settings carry the empirical work: a Habitat-Lab social-navigation agent, which is an LSTM with a ResNet18 visual backbone taking an arm depth camera, a human detector and stereo depth cameras and emitting linear and angular velocities; and a CIFAR10 classifier evaluated on a standard verification benchmark with the alpha-, beta-CROWN family of tools.

## How they did it
They keep the standard propagation machinery and change what is asked of its result. First they define a decision function on abstract outputs that returns the *set* of intervals which could still be the maximum — an index is dropped if some other interval's lower bound exceeds its upper bound, so an index whose whole range is dominated can never win. Second, they formalise the output hierarchy as an upper closure operator on sets of (class, interval) pairs, which groups classes into tolerated categories and maps any output set to the coarsest category containing it. Third, they replace the earlier notion of a per-input perturbation with a *widening* on the abstract input box itself, so the perturbation acts on the box that is actually propagated rather than on concrete points. The verification question becomes: does the category of the reachable answer set stay strictly inside the full output space, or does it overflow into the unacceptable top? If it stays inside, the instance is *abstract safe* — the network's answer did not match the exact target but landed in a tolerated group. They prove this formulation implies the older per-point notion and not conversely, give a two-neuron example separating the two, and show the problem is still NP-complete, so nothing is being made easy in the worst case. In the evaluation they build the hierarchies by hand: for Habitat, velocity classes with a stringent threshold set and a relaxed set; for CIFAR10, three levels — exact classes, then coarse groups (vehicles, terrain vehicles, mammals and so on), then animals versus vehicles — with a 100-second timeout per instance, and they encode the properties in the standard VNN-LIB format so any tool can read them. They also perturb one input feature at a time by a fixed epsilon and watch which level the answer falls into, as a coarse per-feature sensitivity reading.

## What counts as an explanation here
A graded verdict on one instance, not a description of the model. The account is: here is the set of answers the network could still give under this box, here is the coarsest tolerable category that set fits into, and here is therefore which rung of your safety ladder this instance sits on. It is sound in the same sense as the underlying verifier (it never claims a level the reachable set does not occupy) and it is partial by construction — it names no neuron, feature or direction, and the ladder itself is an analyst's judgement, which the authors state openly and defend as standard practice in this literature. The only thing it predicts is where the next instance will land on the ladder, and only for the hierarchy that was fixed in advance.

## How they judged success
Counts of instances in each of four buckets — safe, abstract safe, unsafe, timeout — reported per perturbation size and per abstraction level, against the same tool run with only the exact-class property; a walk through one toy network with hand-computed interval propagation showing that the abstract answer is delivered where the exact answer is inconclusive; an enumeration of the safe regions of a toy input space to confirm that the abstract-safe answer corresponds to a real, small region of the box where the tolerated answer is actually selected; and the ranking of three perturbation types by which rung of the Habitat hierarchy they land on. No reference solution, no human ratings, no ablation of the hierarchy shape — the hierarchy is the intervention, and its variants are compared as columns.

## What they found
On CIFAR10 at perturbation 1/255 the exact-class column gives 46 safe, 4 unsafe; the first grouping gives 46 safe, 2 abstract safe, 2 unsafe; the coarsest gives 46 safe, 4 abstract safe and zero unsafe — the four "unsafe" instances were all cross-category errors of nothing worse than a mammal being called another mammal. The more interesting column is the timeout one: at 2/255 the exact property times out on 12 instances, the middle hierarchy on 8 and the coarsest on 4, while unsafe falls from 11 to 6 to 1 and instances resolving as safe stay at 27 throughout. So the extra levels did not buy safety by reclassifying successes — the 27 safe instances are identical at all three levels, and the movement is entirely out of the unresolved column. That is the paper's real claim and it is a good one: the cost of unresolvedness is being paid for by the tolerated categories rather than by more splitting. The Habitat experiment is qualitative: three unsafe scenarios crossed with three perturbation types (a brightened patch, an epsilon-ball, a probabilistic blackout patch), and the graded verdicts let them order the perturbations by severity — in the first scenario the light patch and the epsilon-ball stay tolerated while the blackout patch crosses into unsafe, which a binary verifier would have reported as three identical failures. The toy example does the smaller job it is meant to: a fixed epsilon on one input feature changes the answer from provably safe to merely abstract safe, a fact obtained from a single interval propagation.

## Limits and open problems they name
The hierarchy is the whole method and it is hand-built by an analyst with domain knowledge, so the technique inherits whatever the ladder encodes; the authors name learning hierarchies from data as future work. Worst-case complexity is unchanged (NP-complete), so the gain is in practice, not in the guarantee, and the paper offers no bound on when the practical gain appears. The per-feature epsilon sweep measures sensitivity, not attribution — the paper calls this "understanding which regions or features greatly impact the safety hierarchy" and the evidence is a handful of toy and qualitative cases. The Habitat evaluation is three scenarios from one publicly released agent and is reported as a demonstration of ranking, not as a measured benefit. The comparison set is small: one tool family, no comparison against a verifier given the same time budget on the exact property, and no measurement of how many additional refinements would have resolved the timed-out instances under the binary question — which is exactly the number that would make the timeout reduction a like-for-like comparison.

## What we take from it
A framing, and a measure. The framing: the dependent variable of a verification account can be a *lattice* rather than a bit, and the correctness condition becomes containment in a coarsest tolerated category, which is a strictly weaker and much cheaper thing to prove than exactness. That is a transferable move for us — an account of a network can be scored at several resolutions of the same question, and the interesting result is not the score at the fine resolution but where the extra resolution changes nothing. The measure: their Table 2 separates three columns that most papers merge — instances that resolve as *safe* at every hierarchy level, instances that shift from unsafe to tolerated, and instances that shift from timeout to resolved. Ours has an exact analogue: an account's coarse verdicts should be compared against its fine verdicts on the same items, with the items that agree at all resolutions reported separately from the items the coarser resolution rescued, because only the second set is evidence that the coarser account is doing work. Mechanical rule for us: **every time our account is evaluated at more than one resolution of the same question (per-token versus per-claim, per-neuron versus per-feature, exact versus tolerated), the results table prints the agreement count as its own column, and a benefit is credited only to the items whose verdict changed — an increase in resolved cases achieved by reclassifying items an exact account had already resolved is a loosening of the question, not a better account.** Cross-reference run 614's exactness ablation: that paper showed a tighter representation changing no answers, this one shows a looser question changing only the answers that had none.
