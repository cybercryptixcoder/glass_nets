# Can small transformers learn the language of Coxeter groups?

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.10936

- **Date:** 2026-11-07

## What they wanted, and why

Frontier mathematics models are trained by organisations whose compute no academic group can match, so a mathematician who uses one understands neither the machine nor why it produced what it did. This paper proposes the opposite arrangement: a model small enough to be read, a mathematical task structured enough that "what the model learned" can be checked against known theory, and a mathematician in the loop who can turn an observed internal pattern into a stated conjecture and then prove it without the model's help. The larger goal is a genuine division of labour — the network proposes structure, the human certifies it — with the certificates being ordinary mathematics rather than a benchmark score. It is a step toward interpretability that produces *mathematical output* instead of descriptions of behaviour.

## What they studied

One tiny TransformerLens `HookedTransformer`: a single attention layer with several heads of small dimension and a small ReLU MLP width, no layer norm, learned absolute positional embeddings, causal attention, a vocabulary of three generators plus padding, sigmoid outputs per generator, and all biases frozen at zero for interpretability (the parameter count, head count, dimensions, learning rate, weight decay and epoch counts are all blanked in the HTML build, so they are not quoted here). The task: predict the **left** or **right descent set** of a group element — i.e. which generators can begin, respectively end, some minimal-length word for it — at every prefix of a word. The unit of analysis is the **attention pattern** of the final token, aggregated over words of a fixed length; the object being predicted is a set of three booleans, not a token. The group is the infinite Coxeter group affine A2, the symmetry group of the triangular tiling of the plane, generated by three reflections with braid relations between each pair.

## How they did it

Three synthetic datasets in increasing order of structure, each a set of words in the three generators with per-prefix binary labels for descent-set membership: (i) random words, which may repeat a generator adjacently; (ii) reduced words, where adjacent generators must differ; (iii) **ShortLex normal-form** words, the lexicographically first minimal-length representative of each element, generated by adapting Casselman's normal-form multiplication algorithm — all 1,998 ShortLex words of length at most 36, split 30/70 train/test with the guarantee that no complete test word appears in training. The model is trained autoregressively with component-wise binary cross-entropy on the per-position labels. Two further manipulations carry the argument. First, **degenerate-data controls**: the same architecture is trained on the random and reduced sets, and test accuracy is tracked as a function of word length, with misclassification broken out by token index. Second, **masking experiments**: on the ShortLex set, one run masks the first tokens of every input and another masks everything except the first tokens, isolating which part of the input the model actually uses. Four training runs with different split, initialisation and training seeds check that the observed attention patterns are not one run's accident. The final step is not an experiment at all: the authors read the attention heatmaps, state two word-theoretic claims they suggest, and prove them by hand with the standard Coxeter toolbox (braid relations, Matsumoto's theorem, the Weyl action on minimal roots, and an argument over alcove galleries).

## What counts as an explanation here

Jointly, three things that are usually kept apart. (1) A **theorem**: two propositions, each stating that the descent set of a normal-form word in this group is determined by a small, specified part of the word — so the explanation's output is a mathematical statement with a proof, not a description of the network. (2) An **attention pattern** treated as the *source* of the conjecture rather than as its evidence: the heatmap is where the idea came from, and the proof is where it is established, so the network is credited with a heuristic and the mathematics with the result. (3) A **behavioural sufficiency claim** that connects the two — the masking study, which is meant to show that the information the theorem says determines the answer is the information the model in fact relies on. The explanation is partial and openly so: nothing here claims a circuit, and the paper names extracting the Brink–Howlett automaton's computation explicitly from the weights as future work.

## How they judged success

Per-prefix and per-word exact-set accuracy on held-out words, with separate accounting for **unseen subwords** (the strict test, since test words are novel but their prefixes may not be); accuracy as a function of word length, which is the length-generalisation test and the one that separates memorisation from an algorithm; misclassification rate by token index, which shows where in the word the model fails; the masking pair (mask the prefix versus mask everything but the prefix) as a causal-usage test; and seed replication of the attention pattern itself. There is no benchmark, no baseline model except uniform attention, and no statistical test — the evidence is the size of the gap between data regimes and the shape of a curve.

## What they found

**Data quality, not capacity, set the ceiling.** Test accuracy on the left-descent task began degrading at some length for the unreduced set, at some longer length for the reduced set, and at no tested length for the ShortLex set, where the model reached near-ceiling accuracy — and generalised to subwords it had never seen, including on a manually split dataset of all 63 words of one length plus random longer words where 7,335 subwords were unseen. On the random and reduced sets the misclassification rate grew rapidly with token index, which the authors read as the model not having learned a length-generalisable procedure; on the ShortLex set it did not.

**Two theorems came out of the heatmaps.** For the right-descent task the final token's attention showed a **same-parity** structure across heads, which suggested: for a minimal-length word, the right descent set is contained in the last two letters, always contains the last, and contains the second-to-last exactly when a braid-flippable pattern appears at an odd index. For the left-descent task attention concentrated hard on the **start** of the word, which suggested something much stronger: the left descent set of any element of this group is completely determined by the **first six letters** of its ShortLex normal form — a bound that does not follow from the known algorithm and that the authors note they could not find stated explicitly in the literature, while expecting experts may recognise it.

**The masking study agreed with the second theorem.** Masking the first tokens of the input cost most of the test accuracy; masking everything except the first tokens left it nearly unchanged. The attention concentration on the opening tokens recurred under every seed, as did the same-parity structure on the right-descent task.

## Limits and open problems they name

One group, one task, one architecture, one layer — and a task chosen partly because a finite word-processing algorithm was already known to exist for it, which is not true of most mathematics. The proofs are theirs, not the model's; the paper is explicit that the model suggested the statements and contributed nothing to their verification, and the generative-AI statement notes that AI helped with code, figures and literature search but not with proofs or prose. They flag that even among experts there is disagreement about what a circuit means (citing the re-interpretation of the Chughtai et al. group-multiplication circuit in terms of quotient groups), and that the obvious next step — exhibiting the Brink–Howlett automaton as explicit computational circuits in the weights — is not done here, only proposed, along with extending the method to other families of infinite Coxeter groups. The proofs also lean on figures (alcove galleries drawn by hand), so the argument is not purely symbolic.

## What we take from it

**A framing, and a warning.** The framing is that a useful testbed for a white-box model is a task with a *known* algorithm and a *stated* ground truth per position, because then an internal pattern can be checked against theory instead of against another probe — this is the cleanest instance in this folder of a task where "the model learned the right thing" is a decidable question, and it argues for preferring structured synthetic domains over natural-language behaviour whenever the goal is prediction rather than description. The warning is sharper and is about evidence type: **an attention heatmap licensed a conjecture, and a proof — not the heatmap — established it.** Read as evidence, "attention concentrates on the first six tokens" would be a plausible-looking pattern that could have been an artefact of length, position or data regime; the paper's own degenerate-data controls show exactly how such an artefact arises (on unstructured data the same architecture produced no length-generalising behaviour at all, so any pattern read off that model would have been noise). So the paper supplies a two-tier standard to copy: the network may *propose* a regularity, and the proposal must be cashed out as a checkable claim before it earns the word explanation. **A measure to import:** accuracy-versus-length plus misclassification-by-index is the cheapest available discriminator between a learned procedure and a fitted shortcut, and it costs one extra grouping of the same predictions — the folder's entries rarely plot either, and the curves here are what make "it learned an algorithm" more than an assertion. **A method note:** masking is used here as the causal test, and its pairing (drop the informative span versus keep only it) is the right shape, because the first arm alone only shows the span matters somewhere while the second shows it suffices — the same keep-only/drop-only pairing we should be using on our own probes. **The caution to carry:** the paper's own deflationary reading of its strongest result — that experts probably know the six-letter bound already — is worth copying as a habit, because a finding that only looks new to the outsider is a limit on the *interpretability claim*, not on the mathematics.
