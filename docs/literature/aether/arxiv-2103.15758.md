# Compositional Abstraction Error and a Category of Causal Models

- **Key:** arxiv-2103.15758
- **Authors / organisation:** Eigil F. Rischel, Sebastian Weichwald; University of Strathclyde (Rischel), University of Copenhagen (Weichwald)
- **Date:** 2021-08-05 (v2); accepted at Uncertainty in Artificial Intelligence (UAI) 2021
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.15758
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

Causal models are always wrong at some level of description, so the useful question is not "is this model true" but "how much worse is this simpler model than the detailed one I already trust". A body of work (Rubenstein and co-authors for exact transformations, Beckers and Halpern for approximate ones) had already formalised the second question, but only for a single step: it could compare a coarse model against a fine one, not account for a chain of simplifications. The authors' observation is that models are built iteratively — someone simplifies one part, someone else another, and the pieces get recombined — so a notion of error that cannot survive being chained is of limited practical use. Their target property is therefore *compositionality*: if the reference model was simplified into an intermediate one and the intermediate one simplified further, the composite simplification's error should be bounded by the sum of the two steps' errors. They argue that without it, a small validated error at one step tells you nothing about the model you end up with.

## What they studied

Finite interventional causal models: a directed acyclic graph over finitely many variables with finite value sets, plus one Markov kernel per variable giving its distribution given its parents, plus the interventional distributions these induce for any subset of variables being set. The unit of analysis is not a single model but a *morphism* between two models — a transformation — and the object that carries the error is the morphism, not the object. No networks, no data, no training. Illustrations are small finite models (a two-air-conditioner room-temperature model with a hundred-valued scale; a cholesterol abstraction where LDL and HDL are replaced by total cholesterol).

## How they did it

- Define the category: objects are finite interventional causal models, and a morphism from a low-level model to a high-level one is a triple — a surjection on variables (each high-level variable abstracts over a set of low-level ones), a *measurement* map that takes the low-level values of a fibre to a high-level value, and an *intervention kernel* that takes a high-level intervention value to a distribution over low-level interventions. Measurement goes low-to-high; intervention goes high-to-low and is a distribution rather than a point, so the physical ambiguity of implementing "set the temperature" is inside the formalism instead of assumed away.
- Choose the error measure as a distance between kernels rather than between outcomes: the square root of the Jensen-Shannon divergence, taken as a supremum over intervention settings. The motivation for an information-theoretic measure over, say, a metric on outcome values is stated as a design argument — specifying a metric on the high-level variables requires having already decided that the high-level variables matter, which is exactly the decision being evaluated, and an information measure needs no such commitment.
- Prove the two lemmas that make the measure usable: composing kernels is non-expansive in this distance, and the distance is *compositional* in the sense that post- or pre-composing both arguments of a distance with the same map cannot increase it. Then define the transformation error as the largest disagreement, over all high-level intervention subsets, between what the high-level model predicts and what you get by converting the intervention down through the kernel, running the low-level model, and measuring back up.
- Prove compositionality as one triangle inequality over a three-model diagram: error of the composite is at most the sum of the errors of the two steps. The proof is the diagram chase itself, and the authors note this makes the paper a worked instance of enriched category theory — the error is an enrichment of the model category over a category of "error spaces" in which the tensor adds errors.
- Build a counterexample showing that the obvious alternative fails: using KL divergence, two legitimate-looking single steps (errors 0.22 and 0.39) compose into a step whose actual error is 1.52, more than twice the sum. An appendix proves the gap is unbounded in general — for any tolerance and any target, KL-inequality can be arranged so individual steps are arbitrarily close to zero while the composite is arbitrarily large — and re-proves it on finite sets so the failure is not an artefact of infinite support.
- Extend from "error relative to my reference model" to "error relative to reality": define an implemented model as a model plus a measurement protocol plus a physical procedure for each intervention, and define its error operationally as the optimal score in a two-player recognition game where a referee either implements the intervention physically or samples from the model, and the guesser must say which. The optimal score turns out to be the Jensen-Shannon distance, so model error is measurable by interventional experiment. Then show that if a transformation is implementation-preserving, the error of the transformed model's implementation exceeds the original's by at most the transformation error — which is the statement that lets a validated reference model certify a simpler one without new experiments.

## What counts as an explanation here

A transformed model together with a certified error, and the certification is by composition rather than by re-measurement. The "explanation" of a coarser account is the morphism: which low-level variables each high-level variable aggregates (measurement) and what a manipulation of the high-level variable does to the low level (intervention kernel). It is approximate by construction and the approximation is quantified — the error is a number in [0,1] on a distance scale — so unlike the exact-transformation literature the answer to "does this abstraction hold" is a bound rather than a yes. Predictive content is direct: from a reference model with known error, the error of any model reachable by a chain of transformations is bounded by the sum of the chain's errors, with no further experiments.

## How they judged success

By proof, plus one deliberately chosen counterexample to a rejected design. The results are: kernel composition is non-expansive under the chosen distance; the distance is compositional; transformation error is subadditive under composition; and the implemented-model statement that error increases by at most the transformation error under an implementation-preserving transformation. There are no experiments, no benchmark, no baseline and no statistics — the paper is a foundations paper and the demonstration of why compositionality is needed is arithmetic on a hand-built three-model chain, reproduced both with a non-compositional measure (KL) and with theirs.

## What they found

The headline result is negative and then repaired: KL divergence between interventional distributions is not merely loose but arbitrarily non-compositional, so a modelling pipeline that validates each simplification locally can end up with a final model that is very far from the reference model it believes it matches. In the worked chain, predictions on one interventional setting drift from a temperature above the low end in 39% of cases to 0.01% — the distribution has moved in a way neither small step's error signalled. Replacing the divergence with the corresponding distance fixes it structurally: the composite error in the same example is 0.37 against a permitted 0.42. The second result is the ground-truth argument: because the recognition-game error of an implemented model is exactly the divergence, and because error only increases by the transformation error along an implementation-preserving chain, a model can be certified against reality by certifying it against a reference that was itself validated — which is the only route available when ground truth is unattainable.

## Limits and open problems they name

Finite, discrete, acyclic models only — the whole construction is on finite sets and finite kernels, and continuous or infinite state spaces are out of scope. The measure depends on the chosen distance, and the authors are explicit that the framework only inherits compositionality for distances for which the two structural lemmas can be proved; they exhibit a Wasserstein-type metric on metric spaces where compositionality *fails* unless every map is required to be non-expansive, and they point out that this shortness requirement is a real constraint rather than a technicality. The implemented-model section is informal: "same effect" and "same" are not made precise, the existence and uniqueness of the induced implementation are stated as expectations rather than theorems, and the functor-to-ground-truth is described as inaccessible. The intervention kernel that encodes implementation ambiguity is left entirely to the modeller to specify, with no guidance on what makes a kernel admissible.

## What we take from it

- **A certified error has to survive chaining, or it certifies nothing.** Our accounts are built in layers — a probe suggests a direction, the direction defines a site set, the site set defines a claim about behaviour — and each layer is usually validated against the layer below it. This paper says that pipeline is unsound unless the error measure is subadditive, and gives the cleanest possible demonstration: two steps each well under tolerance, composite more than twice their sum. Anything we report as "our high-level account is within ε of the network" must be measured against the network, not assembled from per-layer ε's.
- **Use a distance, not a divergence, and say why the distance was chosen.** The argument in §1.5 is worth copying wholesale: a metric on high-level outcomes presumes the abstraction level being evaluated, so an information-theoretic measure is the way to score a candidate abstraction without already having decided that the candidate's variables are the important ones. That matters for us because our batteries do have to choose outcome metrics, and the choice quietly privileges whichever variables the metric separates.
- **The intervention kernel is the honest place to put implementation ambiguity, and we currently put it nowhere.** This paper forces the modeller to state a distribution over low-level manipulations for every high-level manipulation. In our setting that is the distribution over which sites, and which directions at those sites, count as "the same" intervention on a named concept — and the paper's cholesterol example makes the consequence concrete: restricting the implementation set produces a different (in their example, smaller) abstraction error. Every patch battery should declare its kernel, and the declared kernel should appear in the claim, not in the appendix.
- **Error relative to reality, obtained without new experiments, is a formal object and we can borrow its shape.** The recognition game (referee flips a coin, guesser says real-or-model, score is log-loss) turns "how good is this account" into something a human or a discriminator can be asked in a single session, and its optimum is the same distance the theory uses. That is a cheaper evaluation instrument than the batteries we run, and it is comparable across accounts, which our per-battery scores are not.
- **A fourth statement of the general-versus-particular discipline.** The framework gives a bound that is valid for every model in the chain and for every distance satisfying two lemmas, which is exactly the "no universal guarantee is not our account is wrong" distinction our recent entries have been circling — arriving here as a statement about which *measures* admit composition rather than about which *abstractions* survive reparameterisation.
