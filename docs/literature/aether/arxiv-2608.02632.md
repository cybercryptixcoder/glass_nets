# LLMs Can Annotate Attribution Graphs

- **Key:** arxiv-2608.02632
- **Authors / organisation:** Ameen Patel, Max Zhang, Nathan Hu — Stanford University (three authors, equal-contribution note printed; correspondence on a Stanford CS address)
- **Date:** 2026-07-28
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.02632
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Circuit tracing turns a forward pass into an attribution graph whose nodes are individual components — MLP neurons or sparse-autoencoder features — and those graphs are the field's main artefact for reading computation off a model. The last step of producing one is manual: a researcher groups nodes that stand for the same underlying concept into *supernodes*, and on a dense graph that step alone costs one to two researcher-hours. The authors take circuit tracing as a hypothesis-generation tool rather than a proof procedure and ask the smallest question that would unblock it — what is the simplest automated pipeline that annotates a graph well enough to yield insight? Their answer is deliberately unglamorous: describe each feature with an existing automatic-interpretation method, then hand the descriptions to a language model and ask it to group them. The paper matters to us because the artefact it automates, "these forty features are one concept", is exactly the level at which our own account would have to describe what a network is doing.

## What they studied
One model throughout — Gemma-2-2B — decomposed two ways: with Gemma Scope single-layer transcoder features (the main setting) and, as a generalisation test, with the model's raw MLP neurons built through a recent neuron-level attribution toolkit. Graphs come from an off-the-shelf circuit tracer at default settings with a single knob varied, the cumulative node-influence pruning threshold (0.7 for the human-comparison study, 0.4 for the task studies, the latter retaining roughly forty to eighty highest-influence nodes). The unit of analysis is the supernode: a set of features or neurons proposed as one concept, plus the text label and prose description the pipeline writes for it. Data are three prompt sets — fifteen human-annotated reference graphs shipped with the tracer demo, one hundred two-hop capital questions, and one thousand Wikipedia mid-sentence completions — plus roughly two hundred arithmetic prompts used only as a stress case.

## How they did it
Feature descriptions follow the standard automatic-interpretation recipe: five top-activating text excerpts per feature, taken from a hosted feature dashboard, supplemented with the tokens the feature most promotes and most suppresses, and a prompt that asks for a short label and a few fragments rather than sentences and that instructs the describer to prefer a specific entity over a broad category. Output-typed features are described in a "say X" form. Grouping is a three-phase conversation with one annotator model at every stage: present the fifty highest-influence feature descriptions and ask for named supernodes; present the remaining descriptions in batches of fifty to be assigned to an existing group, placed in a new group, or left ungrouped; then a refinement pass that drops irrelevant groups, merges equivalent ones and reassigns misplaced features. Every phase also receives the original prompt and the model's top three next-token predictions, and granularity is judged relative to that prompt — on a Dallas example the instruction says surrounding Southern states may merge while Texas stays its own group. Cost scales linearly with node count: about five cents on a heavily pruned graph of forty to eighty features and about sixty cents on a dense one of roughly six hundred. Validation adapts two automatic-interpretation scores to supernodes: a second model writes each supernode's description from its members' descriptions, and then either picks the matching feature out of ten candidates (one real, nine drawn from ungrouped features in the same graph) or picks the five activating excerpts out of ten. Each condition is run with five seeds, averaged per prompt and then across the fifteen prompts so each prompt weighs equally. The two-hop study checks whether a supernode exists for the intermediate hop (state or country) by stem-and-substring matching against supernode labels, with false positives confirmed and removed by hand, and then tests causality by constrained patching, suppressing and amplifying the group. Appendix-level additions: the whole validation is repeated under two other judge models, under minimum group sizes of two, three and four, and per-feature description scores are reported separately from per-supernode ones.

## What counts as an explanation here
A labelled, grouped graph: every feature carries a one-line description, features are pooled into named supernodes, and a supernode's description summarises its members. It is a partial account by construction — the grouping step sees only descriptions, the original prompt and the next-token predictions, and ignores where in the layer stack or along the prompt a feature sits, how attribution flows between features, and the backward-from-output search that a human tracer uses. It is predictive in one narrow, tested sense: suppressing a recovered intermediate-hop supernode lowers the model's probability of the right answer and amplifying it raises the probability, on prompts the model gets right and wrong alike.

## How they judged success
Three instruments. (1) **Supernode feature detection** — given a supernode description and ten candidate feature descriptions, identify the member; chance 10 per cent. (2) **Supernode text detection** — given a supernode description and ten excerpts of which five activate a member feature, identify the five; chance 50 per cent. (3) A **random-grouping baseline** formed by shuffling features within the same pruned graph, plus the **human annotation** on the fifteen reference graphs as the real comparator. Robustness: three different judge models, three minimum-group-size thresholds, the pipeline with and without its refinement pass, and a feature-level version of both detection scores. For the task study, **hop recovery out of one hundred prompts** with manual adjudication of every miss, then **constrained patching** at suppression and amplification for causal importance. For the exploration study, an **LLM judge with handcrafted interestingness criteria** filtering one thousand graphs to ten for human review.

## What they found
Automated supernodes match or slightly beat human ones on both metrics: feature detection 93.4 per cent in the full pipeline against 66.6 for the human annotations, text detection 83.2 against 81.8, with random grouping at 22.1 and 54.7. Running only on the top fifty to one hundred features — the setting whose group sizes most resemble the human groups — gives 80.8 to 83.1 feature detection, so nearly all of the advantage survives when the pipeline sees far less of the graph. The refinement pass contributes a small consistent gain, and the ordering by condition holds under all three judge models, though absolute scores drop noticeably under the strictest judge while the comparison between conditions is preserved. Grouping is conservative: adding more features to the pipeline mostly packs them into existing supernodes rather than making new ones — the top-200 setting groups 88.5 features into 8.1 groups, the full setting 207.5 into 8.3, while removing the refinement pass yields 250.1 features in 11.1 groups at slightly lower scores. On the two-hop capital questions the pipeline recovers the intermediate-hop supernode in ninety-seven of one hundred prompts; manual review of the three misses found two where the hop feature existed but was not grouped and one where it was never in the pruned graph. Patching confirms the recovered groups are load-bearing. The exploration pass, on one thousand Wikipedia completions, surfaces two graphs the authors found instructive: one where river and stream features are active on a highway prompt with no mention of water and further sampling yields a named river fork, read as planning ahead of the sampled token; and one where several award categories are active in parallel before the model commits to one. The arithmetic stress test is the paper's own counterweight: supernodes there are either surface-token groups or vague numeric-context groups, and an LLM judge classifies only 12 of 1,885 as naming an arithmetic mechanism — 65 per cent are surface-token and 29 per cent are say-number — despite the same graphs scoring 87 and 70 on the two supernode metrics. Generalisation to raw MLP neurons works but with a different grouping rate: 21.6 per cent of neurons are placed into supernodes against 60.2 per cent of transcoder features, so graphs were built with 150 neurons to make the absolute counts comparable, and some intermediate hops that transcoders surface are missing in the neuron decomposition.

## Limits and open problems they name
Single model and single transcoder suite; two task families, one of which is simple; a single annotator model; and a naive grouping procedure that throws away layer, token position and attribution flow while also ignoring the interpretable structure humans find by working backwards from the output. Human interpretation, they note, uses far more context than activating excerpts. The arithmetic appendix is presented as evidence that the grouping is too coarse to recover structure known to exist at the feature level, and the authors name tuning the pipeline toward recovering such structure as future work. They are also explicit that the method is hypothesis generation rather than verification. Not stated anywhere in the paper: per-author task division, an error analysis of the two convolutional-style failures in the hop study beyond the three counted misses, or an account of how sensitive the grouping is to the annotator model itself (only the *judge* was varied).

## What we take from it
**A warning, and it is the sharpest one in the folder for our genre.** The paper prints, in its own appendix, a case where its metric is high and its content is empty: the arithmetic supernodes score 87 and 70 on the detection metrics and name an arithmetic mechanism in 1 per cent of 1,885 groups. The two metrics reward *internal coherence* — a group whose members activate on similar text will be describable and recoverable from its own description whether or not it corresponds to a computation — so a grouping score of this family cannot be used as evidence that a grouping is mechanistic. For our account this becomes a standing obligation: any auto-generated description or grouping we report is accompanied by a **structure-recovery test against a task whose internal mechanism is independently known** (the two-hop hop, the arithmetic pathway), printed beside the coherence score, and a scoring suite whose chance levels are 10 and 50 per cent is never quoted alone. **A method to borrow:** the hop-recovery-with-manual-adjudication protocol is cheap, absolute and interpretable — ninety-seven of one hundred, with every miss inspected and classified as pipeline failure or graph failure — and is exactly the shape of measurement a predictive account needs: a specific, named internal object, a fixed question set, and a per-item verdict rather than an average.
**A framing to steal:** the three-phase grouping with a *granularity instruction tied to the prompt* (merge neighbouring states, keep the target state separate) is the smallest working example of turning a semantic judgement into a rubric, and the refinement pass that merges and reassigns is worth one point of detection score on its own while collapsing group count by a quarter.
