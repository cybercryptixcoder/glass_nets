## What they wanted, and why
Every graphical model type encodes independence structure, but no graphical type can encode *every* independence structure, and the classical fix — pick a graph and assume the distribution is faithful to it — builds a misspecification in before any data arrives. The authors want an object that represents the entire conditional-independence (CI) structure of a distribution without committing to a graph, without faithfulness, and without paying the curse of dimensionality for the privilege. The obstacle is statistical rather than notational: testing one CI relation in a general nonparametric distribution is hard, and there are exponentially many relations to get right simultaneously. Their answer is a partition of the power set of the other variables into intervals, each labelled by one minimal separating set, which they argue is both the right abstraction and the right computational device. The larger goal is a representation of dependence structure that sits between graphical models (tractable but limited) and imsets or separoids (general but intractable).

## What they studied
Abstract *independence models* — ternary relations on disjoint subsets of a finite index set — rather than any particular model family, with probabilistic CI, graph separation, gaussoids and partial correlation as instances. The unit of analysis is a single variable j together with a subset S of the remaining variables: a *relative Markov boundary* (the smallest subset of S that shields j from the rest of S) and then the collection of all subsets that share that same boundary. Nothing is trained; there is no network and no activations. The finite-sample section studies a Hölder-smooth nonparametric class and, as a special case, Gaussian models.

## How they did it
They work in the theory of compositional graphoids — an abstract independence model obeying the standard graphoid axioms plus composition — and note that every graphical model's separation relation, every regular Gaussian distribution (faithful or not) and every gaussoid is one. For a fixed variable j and scope S they define the relative Markov boundary m(j;S) as the intersection of all subsets of S that make j independent of the rest, which the intersection axiom guarantees is well defined, and prove that the sets achieving that minimum form an interval. The neighbourhood lattice is then everything with the *same* boundary: the set of U whose own relative boundary equals m(j;S). They prove this is a convex sublattice of the subset lattice, that its infimum is m(j;S) and its supremum is S plus every variable conditionally independent of j given m(j;S), and — the central result — that the distinct lattices partition the entire power set. That partition they call the neighbourhood lattice decomposition, with a t-sparse version keeping only lattices whose minimal element has size at most t. Two algorithms follow naturally: compute one lattice by learning the boundary (a Grow-Shrink adaptation) and then scanning the complement for variables independent of j given it, and compute the whole decomposition by repeatedly picking a subset no discovered lattice covers — findable in polynomial time only because the lattices are disjoint. They then replace the CI oracle with a thresholded *generalised partial correlation*: regress each variable on the conditioning set nonparametrically, take the L² inner product of the two residual streams and normalise. Finally they show that for Gaussian data the object coincides with a "regression lattice" — the sets U whose partial regression coefficients for j have the same support — and give an independent proof of the lattice property through the lattice of orthogonal projection operators on a Hilbert space, where partial orthogonality is exactly vanishing of a product of projections.

## What counts as an explanation here
A non-graphical representation of dependence: for each variable, a partition of all possible conditioning sets into intervals, each interval standing for one minimal separating set. It is complete for CI — any query "is A independent of B given C" reduces to checking set inclusions across the stored intervals — but it is not causal and contains no mechanism, no functional form and no directions in activation space. It is predictive in the strict sense that the whole CI structure can be enumerated from it and, under the stated separation condition, recovered from a finite sample with high probability.

## How they judged success
Proof first: the lattice and partition theorems, the query complexity of the algorithms in numbers of CI queries, and a high-probability recovery theorem. The statistical claim is a sample-size condition rather than a benchmark: with an α-separation margin on the generalised partial correlation, a smoothness class, and a sparsity bound t on boundary sizes, the estimated sparse decomposition equals the population one with probability at least one minus a constant times exp(−c n α²), as long as α² dominates a nonparametric estimation term plus (t+4) log d / n. Two worked specialisations are given — a Hölder class, where the estimation term becomes the usual n^(−2s/(2s+t)), and the Gaussian case, where it collapses to the parametric log n / n. The illustrative example is a 15-node graph on which they count CI queries versus the number of true relations.

## What they found
The decomposition exists for any compositional graphoid and is computed by a polynomial number of CI queries in the number of distinct lattices, so the cost tracks the complexity of the *structure*, not the size of the power set. On the 15-node example, one node's structure contains 62,592 distinct elementary CI statements out of 114,688 candidates, and all of them follow from 319 stored intervals obtained with 2,208 CI queries; the largest relative boundary for that node has size five, which they take as the sparsity of its structure. The naive sparsity-only bound on the number of intervals (the sum of binomials up to t) is shown to be conservative by roughly an order of magnitude on that example. Statistically, the effective dimension for nonparametric estimation is the boundary size t rather than the ambient dimension d, so CI structure becomes learnable when d is much larger than n at the price of a logarithmic factor in d — which the authors present as the main consequence, since recovering even a single relation in that regime is otherwise hopeless. In the graphical case the neighbourhood lattice recovers familiar objects: the boundary is just the neighbour set within S, and removing the node splits the lattice into a disjoint union over connected components. The Gaussian case is shown to be the special case where the probabilistic lattice and the regression lattice coincide, and partial orthogonality is noted as weaker than conditional independence in general.

## Limits and open problems they name
The full decomposition is exponential in the sparsity parameter, so the sparse version is only tractable when boundaries genuinely are small. The high-probability guarantee is worst-case and says nothing about the constants, and the authors say the bound on the number of intervals could be much too conservative. The CI test is a linear-correlation-based measure, so it detects only second-order dependence — the concentration result is for the generalised partial correlation, not for arbitrary conditional dependence, and they state that their results extend to any dependence measure with a similar concentration inequality, which they do not supply. The relation still needs the intersection axiom for boundaries to be well defined; composition is needed for the join, and they note that without it the object survives only as a meet semi-sublattice. They list open questions: what happens for non-compositional graphoids, sharper bounds on the number of lattices, and learning general (chain, mixed) graphical models without faithfulness.

## What we take from it
(1) A *framing we can use directly*: the honest primitive for a white-box account is not "what does direction d mean" but "what is the smallest set that shields this thing from the rest, and which other sets do the same job" — that pair (a minimal boundary plus the interval of sets indistinguishable from it) is precisely the object our own probing reports keep gesturing at when they say a feature is or is not redundant given some context. Since entry `arxiv-2310.17611` established that partial orthogonality in a d-dimensional embedding space is a compositional graphoid, this paper is the general theory of the object entry `arxiv-2310.17611` constructed a special case of, and it supplies the part that paper lacked: a compact partition plus algorithms, rather than one boundary per query. (2) A *measure and a cost model*: report the size of the boundary and of the interval beside any conditional claim, and report the number of queries the structure costs, because the whole point of the decomposition is that a small number of intervals generates an exponentially large set of relations — a claim of the form "we recovered all dependencies in this model" is a claim about intervals, and should be quoted with both counts. (3) A *warning about the test*, which is the sharpest one in this folder for our purposes: because the statistic is a normalised regression residual inner product, a variable can fail to show up in a boundary for reasons that have nothing to do with independence in a learned network — nonlinear dependence that leaves residuals uncorrelated is invisible, so "conditionally independent" read off a correlation-based test is a weaker statement than the graphoid theory licenses, and we should say which test produced the CI structure we draw, not only what the theory says about it. The projection-lattice proof also carries a lesson for our probes: the lattice structure of these intervals is inherited from the order structure of orthogonal projections, which means the interval representation is not a notational convenience but a consequence of the geometry — so a subset-lattice-shaped account of a probe's context set has a reason to exist before any experiment does.
