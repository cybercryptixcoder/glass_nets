# Preventing Gradient Attenuation in Lipschitz Constrained Convolutional Networks

- **Key:** arxiv-1911.00937
- **Authors / organisation:** Qiyang Li, Saminul Haque, Cem Anil, James Lucas, Roger Grosse, Jörn-Henrik Jacobsen; University of Toronto and Vector Institute (Li and Haque listed as equal contributors)
- **Date:** 2019-11 (NeurIPS 2019; arXiv v2 dated 09 Nov 2019)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.00937
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Enforcing a small Lipschitz constant by construction is the cleanest route to a provable robustness radius, a stable optimiser and a usable Wasserstein estimator, but the field's provable constructions were either loose (they bound the kernel by a constant multiple of something else), expensive (they need an iterative projection at every step), or unstable in training. A prior paper had diagnosed why provable Lipschitz-constrained *fully connected* ReLU networks underperform: a layer that is provably non-expanding can only shrink the backward gradient, so stacking many of them attenuates gradient norm and the network cannot use the nonlinearity it has. That paper's fix was to make every layer norm-preserving and to replace ReLU with a sorting activation, and it worked only for dense layers. This paper asks the convolutional version of the same question: can one write down a parameterisation of convolutional kernels that is exactly orthogonal as an operator, cheap enough to train at CIFAR scale, and expressive enough that the gradient-norm-preservation argument survives — and if so, does it actually beat the existing provable-robustness and Wasserstein baselines?

## What they studied
Small and Large convolutional classifiers (the "Small"/"Large" nets from Wong et al.'s convex-outer-approximation work) on MNIST and CIFAR-10 under an ℓ2 threat model, plus a fully convolutional Wasserstein critic (the DCGAN critic with batch normalisation removed) trained against a frozen STL-10 GAN generator. Unit of analysis: the **convolution operator** as a linear map, treated through its orthogonal (or nearly orthogonal) kernel, and through the singular-value spectrum of the Jacobian. Four competing ways of making a convolution 1-Lipschitz are compared on the same architecture: one-sided spectral normalisation (OSSN), reshaped-kernel orthogonalisation (RKO, the Parseval-style reshaping made exact via a differentiable orthogonalisation of the reshaped matrix), singular-value clipping and masking (SVCM), and their own block-convolution orthogonal parameterisation (BCOP).

## How they did it
They start from the published representation of 1-D orthogonal convolutions as a channel-mixing orthogonal matrix followed by a chain of kernel-size-2 factors built out of complementary symmetric projectors, and from its 2-D extension obtained by alternating horizontal and vertical factors. Their central diagnostic move is topological: since a symmetric projector is determined by its range and projectors of different rank cannot be continuously deformed into one another, they count connected components of the whole space. In 1-D they prove the space is compact with a component count linear in kernel size and channel number; in 2-D with 2×2 kernels they prove the count grows at least quadratically in channels. They then prove that a convolution over n channels built from arbitrary-rank projectors can be embedded in a convolution over 2n channels built **only** from half-rank projectors, reproducing the first n output channels from the first n input channels — so a single connected component of the enlarged space contains an image of every component. Their parameterisation therefore *chooses* to live in that component: initialise a random matrix, orthogonalise it, form a projector by multiplying it by its transpose to get exactly half rank, and stack these into the kernel. The orthogonalisation is Björck's iteration run a fixed 20 times with one series term, so "orthogonal" is up to the residual of that iteration, not exact. Architecture follows from the constraint: no residual connections (a 1-Lipschitz residual is claimed to force identity-plus-bias), no batch normalisation (scaling is not GNP), cyclic padding in place of zero padding (a short argument in the appendix says zero-padded orthogonal convolutions collapse to size 1), and invertible downsampling in place of striding and pooling. Each configuration is repeated five times with mean and standard deviation; robustness numbers are certified by the margin divided by the square root of two times the Lipschitz constant, plus four empirical attacks (PGD with CW loss and FGSM on the whole test set; Boundary and Pointwise attacks on the first hundred test points).

## What counts as an explanation here
A **parameterisation plus a topology theorem**, not a description of what the network computes. The explanation is: here is a connected, exactly-parameterised family of operators that provably cannot expand any vector, so the network's Lipschitz constant is the product of its layers' bounds and the certificate is a scalar function of the output margin. The topological result is the part that does explanatory work — it says *why* the obvious construction traps a gradient method (the optimiser is confined to the component it initialised in) and *why* paying a factor of two in channels dissolves the trap. The paper is explicit that the family it lives in is a strict subspace of all orthogonal convolutions and that a complete parameterisation is left open, so the account is knowingly partial.

## How they judged success
Provable robust accuracy against a certified ℓ2 ball (ε = 1.58 on MNIST, 36/255 on CIFAR-10) as a fraction of the full test set, clean accuracy, and four empirical attacks as checks that the certified number is not vacuous; plus 1-Wasserstein lower-bound estimates on 6,400 real/generated pairs (larger is better since the estimate is a lower bound). The methodological controls are: identical architectures across the four Lipschitz methods, a stated effort to keep every method's constraint *tight* (ten power iterations for the spectral baseline, fifty-iteration singular-value clipping every hundred steps, exact orthogonalisation for the reshaped-kernel variant), five seeds with spread, a singular-value histogram of a trained layer for each method, a frozen-convolution ablation as a sanity check that the constrained layers are doing the work, and two external comparisons whose baselines' numbers are quoted from the baselines' own tables and repositories.

## What they found
BCOP leads the same-architecture comparison on every cell of the main table: on CIFAR-10 Large it reaches 72.16 ± 0.23 clean and 58.26 ± 0.17 certified, against 70.01/55.76 for the reshaped-kernel variant, 69.65/53.61 for singular-value clipping and 67.51/53.64 for one-sided spectral normalisation; on MNIST Large 98.69/56.37 against 98.44/55.18. The singular-value histograms are offered as the mechanism: the spectral and reshaped-kernel baselines drive many singular values to zero (an operator that uses far fewer than its available directions), clipping starts them near a half and pushes some up, and BCOP holds all of them at one by construction. Against external methods the margins are much wider (CIFAR-10: 72.16/58.26 against 62.60/49.97 for the dense norm-preserving baseline and 59.76/50.60 for the convex-outer-approximation baseline), while on MNIST clean accuracy the dense baseline is nominally ahead by 0.02. In Wasserstein estimation the best estimate comes from the only configuration that preserves gradient norm in *every* layer (9.91 ± 0.11 with the sorting activation, against 8.95 for reshaped kernel and 7.39 for spectral norm with the same activation; the ordering holds with ReLU, at lower values for all three). The frozen-convolution ablation certifies 0.00 ± 0.00 robust accuracy on MNIST Large versus 56.37 trained, which localises the work in the convolutional stack. One appendix counterexample shows a 2×2 orthogonal kernel outside the family — and then shows that the counterexample is a reordering of the paper's own 1-D factors, so the incompleteness witness is mild.

## Limits and open problems they name
The parameterisation spans a strict subspace of orthogonal convolutions, and the authors say a complete one might train stronger networks; whether allowing reordering of the 1-D components (or composing several such convolutions) would cover the whole space is stated as open. The connected-component embedding costs a doubling of channels and, in their accounting, four times the parameters. Architectural price: no residual connections, no batch normalisation, cyclic padding only. The comparison is restricted to *deterministic* certification, explicitly because probabilistic smoothing gets much higher certifiable radii, so the SOTA claim is scoped. Also unexamined in the paper: the fixed twenty-step orthogonalisation is a numerical approximation that is never stress-tested, and the 2n-channel embedding (a theorem about what the family *can* represent) is invoked as the motivation for a parameterisation that in practice only ever builds full n-channel convolutions with half-rank projectors, so the theorem's guarantee and the implemented code are not the same object.

## What we take from it
- **Method (topological diagnostics before designing a parameterisation):** counting connected components of the constrained set, then asking which component the initialisation sits in, predicts a specific failure mode of gradient training — and motivates an explicit fix — without any experiment. This is the sharpest available template for "read weights, predict what training can reach": the shape of the feasible set is a property of the *model class*, checkable on paper.
- **Framing (a connectedness argument as the justification for a redundant parameterisation):** the extra factor of two in width is bought to make one component surjective onto all of them. When we design a parameterisation with a guarantee, we should ask which connected component we are in and what the guarantee excludes.
- **Method (singular-value spectrum of a trained layer as the explanation of a comparison):** the paper's account of *why* it wins is a histogram, not a table — baseline operators shrink their own spectra (lost expressiveness) while the constrained one cannot. For us this is the cheapest form of "read the weights and say what the model became".
- **Warning (the "tight constraint, same architecture" control is what makes the comparison meaningful, and it is a claim about effort, not a theorem):** each competitor's tightness is achieved by a chosen iteration count, and only the authors' method is *exact by construction*; the four-point gap on CIFAR should be read as "with these hyperparameters", the same lesson as the equal-Lipschitz collapse on the Cayley-transform paper.
- **Warning (a numeric orthogonalisation with a fixed iteration count is a hyperparameter nobody swept):** twenty Björck steps at one series term is an approximation whose error is never reported. Anything we certify as "orthogonal" should print the residual, the same way a certificate should print its abstention rate.
- **Shortcut (ablating the architecture down to the constrained part: no residuals, no batch norm, cyclic padding, invertible downsampling):** these are forced consequences of the constraint, and they are also the confounds that separate this paper's results from the earlier parametric-robustness literature; a reader who wants to attribute the gain to orthogonality must hold the architecture fixed, which is exactly what the internal comparison does and the external one does not.
