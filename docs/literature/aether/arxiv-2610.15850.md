## What they wanted, and why

The paper's starting observation is a mismatch of method and use that runs through essentially all SAE work: a latent is *found* by watching how its activation co-varies with a behaviour across natural prompts, and then it is *used* by an intervention — steering along its decoder direction, zeroing or mean-replacing its code, patching it in from another prompt. Discovery is observational; use is interventional. The authors want to know whether the first licenses a claim about the second, and they narrow "claim about the second" to a single precise quantity rather than a vague notion of importance. The goal is to say what the numerical number a discovery score puts on a latent actually measures, and — since the answer turns out to be "a different thing" — to supply the estimand that a practitioner should be reporting instead. It sits directly in the folder's line of work on whether an account of a model's internals can predict what happens when you push on them.

## What they studied

Four public model–SAE pairs, each a residual-stream SAE at one layer: GPT-2, Gemma-2-2B, Gemma-2-9B and Llama-3.1-8B, drawing on the released GPT-2 SAE set, Gemma Scope and Llama Scope. Eleven tasks: country capitals in free and multiple-choice form, country languages, birthplaces, occupations, antonyms, last letters, few-shot induction, indirect object identification, simple addition and two-digit subtraction. The unit of analysis is a single SAE latent at one hook, and the object being predicted is a scalar model output — the teacher-forced log-probability of the answer, standardised within task. Prompts are partitioned into strata by task, answer and prompt length so that activation variation within a stratum reflects the prompt and not a change of answer or length; the paper shows that pooling strata would make a probe largely a contrast between answers and lengths.

## How they did it

They define the target of the whole exercise as *intervention attribution*: for an intervention that edits the activation by some amount depending on the prompt, the attribution is the mean over prompts of the edit's inner product with the model's gradient at the activation — the first-order effect of that specific edit, averaged. This is one backward pass per prompt, the same quantity attribution patching estimates. The observational side is represented by two things: an arbitrary discovery statistic (they use a published dependence-aware feature-importance pipeline, referred to here by its initials, with per-task false-discovery control) and, as a canonical continuous analogue, a least-squares probe on a latent's final-position score, whose coefficient implies a predicted change for a given edit. Prompts are split once — a selection split fixes active latents, tests and candidates, and an evaluation split supplies every gradient, intervention and estimate. They then (a) prove a non-identification result, (b) decompose the probe's error exactly into three terms and (c) test across the four models whether the gap is large, whether attribution predicts realised steering and ablation, and whether the choice of estimate changes which latents one would pick. Steering is checked against a central finite difference; single-latent zero- and mean-ablations are checked against prompt-specific gradient predictions; held-out feature sets selected by different rules are compared on realised ablation effect. Several analyses are declared post hoc, and steering step sizes are scaled on the evaluation split, which the authors flag as making those comparisons descriptive rather than inferential.

## What counts as an explanation here

Not a description and not a dictionary: a single number with an estimand attached. The account the paper offers of a latent is "this is what a stated intervention on this latent does to this output, to first order, in this stratum", computed from one gradient pass, with a held-out interval and the terms of the decomposition printed beside it. It is explicitly partial and explicitly local — the authors say directly that whether a latent is the sole mechanism behind a behaviour, or necessary or sufficient for it, is a broader question this does not answer. The predictive content is narrow but checkable: the attribution should track realised small-step interventions and should break down predictably as the step grows.

## How they judged success

Four instruments, all against realised interventions rather than against each other: (1) the ratio of attribution magnitude to probe-coefficient magnitude for selected directions under unit steering, with intervals that resample strata within task; (2) agreement of the unit-direction attribution with the realised central difference quotient at a quarter of a within-stratum standard deviation, and the share of pairs where attribution and coefficient differ in sign from the realised effect; (3) mean absolute error and sign agreement of prompt-specific gradient predictions against realised single-latent ablations, compared with probe-based predictions; (4) held-out set ablation — select up to ten latents per task on training folds by each rule, then measure the absolute mean ablation effect on held-out prompts, with normalisation by edit norm and two-stage intervals. Stratum-level intervals use a many-strata normal approximation whose coverage they check by simulation, and are the ones they report as below nominal for the Stein-residual term.

## What they found

The gap is large and systematic. For the discovery-statistic-selected directions under unit steering, the attribution is only about three to five percent of the probe coefficient in the median across models, and the ratio is below one tenth for most pairs — the median attribution-to-coefficient ratio is reported as 4.6%, 3.2%, 2.8% and 3.2% for the four pairs. It is not an artefact of that particular selection rule: latents picked by coefficient magnitude show coefficients exceeding realised small-step steering quotients by more than two orders of magnitude in the model-specific medians. The decomposition says why — under both selections the Stein residual and the omitted-variable term are each comparable in size to the coefficient itself. Attribution does predict realised interventions: it closely matches the small-step steering quotient and is closer than the coefficient for every pair; on single-latent ablations with nonzero realised effect, gradient predictions match the realised sign in 91–97% of cases against 17–35% for probe-implied changes. Range of validity is bounded honestly: at a displacement scaled to each latent's maximum observed activation the first-order prediction still agrees in sign for nearly all pairs, but at five times that scale it fails for most pairs. And the choice matters downstream — attribution-selected sets give larger absolute mean ablation effects on held-out prompts than correlation-, rank-test- or coefficient-selected sets in every model–task unit, with intervals excluding zero.

## Limits and open problems they name

The attribution is a first-order, local, intervention-specific quantity; finite effects need the intervention evaluated at the scale of interest, and downstream nonlinearity breaks the approximation, which they state and demonstrate. The empirical coverage of the Stein residual is below nominal at the observed stratum sizes, and the theorem does not cover the percentile bootstrap intervals used for medians and shares. Only four model–SAE pairs at one layer each, eleven tasks, teacher-forced outputs, and few selected pairs for the smallest model (twenty-three). Inference assumes independence across strata, and relaxing it toward template clusters is listed as future work. The construction extends to any layer, dictionary, linear readout and intervention that keeps the SAE error term, but everything measured here is a residual-stream SAE of the kind already released.

## What we take from it

Three things, in order of use. First, a framing to adopt wholesale: separate the estimand from the statistic. "This latent matters" is not an estimand; "this stated intervention on this latent changes this output by this much" is, and the second is computable from one gradient pass. Second, a hard constraint on our own predictive account: if it predicts the effect of an intervention from an observational statistic, it will overstate that effect by one to two orders of magnitude unless it models the three terms — the slope-across-prompts residual, the mismatch between the positions a readout looks at and the positions an edit touches, and the covariance between edit size and local sensitivity. That third term is why rare-firing latents behave the way they do under ablation, and it is the term the folder has been describing qualitatively as "presence without use". Third, a cheap instrumentation the folder should copy: report the share of the mean edit that falls outside the span of observed activations. That single number is the operative version of the polytope-lens point about a direction being valid only inside a region — here it says whether the observational data contained any information about the direction being pushed. The worked example is the warning that travels furthest: the top-ranked latent by the discovery statistic barely moves the answer under ablation while the twenty-third-ranked one moves it clearly, and one backward pass anticipated both.
