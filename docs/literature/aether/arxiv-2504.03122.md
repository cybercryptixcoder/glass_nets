## What they wanted, and why
Causal discovery from observational data alone leaves a graph identified only up to its Markov equivalence class, and the experiments that would resolve the rest are the expensive part of the process — a gene knockout, a clinical manipulation, a lab run. Prior work on intervention design splits into plans committed in advance and adaptive plans re-chosen after each round, and the adaptive ones tend to optimise a single thing (usually the number of experiments), leaving out the practical side of the problem: what an experiment costs, how many variables can be perturbed at once, which combinations are simply not allowed. The authors want one planner that takes the current partial knowledge and picks the next intervention set while treating those practical limits as first-class constraints, and they argue the value of the design is that it can be re-aimed at a different objective without being rewritten. The larger goal is recovering the full DAG with as few rounds of expensive manipulation as possible.

## What they studied
Synthetic Erdős–Rényi graphs over a grid of node counts from 3 to 256 and edge probabilities from 0.05 to 0.95, 50 random graphs per setting, plus nine standard benchmark networks ranging from asia (8 nodes, 8 edges) through sachs, insurance, alarm, hailfinder, win95pts and pathfinder to andes (223 nodes) and link (724 nodes, 1125 edges). The unit of analysis is the whole graph, represented not as a CPDAG but as a *partially known graph* whose node pairs sit in four states — unknown adjacency, adjacency established but unoriented, semi-directed (edge may exist in one direction or not at all), and fully known — with a confirmed-absence state alongside them. The essential graph is a special case of this representation, which is why their state can carry background knowledge, forced edges and forbidden edges from the start.

## How they did it
Each round, an integer program is solved over binary variables saying which vertices to intervene on and which edges get tested. A pair receives an *orientation test* exactly when one endpoint is intervened on and the other is not; it receives an *adjacency test* when neither endpoint is intervened on. The objective is the plain count of edges in the three uncertain classes that the chosen intervention set subjects to a test, and the constraints are a budget mixing per-variable intervention cost and per-variable observation cost, a cap on how many variables may be perturbed simultaneously, and the linear implications that tie each test indicator to the intervention choice. After the intervention is run and the CI oracle answers, a transition table moves each tested edge to fully known, to a reverse-direction semi-directed state, to mere adjacency, or to confirmed absence; Meek's four orientation rules are then applied to exhaustion so that directions propagate without further experiments, and the loop repeats until nothing uncertain remains. All experiments start from the true essential graph rather than from a data-driven skeleton, so the study measures the orienting phase alone, with a perfect conditional-independence oracle and costless experiments for the reported runs. The baselines are random: at each round the random arm picks a subset up to the same cap uniformly, with no reallocation toward remaining ambiguity.

## What counts as an explanation here
An experiment-design policy, not a model of the system. The understanding is the integer program plus the transition table: given the current state of knowledge and the constraints, the policy names the next intervention set and predicts how many edge statuses it will touch. It is a myopic one-step planner — the objective counts tests *scheduled*, not information gained, and the paper says in its own appendix that this is a computationally feasible proxy for the theoretically cleaner average-gain-over-the-equivalence-class objective. The certificates are about the procedure's behaviour (finite termination, soundness of what gets written into the known set), not about optimality of the resulting sequence.

## How they judged success
Number of intervention rounds to full orientation and total number of variables manipulated, both reported as the *difference* between the random baseline and the adaptive planner per setting, so zero means parity and positive means the random arm needed more. Reported as distributions over seeds within each (density, cap, size) panel, with medians and interquartile ranges, and the real-network results are read against each graph's structural statistics (node count, edge count, degree moments, count of v-structures). The theory is three statements in the appendix: finite convergence of the iteration, correctness of the final known set under sufficiency, faithfulness, perfect interventions and a perfect oracle, and single-step optimality of the IP solution relative to its own stated objective.

## What they found
Across every synthetic setting the median saving is non-negative, so the planner is never worse than random on the median and often better, and the size of the saving is governed by how much work Meek's rules can do for free. In the dense regime the advantage is largest precisely when only one variable may be perturbed — a single random knockout is often uninformative while the solver repeatedly targets a hub — and it collapses as the cap rises, because almost any two perturbations then break enough cycles for propagation to finish the job. On real networks the pattern repeats and is attributed to topology: pathfinder, which has one node of degree 106, shows a median saving above forty rounds; hailfinder and insurance, with skewed degrees and moderate v-structure counts, show savings in the single digits; link, with 821 v-structures, shows essentially no median improvement because most of its edges are already oriented inside the equivalence class. The authors' summary is that a planner of this kind earns its computational cost where orientation hinges on a few critical hubs, or where only one variable can be perturbed at a time, and approaches parity when propagation alone does most of the work.

## Limits and open problems they name
Their own list: sufficiency (no unobserved confounders), faithfulness, a perfect CI oracle, perfect interventions, acyclicity and finiteness are all assumed, and relaxing them toward noisy finite data with hidden confounders is named as the future work. The objective is myopic, and they concede in the text that better objectives exist but are more expensive to evaluate. Most consequentially, the reported study barely exercises the part of the design they claim is the contribution: since intervention costs are treated as negligible, the budget constraint is dropped from the fitted model, only the cap and the uniform random baseline are compared, and the authors state plainly that no other adaptive strategy in the literature supports the same constraint set, so no such strategy was compared against.

## What we take from it
A framing and a warning. The framing is the state representation: splitting each pair of sites into unknown / adjacent / semi-directed / known, with a separate confirmed-absence status, is a cheap way to carry background knowledge, forced edges and absences through a probing loop, and the transition table is the portable part — it says exactly which test outcome moves a pair to which state, and it is auditable line by line. The warning is about what a modular IP buys and what it hides: the constraint layer is genuinely reusable (they extend it in the appendix to super-additive joint costs, to banning combinations, to batched parallel experiments and to per-round changing budgets), but the objective they actually solve is a count of scheduled tests, and the paper's own comparison set is random alone with costs switched off, so the numbers establish "better than random under a cap" and not "prices the budget". For our batteries the lesson is to keep the state machinery and import the objective, not the other way round — pick the information-gain or rival-separation objective first, then let the constraints do their work; a constraint-rich planner scored only against random tells us almost nothing about the constraints.
