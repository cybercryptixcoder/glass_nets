# Lipschitz constant estimation of Neural Networks via sparse polynomial optimization

- **Key:** arxiv-2004.08688
- **Authors / organisation:** Fabian Latorre, Paul Rolland, Volkan Cevher; EPFL, Switzerland
- **Date:** 2020-04-18
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.08688
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

Upper bounds on a network's Lipschitz constant are what make a robustness certificate mean anything — the exact constant is a supremum over an unbounded input set, so every practical claim about adversarial stability is really a claim about an upper bound. The dominant certified route in 2020 was a semidefinite program built from activation sectors, and it had a hard ceiling: it was written for the Euclidean norm, it scaled badly with width, and it produced one global number for a network that is only ever queried inside a small ball. The authors wanted a formulation where the norm is a parameter rather than a fixed choice, where the sparsity of the weight matrices is exploited instead of flattened away, and where the same machinery can be narrowed to a region of input space. They frame it as fitting the network's Lipschitz estimate into a general polynomial-optimisation hierarchy, so that the SDP of the prior work becomes one point on a designed ladder rather than the only tool.

## What they studied

Feed-forward compositions of dense and convolutional layers, restricted to activations whose derivative is continuous and bounded between zero and one — exponential linear units and softplus are the named examples, and the differentiability requirement explicitly rules out the rectifier, so every ReLU result in the paper is out of scope by assumption. The unit of analysis is the whole network as one scalar-valued function, with one output selected: the MNIST experiment estimates the constant to a single class logit rather than to the ten-way vector. The signature object is what they call the norm-gradient polynomial, a polynomial in one real variable per layer (each variable standing for the derivative of the activation at that layer's pre-activations) plus one variable per input coordinate, whose maximum over a box is the bound. Experiments use random-weight networks from forty to three hundred twenty units per layer at controlled sparsity, and one pruned MNIST classifier with two hidden layers of three hundred and one hundred units, ninety-five percent of whose weights were removed without loss of accuracy.

## How they did it

The derivation starts from the standard identity that a differentiable function's Lipschitz constant equals the supremum of its dual-norm gradient, proved in an appendix for convex open domains; for a feed-forward stack the chain rule gives a product of weight matrices interleaved with diagonal matrices of activation derivatives, so the whole gradient is a polynomial in the derivative variables once those are treated as free symbols. Bounding each derivative to the unit interval and the read-out vector to the unit dual ball yields a polynomial maximisation over a box, which is generally intractable. They then apply a positivity certificate: a value is a valid upper bound exactly when the polynomial formed by subtracting it from the objective is positive on the box, and a classical representation of positive polynomials on the cube — a weighted sum of monomials of the form coordinate-to-a-power times one-minus-coordinate-to-a-power — turns that condition into linear equalities in nonnegative weights. Truncating the certificate at degree k and minimising the bound subject to the equality gives a linear program, one per degree, with the first nontrivial degree equal to the network's depth; the sequence is non-increasing and gives a valid bound at every level. Unconstrained, this LP is enormous — they compute the weight count for a hundred-neuron one-hidden-layer MNIST net at roughly one and a half million at degree two and nine hundred thirty million at degree three — so the second half of the paper is the sparsity argument. They define a valid sparsity pattern as a partition of the variables into cliques such that the polynomial splits as a sum of pieces each living on one clique, with a running-intersection condition across cliques, then build the network's computational graph (a vertex per unit per layer, an edge wherever a weight is nonzero) and read a pattern off it: for each output unit, the clique is that unit together with every upstream unit that can reach it by a directed path. A hereditary result for such patterns then lets the positivity certificate be assembled clique by clique, which changes the LP size from a power of the total unit count to a power of the largest clique times the number of cliques. They count this for four regimes — dense, arbitrary pruning, and two-dimensional convolution — and give the first-degree SDP alternative in the same language, showing that the earlier one-hidden-layer-only SDP is just the degree-one sum-of-squares truncation of the same problem, and that the restriction to one hidden layer was never a real limit. For the local version they replace the unit bounds on the derivative variables by interval bounds obtained from a cheap forward interval pass over an input ball, then rescale each variable's interval onto the unit cube and re-run the same LPs.

## What counts as an explanation here

A numerical certificate and nothing else. The output is a scalar upper bound at a chosen hierarchy level, together with the linear program that witnesses it — the nonnegative weights in the positivity certificate are the dual object, and no interpretation of them is offered or attempted. The account is explicitly partial: every bound is an upper bound from a relaxation, so the gap to the true constant is not characterised, only observed. The one structural statement is the clique decomposition read off the weight-support graph, which is a description of the network's wiring rather than of its behaviour.

## How they judged success

Six estimators compared on the same networks: the semidefinite bound, two levels of their own linear-program hierarchy, the prior semidefinite method rescaled by the square root of the input dimension (the norm-equivalence hack they are arguing against), the naive per-layer product bound, and a sampled lower bound computed by evaluating the dual-norm gradient at fifty thousand random points near the origin. Because no true constant is available they report relative error against that sampled lower bound, averaged over ten random networks per configuration, at four widths and several sparsity levels, with computation time plotted separately for each method; the convolutional case is analysed analytically for clique size and for the arbitrary-sparsity case they concede the pattern's validity condition can fail and that a chordal completion would be needed. Solver details differ between the arms — a commercial linear-programming solver for their hierarchy, a commercial conic solver for the SDP — and their own protocol states the gap between local and global estimates as evidence that larger certified balls are available, without quantifying how much larger.

## What they found

At the lowest nontrivial degree the hierarchy is already comparable to the semidefinite bound, and one degree higher it is uniformly better across every tested configuration; on the two-hidden-layer random networks the semidefinite arm's bounds on the one-hidden-layer networks were too large to appear on the plot, as were the layer-product bounds throughout, which is the clearest single statement in the paper about how loose the trivial alternative is. Computation time for the hierarchy tracks the sparsity level while the semidefinite time tracks only the size, so at fixed sparsity the cheaper method also becomes the tighter one as networks grow — the accuracy/cost dial is a real design choice rather than a single point. On the pruned MNIST classifier the sampled lower bound, the degree-four hierarchy, the degree-three hierarchy, the semidefinite bound and the layer product read, in that order, 84.2, 88.3, 94.6, 98.8 and 691.5: their tightest bound sits a few percent above a sampled lower bound while the trivial product is eight times higher. The local bounds for shrinking radii sit well below the global bound with a visible gap at every width tested, which is the paper's argument for region-aware certification.

## Limits and open problems they name

The rectifier is excluded by the differentiability assumption, so the largest population of networks in practice is not covered — a point the paper states and does not resolve. The sparsity pattern read off the graph is only proven valid for dense networks; for pruned or randomly sparse ones the running-intersection condition may fail, in which case the bound remains valid but the hierarchy's convergence to the polynomial maximum is lost, and they report this as an empirical observation with no bound on how much quality is lost. Convolution is handled by clique counting rather than by a convolution-aware reformulation, and the two-dimensional clique estimate grows cubically in the kernel parameter. Scaling to deeper and larger networks is left open with a list of four directions: splitting into sub-networks, parallel optimisation over polynomial structure, low-memory first-order methods for the semidefinite arm, and exploiting weight-sharing symmetry to shrink the programs.

## What we take from it

The method, and the most transferable thing in the entry: **the network's weight-support graph, read backwards from each output unit, is a valid decomposition of the certificate into per-output cliques** — which makes the bound attributable to an output path without any extra machinery, and is a cheaper structural instrument than any of the per-layer slack blocks the constraint head solves for. The measure: relative error against a sampled lower bound, printed for every arm in the same table, is exactly the interval discipline this survey keeps demanding, and the 84.2-to-691.5 spread on one network is the cleanest illustration we have of why an upper bound reported alone is uninterpretable. The framing: treating the certificate hierarchy's degree as a dial and reporting the accuracy/cost curve rather than a single operating point is a template we should copy whenever we report a bound. The warning: this paper's own admissibility condition silently excludes the rectifier, so a claim of the form "certified for this network" must always be accompanied by the activation class, because a differentiability assumption can remove the entire population being measured. For our own account: the polynomial-in-derivative-variables parameterisation is the same object as the constraint head's multiplier — one free scalar per layer standing for where the activation sits in its sector — and here it is bounded by an interval derived from an input region, which is the natural handle for a prediction-under-intervention claim.
