# PLOT: Progressive Localization via Optimal Transport in Neural Causal Abstraction

- **Key:** arxiv-2605.06979
- **Authors / organisation:** Jonathn Chang, Arya Datla, Ziv Goldfeld; Cornell University
- **Date:** 2026-05-07 (v1); v2 2026-10-04
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.06979
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

Causal abstraction turns interpretation into a localization problem: if an interpretable high-level variable is really implemented by the network, then a matching internal intervention must reproduce the abstract counterfactual. Distributed alignment search (DAS) makes that operational but only after somebody proposes a site — it fixes a layer, learns a rotation there, and reports how well the rotated subspace swap agrees with the abstract swap. Sweeping the whole candidate family (every layer, every subspace size, every coordinate block) is the expensive part, and the paper's complaint is that search and fitting have been fused into one loop even though they are different problems. The goal is a way to *find* where a variable lives without gradients and without learning a rotation, so that the expensive gradient-based fitting can be aimed at a few candidate locations instead of all of them. The larger aim is a division of labour: a matching layer that proposes correspondences from behaviour alone, and an optional subspace layer that refines one. (It also fits the folder's standing interest in which parts of an interpretability pipeline are measurement and which are optimisation.)

## What they studied

Models of three sizes on three tasks. At the small end, a ReLU MLP (three hidden layers of width 16, 850 parameters) on hierarchical equality (HEQ) from the MIB family, with two binary abstract variables. In the middle, a single-layer GRUCell recurrent network with hidden size 16 on 4-bit ripple-carry addition, where the abstract variables are the internal carries C1, C2, C3 and the neural sites are the recurrent states at four timesteps. At the large end, Gemma-2-2B (26 residual layers, width 2304) on the MIB multiple-choice colour-copying benchmark, with two abstract variables (an answer-pointer index and the answer symbol), localized over final-token residual-stream activations. The unit of analysis is the *site*: a subspace inside a layer, in native coordinates, PCA coordinates, or (for the DBM baseline) SAE features — but a site is defined by an intervention handle, not by a state-to-state map.

## How they did it

1. Draw base/source input pairs and split them into three disjoint banks — fit, calibration, test — with the authors explicitly renaming the usual train/validation/test to reflect the distinct roles.
2. For every abstract variable and every candidate neural site, run the interchange intervention and record its *effect on the shared task output*, not on the internal state: for the abstract side the difference between the counterfactual and factual abstract output, for the neural side the difference between the softmax over the intervened logits and over the factual logits. Aggregate each site's per-pair effects into one signature vector.
3. Fit an entropic optimal-transport coupling between the collection of abstract signatures and the collection of neural signatures (Sinkhorn on squared Euclidean cost). The coupling matrix is a soft correspondence: each row says how much mass the variable sends to each candidate site. When the candidate family is large and contains sites expected to be irrelevant, use one-sided unbalanced OT, which pins the abstract marginal and lets the neural marginal absorb less mass, so junk sites can go unmatched.
4. Calibrate the coupling row into a handle: keep the top-K sites by mass, renormalize the weights, and apply the swap with a global strength multiplier λ, interplaying the interventions in forward-pass order when the selected sites span layers. K, λ and the entropic parameter are chosen on the calibration bank by held-out interchange-intervention accuracy — never on the test bank.
5. Progressive variant: run the coarse matching over a hierarchy of candidate families (layers or timesteps first, then coordinate blocks or PCA bands within the chosen parent), selecting the parent by calibration accuracy before refitting inside it.
6. Evaluate on the test bank, and — a deliberate departure from the benchmark they reuse — report accuracy separately on *sensitivity* pairs (the source changes the target variable) and *invariance* pairs (the source leaves it fixed), then average.
7. Optionally feed the coarse localization into DAS, so the rotation search runs only at the selected layer instead of all of them, and compare accuracy and runtime against full DAS and against masking/DBM baselines.

## What counts as an explanation here

A correspondence plus an executable intervention handle: the deliverable is a set of sites, weights, a support size and a strength multiplier per abstract variable, which applied to a base activation reproduces the abstract counterfactual on held-out pairs. It is explicitly a *handle*, not a state-to-state map — the authors are careful that high empirical interchange accuracy "does not by itself establish an exact or constructive abstraction." The account is partial by construction: it says where a variable is realized well enough to intervene, and the paper says nothing about the network's internals beyond the sites it names. It predicts something concrete and testable (the output under interchange at the named sites on unseen pairs), which is stronger than a description but weaker than a circuit.

## How they judged success

Interchange intervention accuracy (IIA) on held-out test banks as the primary metric, split into sensitivity and invariance halves and then averaged, with mean ± standard deviation over 5 seeds (MCQA, binary addition) or 10 seeds (HEQ). Baselines: DAS with a full sweep of layers and subspace sizes, DBMs in canonical, PCA and SAE representations, and masking. Ablations: OT versus cosine matching versus direct IIA matching on the fit bank (evaluation-aligned, flagged as such); one-sided UOT versus balanced OT for layer selection; three effect-signature constructions (family-wise, total-variation, KL); one-stage versus two-stage localization; native versus PCA coordinates; handle-dimension caps. Secondary metric is serial runtime, defined explicitly (signature construction, coupling/training, calibration over a stated grid, test evaluation; excludes model training) and acknowledged to understate wall-clock for methods that share initial stages.

## What they found

HEQ: both PLOT and DAS recover accurate handles, average test IIA 0.9931 ± 0.0127 for PLOT against 0.9967 ± 0.0016 for DAS, with PLOT about 47× faster in serial runtime (3.94 s vs 184.31 s) and no learned rotation at all. Binary addition: PCA coordinates beat native substantially (0.767 → 0.900 one-stage, 0.792 → 0.936 two-stage); the best gradient-free PLOT result is two-stage PCA at 0.936 ± 0.028, and PLOT-guided DAS matches full DAS exactly (0.991 ± 0.007 both) at roughly 3.6× lower runtime (15.2 s vs 54.7 s), selecting the same timesteps the exhaustive sweep found. MCQA with Gemma-2-2B: the gap between gradient-free and gradient-fitted is real but shrinking — best gradient-free handle 0.8610 ± 0.0526 (two-stage PCA), PLOT-guided DAS 0.8750 ± 0.0286, full DAS 0.8960 ± 0.0213 at roughly 20× the runtime (509.60 min vs 25.27 min); DBM PCA 0.8570 and the SAE representation 0.7560. The most striking ablation is the OT-versus-UOT layer-selection table: under balanced OT the answer-pointer variable lands on layer 6 with accuracy 0.000 on four of five seeds, because two abstract rows must spread mass over 26 layers, while one-sided UOT selects a high-scoring layer for that variable on all five. Among matching rules, KL-signature UOT is the strongest two-stage variant (0.8620) and cosine is the fastest (15.60 min) but weakest (0.8000); a direct accuracy-matched comparator reaches 0.8530, so the transport machinery is not what carries the result — the global constrained matching with a relaxed marginal is, and only by a few points. Signature choice interacts violently with staging: the KL signature is best when staged and catastrophic when not (0.5955 → 0.0085 one-stage native; 0.8380 → 0.4840 one-stage PCA).

## Limits and open problems they name

Scaling to larger models needs engineering the paper does not do (batching, activation caching, approximate signatures, distributed transport computation). The authors list sharper localization rules and finer hierarchical signature constructions as open. Their own K/λ/ε selection is a grid search on a calibration bank, so the reported accuracy is the maximum over a search — and the search budgets differ a lot between methods (thousands of calibration settings for PLOT on HEQ against a layer × dimension sweep for DAS). The MCQA experiment reuses a 210-row public benchmark by generating a synthetic 10,000-row replacement, and evaluates on exact top-1 token agreement where the benchmark's own protocol only required the answer letter to appear in a longer continuation — so their numbers are not directly comparable to MIB's published ones. Future work proposes applying the same matching to features in transcoder replacement models and attribution-graph nodes, which is explicitly not attempted.

## What we take from it

A **framing** and a **warning**. The framing: search for a correspondence and fit an intervention are two problems, and the search can be done without gradients by comparing *output effects* rather than internal states — this is the folder's cheapest concrete recipe for the held-out-localisation question, and it fits our constraint that a white-box account must be scored by what changes in the output, not by internal similarity. The warning is sharper than the result: the balanced-OT failure is a ten-line demonstration that a matching objective which must explain *all* candidate sites will assign mass to sites that do nothing, and will then look confident while being wrong (accuracy exactly 0.000, not "low"). Our batteries have the same shape whenever we force a global correspondence across a candidate set that contains irrelevant entries. Three more transfers: (i) their sensitivity/invariance split is the discipline of `arxiv-2402.17700` re-derived with a better justification — a handle that learns nothing scores perfectly on an invariance-heavy mixed set, so the mixed average hides the failure; (ii) their honest ablations show the headline mechanism is worth a few points over a naive comparator (0.8620 vs 0.8530 for direct accuracy matching), which is the correct way to publish "our mathematical machinery helps"; (iii) the KL-signature collapse under one-stage localisation is a reminder that a signature tuned alongside a search strategy is measuring the *pair*, not the signature — exactly the same lesson as the alignment-shape dependence in `arxiv-2112.02505`.
