# Grokking Can Be Steered: Selecting Desirable Solutions By Choice Of Learning Rule

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.19477

## What they wanted, and why
A circuit read out of a trained network is usually treated as a fact about the task: modular addition groks to a Fourier algorithm, and the circuit that implements it is reported as small and concentrated, so concentration looks like something the problem demands. The authors ask the prior question — is the concentration a property of the task or of the training procedure that happened to be used? Their lever is the **learning rule**, the pair (credit-assignment algorithm, optimizer), which is already known in the implicit-bias literature to select among the many parameter settings that fit the data. If choosing a different learning rule changes how many neurons implement a fixed algorithm while leaving the algorithm and the fit unchanged, then circuit size is a selection effect and every circuit number a mechanistic study reports is conditional on an untracked training choice. That is the larger goal: separate what the task requires from what the optimizer picked, so that reported circuits can be compared across runs and pruned circuits can be attributed to the right cause.

## What they studied
Unit of analysis: the **first hidden layer's neurons** and their outgoing weight norms, in a two-hidden-layer width-256 ReLU MLP trained on modular arithmetic mod 31 (one-hot concatenated operands, 40% of pairs held out, full batch) — plus credit equalization reproductions on modular subtraction and multiplication, sparse parity (20 bits, three relevant, ~2000 and ~300 training examples), permutation composition in S₅, a transformer with a wide MLP, three non-gradient-term settings (coupled decay, Adam's ε under AdamW, a trainable per-neuron gain with layer norm), and a 30M-parameter six-block GPT-2-style transformer on TinyStories (393M tokens) as a scale test. Learning rules compared: backprop+Adam as reference, credit equalization at strengths β, LARS, LAMB, RMSProp, Lion, Adafactor, Muon, Shampoo, Nero, deep feedback control (DFC), and six rules used only to test which grok at all (feedback alignment, direct feedback alignment, sign-SGD, three constructed orthogonal-component rules).

## How they did it
Algorithm and circuit are separated by definition: every rule that groks is checked to recover the same periodic Fourier computation (input frequencies used, a trigonometric fit of the logits, agreement across seeds and rules), so any difference found is implementation, not function. Concentration is measured as the **participation ratio of outgoing weight norms** across the 256 neurons of layer one, read as an effective neuron count; two activity-/effect-based measures (activation-weighted norms, and the mean absolute change in the correct-class logit when each neuron is removed) are reported alongside so the weight-only measure is not the sole evidence. The core intervention is **credit equalization**: the incoming-weight gradient of each neuron is rescaled toward the layer mean magnitude with strength β, direction preserved, before it enters Adam — β = 0 is ordinary training, β = 1 makes every non-zero neuron gradient the same length. Comparisons share data split, initialization, loss, optimizer and hyperparameters, and the effect is measured both when training from scratch and when switched on **after** grokking. A group-size sweep replaces the per-neuron factor with one factor per group of m neurons (m = 1, 2, 4, 8, … n) to locate which granularity matters, and the eight optimizers are then run under the same protocol to see whether the ones that normalize per neuron sort out as predicted. Counter-controls constrain incoming weight norms instead of credit, sweep learning rate × weight decay, and use dropout; a linear diagonal model is worked through analytically (Propositions 1–5) and the per-neuron condition is tested by rescaling only the non-gradient term of each neuron's update while holding its credit/decay ratio fixed. Consequences are then measured three ways: random, norm-ranked and greedy neuron deletion; magnitude and greedy pruning; and fine-tuning from the addition network to subtraction, multiplication, and readout-only targets, with the from-scratch time as the reference.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **neuron-level circuit plus a mechanistic account of its size**, and specifically a one-line causal condition rather than a description: a neuron stays in use when its credit is large relative to the non-gradient term of its own update. The claim is not a decomposition of the computation (the algorithm is assumed and verified, not explained) but a decomposition of the *variance in implementation* — which training-rule property decides how the computation is spread. It predicts: change that ratio per neuron and the circuit redistributes; equalize the ratio away and it stays concentrated; and the resulting circuit, not the rule that made it, predicts deletion tolerance and adaptation speed. Partial by design — it says nothing about which algorithm is learned, and the paper is explicit that the theory isolates the mechanism only in a simplified linear model.

## How they judged success
Circuit participation (n·PR as effective neurons) at matched training loss, margin, parameter norm and test accuracy; algorithm identity verified independently by a Fourier fit that classifies held-out pairs; two non-weight concentration measures (activation-weighted participation, ablation participation); the β sweep as a dose-response; the **group-size sweep** as the discriminating test (per-neuron factor vs groups of 2/4/8+); eight optimizers run under one protocol, with credit participation reported against circuit participation; the "ratio held" control (equalize gradients while preserving each neuron's credit-to-decay ratio) as a falsification arm; deletion tolerance as normalized area under test-accuracy-vs-deleted-fraction (0 to 0.8) plus greedy deletions to 0.5 accuracy; pruning to 0.9 and adaptation epochs to 0.9; and a weight-norm-in-null-directions statistic q separating grokking from memorizing rules across nine configurations × three seeds.

## What they found
Reference backprop+Adam groks to ~154 of 256 effective neurons (participation 0.60), with ~84 neurons nearly unused (outgoing norm below a tenth of the layer mean). Raising β to 1 lifts participation to 0.99 (~254 effective), test accuracy only 0.998 → 0.976, with training loss, margin and parameter norm within a few percent; at width 2048 the reference uses 332 effective neurons against 2046 equalized. The intervention also **reorganizes an already-grokked** network: participation rises above 0.95 within 300 epochs of switching, test accuracy stays above 0.98, and a control continuing with the reference rule stays at 0.60. Granularity is decisive: groups of eight or more stay within ~0.02 of the reference's 0.60, groups of four reach 0.70, pairs 0.75, and only single-neuron factors reach 0.99 — because equalizing group means leaves within-group credit ratios intact. Of eight optimizers only Nero (per-neuron normalization) distributes (0.986 at 0.985 test); the other seven land in 0.470–0.613, including Lion and Adam-mini, which equalize *update norms* but normalize the decay together with the gradient and so leave the ratio unchanged. The one condition explains three previously separate routes to dead units — coupled weight decay, Adam's ε, and a decayed per-neuron gain — and rescaling only that non-gradient term per neuron reproduces the redistribution (0.993, 0.981, 0.997) while holding the ratio keeps the circuit concentrated (0.574, 0.818, 0.343). Distributed circuits tolerate deletion better under all three procedures (random 0.770 vs 0.674, norm-ranked 0.581 vs 0.332, 61 vs 23 greedy removals); concentrated circuits prune further (62% vs 20% by magnitude) and adapt from addition to subtraction in 640 epochs against 1540 distributed and 1410 from scratch — about two-thirds of that advantage comes from the nearly unused neurons, and it vanishes for multiplication, whose Fourier basis differs. At scale, per-unit decay balancing in the 30M transformer cuts nearly unused MLP units from 23.9% to 1.3% with lower validation loss in every seed (AdamW sits at 0.9%). Rules that grok are separated from memorizing rules without overlap by the null-direction weight norm q (5.3–8.5 vs 26–135).

## Limits and open problems they name
The theory is a simplified diagonal linear model without adaptive normalization, and it covers only rescalings that preserve update directions; the non-linear and optimizer results are empirical. Under AdamW with no decay on normalization parameters the effect is small (0.91–0.96) and **not** explained by the condition, and for Adam's ε holding the per-neuron ratio fixed removes only about half the effect. Algorithm checks cover modular addition only. The adaptation advantage appears only for targets that need new hidden combinations of the operand frequencies, not for readout-only changes. Robustness is tested only as neuron deletion. Code is promised on acceptance, not yet public.

## What we take from it
A **warning** first, and it is a strong one for our probe battery: a sparsity or circuit-size number read off a trained checkpoint is a statement about the training run as much as about the task, so any "N neurons/features implement this" claim we publish should name the optimizer, where weight decay enters the update, and whether the non-gradient term was normalized per unit. A **measure**: participation ratio on outgoing norms, reported as an effective count with the activity-weighted and ablation versions beside it, is a cheap, weight-only circuit-size statistic that moves monotonically with a controlled intervention and agrees with effect-based measures — exactly the sort of quantity our white-box model should predict rather than discover post hoc. A **framing** worth importing wholesale: describe a learned implementation at two levels, algorithm (verified by a functional identity check) and circuit (spread across units), and treat a change in the second with the first held fixed as the object of study — our predictive model wants to say not just *what* computation a layer performs but *how many units it is spread over*, and this is a case where that number is causally traceable to a single per-unit term. A **shortcut** for cheap causal work: switching the intervention on after convergence redistributes the circuit within 300 epochs at unchanged accuracy, so implementation-level questions do not require training from scratch. And a **design lesson**: groups of eight or more behave like no intervention at all, so any groupwise normalization or regularisation we apply while trying to test per-unit hypotheses will silently test nothing.
