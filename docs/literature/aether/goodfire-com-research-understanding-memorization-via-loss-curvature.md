# Understanding Memorization via Loss Curvature

- **Key:** goodfire-com-research-understanding-memorization-via-loss-curvature
- **Authors / organisation:** Jack Merullo, Srihita Vatsavaya, Lucius Bushnaq, Owen Lewis (Goodfire); post written by Siri Vatsavaya and Michael Byun; Goodfire
- **Date:** 2025-11-06
- **Link:** https://www.goodfire.com/research/understanding-memorization-via-loss-curvature/
- **Kind:** blog/research post (summarising an arXiv paper; the paper itself was not read here)
- **Read from:** full blog post (all sections; figures not inspected)
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Language models reproduce large stretches of their training data, and the field has almost no account of where that content lives in the weights, whether it is separable from the machinery that does general computation, or how much of a given capability rests on it. They want a *basis for weight space* in which "this is memorised" and "this is computation" are different coordinates, so that the question stops being "which parameters store Harry Potter" and becomes "which directions in this matrix are used by many examples and which by one". The larger goal is a diagnostic that needs no labelled forget-set: something you can point at a trained model and some unlabelled text and get a decomposition of its weights by how widely each component is exercised. They also want a capability-by-capability map of how much each task leans on the memorisation end of that basis, which is a claim about which mechanisms sit where rather than a claim about any single example.

## What they studied
Pretrained language models and vision transformers; the post names Llama 3.1 70B as the motivating recitation example but does not enumerate the full model set, sizes or layer counts, which live in the linked paper and its ablations. The unit of analysis is a **direction in the space of a weight matrix**, derived from second-order loss statistics over a dataset, not a neuron, head or feature. Data used for the edit is unlabelled text sampled from the model's own training corpus; evaluation uses memorised sequences the edit was and was not fitted on (Dolma validation sequences versus historical quotations the statistical pass never saw), a suite of downstream tasks spanning reasoning, arithmetic and closed-book QA, and a ViT trained with ten percent random label noise to force memorisation.

## How they did it
The method is deliberately close to PCA with the objective swapped: instead of directions of maximum variance in activations, find directions along which the *loss* responds most. Forward and backward passes over a random sample of training sequences yield activation and gradient statistics; a K-FAC approximation turns those into a curvature estimate over weight space; the curvature eigenvectors give a new basis in which each weight matrix is re-expressed; and the low-curvature components are zeroed. So the edit is a projection-and-truncate on a fixed basis computed once, not an optimisation loop — they stress there are no trainable mask parameters, though a small set of known memorised sequences is used to choose which layers to edit and how hard to truncate. The intellectual pivot is what curvature means at *dataset* level rather than per-example: prior work shows a single memorised example has a sharp, brittle loss, but when the loss is averaged over many examples the relation inverts, since a direction that only one example depends on barely moves the average, while directions underpinning attention, composition or reasoning move it a lot. Hence moderate curvature marks shared computation, near-zero curvature marks rarely-used patterns including memorised content. Comparisons are against BalancedSubnet, a state-of-the-art unlearning method that learns a parameter mask to raise loss on a forget set while holding performance on clean data.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **basis, plus a decomposition of every weight matrix into widely-used and narrowly-used parts**. The explanation is not a story about a datapoint and not a feature dictionary over activations; it is an assertion that the weight tensor factorises into a component whose removal is felt across the corpus and a residue whose removal is felt on few examples, together with a claim that the residue is where memorisation lives. It is partial by construction — a global spectral summary, not a per-memory address — and it does make predictions: truncating the low-curvature end should impair recitation broadly, generalise to memorised sequences held out of the statistical pass, and leave reasoning intact. The authors are explicit that memorisation need not reside in a particular layer or head; what it has is a *location in the curvature spectrum*, which is a reusable coordinate rather than an anatomical one.

## How they judged success
Three kinds of test. (a) **Held-out recitation**: memorised sequences from Dolma validation (shared with the statistical pass) versus historical quotations (never seen by either method), with the unseen-quote gap used as evidence that the two methods find memorisation through different mechanisms. (b) **Downstream capability sweep**: accuracy on reasoning, arithmetic and QA tasks before and after the edit, reported as a spectrum rather than a single average, plus a qualitative check on whether completions stay coherent and how perplexity on clean text moves. (c) **A synthetic memorisation control**: a ViT trained on ten percent random labels, where a generalisable rule cannot exist, so any accurate recovery of those labels must be memorisation; they measure the recitation rate on noised labels, the accidental recovery of the true labels, and validation accuracy, all before and after editing. The baseline throughout is BalancedSubnet, chosen because it is targeted-and-optimised in contrast to their untargeted-and-closed-form edit.

## What they found
In the ViT control, recitation of the noised labels falls from 81.6% to 3.5% while the model recovers the *correct* label on those same examples 66.5% of the time (up from 10.5% before the edit), and validation accuracy rises from 67% to 71.7% — the truncation behaves like regularisation, removing overfit structure and letting the underlying rule show through. In the language models the K-FAC edit suppresses recitation of memorised quotations that neither method was trained on far better than BalancedSubnet, which corrupts non-forget sequences and manages untargeted memorisation only inconsistently; edited models stay more coherent and closer to the original perplexity. Across tasks the dependence forms a spectrum: tasks that are pure memorisation collapse to 3–16% of original accuracy, logical reasoning is unaffected or slightly better, and arithmetic and closed-book factual recall sit in between and pay a real price. Their GSM8K example is diagnostically clean: the reasoning chain survives intact and the failure is confined to the final arithmetic step, so the benchmark loss is an arithmetic loss wearing a reasoning-benchmark label. Factual recall is the same shape — "Paris is the capital of France" is not verbatim sequence memory, but it is a narrow, specialised pattern and behaves like one under truncation.

## Limits and open problems they name
The edit is coarse: with no per-memory targeting it can be heavy-handed, and some memorised examples survive it. The method is only "unsupervised" in a qualified sense — hyperparameters such as where to edit and how aggressively are still picked using a small set of known memorised data, though they expect dataset statistics alone might suffice later. Everything is at the level of a weight-matrix basis, so nothing localises a particular memory; the finding is that memories share a curvature region, not that they share a place. The post is a summary of a paper, and the ablations across model families and the account of how curvature patterns emerge over training are not reproduced in it. Open directions they list: make the edit finer-grained and then retrain memorisation-heavy-but-desirable skills back in; train arithmetic to rest on sturdier mechanisms; modulate memorisation at inference time; ask whether there is an optimal amount of memorisation per task; and place reasoning-heavy advanced mathematics on the spectrum using more capable models.

## What we take from it
- **A framing we can steal almost directly.** Our goal is a white-box model of a black-box network, and this paper insists on a *basis* for a weight-derived object whose coordinates have an operational meaning ("widely used" versus "narrowly used"), then predicts what intervention on that basis will do. That is the template: choose a basis, name what each axis predicts about an intervention, and let the held-out-probe test adjudicate.
- **A measure with a built-in control.** The inverted-curvature argument generalises beyond memorisation: an averaged loss is insensitive to components only a few inputs depend on, so a per-example sharpness statistic and a dataset-level statistic of the same quantity can point in opposite directions. Any time we compute a per-input reading and then average, we should state which level the claim lives on.
- **The held-out-generalisation test as the discriminator.** Two methods both claimed to remove memorisation; the one that also removed sequences it had never been fitted on was doing something mechanistic, and the one that only hit its own forget set was doing something local. Our probes need the same distinction: a probe that only predicts its own fitting distribution has not earned a mechanism claim.
- **A warning about benchmark labels.** GSM8K looked like a reasoning failure and was an arithmetic failure; the reasoning was intact throughout. Their per-step inspection is the move — a composite benchmark score can hide that the component that broke is not the one the benchmark is named after.
- **The regularisation surprise.** Removing low-curvature structure improved validation accuracy in the noisy-label ViT. Deleting a component of a representation is not automatically a cost; a white-box model should be able to predict when removal helps, and this is a concrete case where the naive prediction is wrong.
- **A method sketch worth reusing:** closed-form basis from second-order statistics, then truncate — no optimiser, no learned mask. Cheap, and the cheapness is what makes the capability sweep affordable.
