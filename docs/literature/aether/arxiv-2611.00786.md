## What they wanted, and why
Circuit discovery writes a network's computation as a graph: nodes are units, edges are pairwise influences, and a mechanism is a subgraph. The author argues that this is the wrong shape for the phenomenon interpretability keeps meeting — a feature spread over a group of units, and a unit participating in several features. Their motivating observation is causal rather than anatomical: suppressing several members of a group at once can move the representation in a way that suppressing them one at a time does not, and no edge can carry that. Hypergraphs would be the general fix but the search space is unusable (testing every pair of disjoint subsets of N neurons is on the order of 3^N), so they take the tracial subclass where a group of predictors acts on one target — simplices — and build a filtration so that the *order* in which relations appear is itself part of the description. The larger goal is a description of what a trained network does for a given input distribution that is (a) higher-order, (b) context-dependent by construction, and (c) checkable against a network whose computation is known.

## What they studied
Small feedforward ReLU MLPs on a synthetic four-class task: points in [−3, 3]² classified by quadrant. Two architectures — a [2, 4, 4, 4] net whose four first-layer units each detect one sign of one coordinate (the authors call it *monosemantic*) and a [2, 3, 4, 4] net whose three-unit bottleneck forces each first-layer unit to mix both coordinates (*polysemantic*) — each in three versions: handcrafted weights with an exactly known computation, trained weights (Adam, lr 0.005, batch 256, 200 epochs × 10,000 draws; accuracy 0.997 and 0.999 on a 1,000-point evaluation set), and randomly initialised controls (accuracy 0.243–0.258, near the 0.25 chance level, three seeds). Seven to eight internal neurons per net. The unit of analysis is a **directed simplex whose vertices are internal neurons and whose target is one further neuron**, with a scalar date attached: the amount of predictor activation that must be removed before the target's change becomes statistically detectable.

## How they did it
1. **Activation data, then no further gradient reading.** For an input set X they record the internal activation vector a(x) for every x ∈ X. Nothing else about the network enters except the weights, which are used to propagate trajectories.
2. **Input contexts, deliberately constructed.** One-hot sets X_Q for each quadrant collect inputs whose output direction is within a tolerance η of a one-hot target vector (η = 0.01 in the main experiments); *two-hot* sets collect inputs on the boundary between two adjacent quadrants, where the network is undecided; a random set is 128 unselected points. This is the paper's most consequential design choice: the complex is defined *relative to an input distribution*, and the same network yields markedly different complexes for one-hot, two-hot and random inputs.
3. **Coherent counterfactual ablation (CCA).** Zeroing activations puts the state off the manifold of reachable states, so instead the author moves the *input* along a path that drives a chosen predictor set P to zero activation. At each step the minimal-norm input velocity achieving the prescribed rate of decrease of the active predictors is the pseudoinverse J_P⁺ applied to a direction that reduces all still-active predictors at equal speed, which avoids the easiest-to-kill predictor dominating. Where the activation map is not differentiable (ReLU region boundaries) a Jacobian from the B-subdifferential is chosen, kept only if it is self-consistent with the region it enters. The trajectory is reparameterised by the fraction s of predictor activation removed, so s = 0 is the factual state and s = 1 the fully ablated one.
4. **Relevance test.** For each target n_q, the change in its activation along the trajectory, pooled over X, is tested against a target-specific margin ε_q = ε · max(std of that target's activations, 0.05 × mean std over neurons), using a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the Hodges–Lehmann pseudomedian. The null is therefore "the effect is smaller than this scale-free margin", not "the effect is zero".
5. **Inductive construction with multiple-testing control.** At dimension d each admitted (d−1)-simplex is extended by each candidate target; candidates are admitted only if p ≤ c_d at *full* ablation, where c_d comes from Bonferroni (α = 0.01) over the family tested at that dimension; then the filtration value is the *last persistent crossing* of the cutoff along the ablation axis, taking the maximum with the filtration values of the faces so a simplex can never precede a face. Stop when a dimension admits nothing. Reported configuration: B = 128 inputs, ε = 0.01, α = 0.01, Bonferroni, K = 1000 ablation values, Δt = 0.01.
6. **A distance between descriptions.** Two complexes on the same vertex set are compared by integrating the Jaccard distance of their sublevel sets over the filtration, vertices excluded (they are present at every scale and would otherwise pull all distances down).
7. **Validation against the known computation, then against four circuit-discovery methods** (ACDC; EAP; EAP-IG with KL; EAP-IG with logit difference) applied to the first→second-layer connections, each keeping as many connections as ACDC kept on the same input set (three to ten), with corrupted inputs made by negating a random non-empty subset of coordinates.
8. **A sweep before the headline numbers.** 560 combinations of classifier × quadrant × sample size B × margin ε were computed once at the finest ablation grid, from which 20,160 complexes follow across test level, correction and spacing; plus sweeps over integration step and input tolerance.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **context-conditioned topological object, not a mechanism and not a predictor**: a filtered directed simplicial complex over internal neurons, in which a simplex asserts that the joint suppression of its predictor set moves its target, and its filtration value says how much of that suppression is needed. The author is explicit that it describes the network's *functional response to a distribution of inputs* rather than its wiring: edges may run backwards (from second to first hidden layer) and within a layer, because moving the input can move anything, and circuit methods can only ever express the forward-adjacent subset (40% of edges in the monosemantic net, 33–50% in the polysemantic one). Dimension is intended to mean the width over which a feature is spread, but the paper concedes that its test only asks whether the *joint* ablation has an effect, and never compares that effect with the sum of the individual ones — so an admitted higher-dimension simplex is compatible with the predictors acting independently. The closest it comes to an explanation of a known machine is the handcrafted arm, where each simplex's filtration value is derived by hand from the geometry of the ablation path.

## How they judged success
Four instruments, in increasing strictness. (i) **Recovery in handcrafted nets**: does the complex agree with the analytically derived one across the four quadrants up to relabelling (the monosemantic net is symmetric under 90° rotation, so the four complexes must coincide, and this is used as a consistency check of the construction). (ii) **Separation**: does a two-hot complex sit closer to the two complexes it mixes than to the other two, with a reported *separation margin*; and does the Jaccard distance relate to an ordinary distance between mean activations (rank correlation over the six input-set pairs). (iii) **Size and dimension against trained and random controls**: trained versus untrained complexes of the same architecture on the same inputs, with size, maximum dimension and per-seed spread as the read-outs. (iv) **Agreement with circuit discovery**: recall of forward-adjacent edges and AUC of each method's score for membership in the DSC, against a random-selection probability computed for the same number of connections (0.002–0.12 depending on input set). Stability is judged by a parameter sweep: same simplex set, and no reordering of filtration values.

## What they found
- **The construction recovers the designed computation in the handcrafted nets.** Monosemantic: every one-hot complex has 10 edges and 4 two-simplices, identical across quadrants after relabelling; the four earliest edges pair the active first-layer units with the active second-layer unit in *both* directions (the backward edge is correct in kind — you cannot switch off x+y without reducing x and y), and the two-simplices mark moves into adjacent quadrants. Even the AND gate is only partly visible: n₅ needs both n₁ and n₄, but because n₁→n₄ has no effect at any ablation depth the joint relation cannot be admitted, and the conjunction shows up as two edges with no simplex over the pair.
- **The polysemantic net's complexes follow its bottleneck.** Each active first-layer unit is connected in both directions to its copy in layer two; Q₄'s complex contains every simplex of Q₁'s and Q₃'s plus four edges out of the biased "none-of-these" unit n₇; the filtration separates immediate wiring (below 0.01) from second-order consequences (0.31 to Q₁, 0.59 to Q₂ from n₂/n₅).
- **Mixing is detected, but only in the handcrafted nets.** Two-hot complexes are nearest to their own two constituents in 4/4 pairs for both handcrafted nets (minimum separation +0.16 monosemantic, +0.22 polysemantic) and in **3/4** for each trained net, where the minimum separation turns *negative* (−0.15 and −0.037). A two-hot complex is also not the union of its two one-hot complexes (distance 0.62–0.68 and 0.49–0.54 to the union).
- **Size and dimension do not show that structure was learned.** Handcrafted complexes top out at dimension 2 and 13–23 simplices; trained ones reach dimension 3–4 with 24 to 365 simplices. But randomly initialised nets produce complexes of comparable size — for [2, 4, 4, 4] five of twelve untrained complexes are *larger* than the trained one on the same inputs (table: trained Q₂ 220 against untrained 370, 419, 8 across three seeds; untrained ranges per quadrant run 8–419), and three of the 24 untrained complexes reach dimension 4. Two untrained complexes contain only vertices.
- **The 1-skeleton agrees with circuit methods exactly where they can agree.** In 26 of 32 comparisons the method kept every forward-adjacent DSC edge (EAP-IG with logit difference 8/8, ACDC and EAP-IG with KL 7/8, EAP 4/8); the AUC of the methods' scores for edge membership averages 0.86 with a minimum of 0.65. The disagreements are mostly the DSC's extra reach, not conflict: of 103 kept connections the DSC lacks, 68 leave a first-layer unit that is inactive on the whole one-hot set (and so cannot be ablated), 16 leave a unit active on at most a fifth of it.
- **The method is insensitive to most knobs and sensitive to the input distribution.** Every relevance margin from 0.001 to 0.2 gave the *same* simplex set at B = 512; ε = 0 did not, and complexes kept growing with sample size there (28–90 simplices vs 6–16), which the author traces to the zero-inflation of the change distribution and a power argument — the test needs at least ~29% of targets to move beyond the margin, and at that rate half-power needs about 70 inputs rather than the minimum of 13 set by 2^−B. All four multiple-testing corrections, including none, returned the same simplex sets (Bonferroni chosen for dependence-robustness), as did all four test levels at B = 512. The random-input complex of the monosemantic net is *empty of edges* because the per-quadrant edges are diluted; in the polysemantic net it contains Q₁'s complex as a subcomplex yet sits 0.71 away from it, purely because the shared simplices enter at 0.97 instead of 0.31.
- **Where the statistical machinery bites, it bites on trained nets.** In the trained [2, 3, 4, 4] net Q₃, Bonferroni removes 6 edges around n₆ (p between 9.1×10⁻⁴ and 5.4×10⁻³, under α = 0.01 but over the cutoff 2.4×10⁻⁴) and, because a simplex needs all its faces, this also removes 206 further candidates untested; 21 of 1,475 tested candidates are such rejections. In the trained nets 92 of 1,184 admitted simplices cross the cutoff more than once along the ablation axis, so the filtration value is defined by the last crossing; 20 candidates pass at an intermediate depth but not at full ablation and are therefore not admitted. No handcrafted complex depends on the correction.

## Limits and open problems they name
Two motivating questions remain open, by the author's own account. Whether the complexes identify polysemantic neurons: in the polysemantic net the unit belonging to the most maximal simplices is n₇, the biased none-of-these detector, at 14 across the four complexes against two to seven for each of the six genuinely mixed units — so membership count does not pick out the mixed units. And whether higher-dimension simplices record higher-order interactions: the relevance test compares the joint ablation with *nothing*, and because a simplex is only tested once its faces are admitted, a joint effect between predictors that have no edge between them is structurally invisible. Scope is small ReLU MLPs on a synthetic task with known references; candidate count grows as O(N^(k+1)), which caps network size and simplex dimension; the multiple-testing correction is applied per dimension, so error is not controlled jointly across dimensions, input sets and networks; and because the CCA is built from the local Jacobian, it can fail to continue at a ReLU boundary even where a fully ablated state exists elsewhere on the manifold. Next steps are larger networks and natural data.

**Limits I would add, marked as mine:** the "polysemantic" and "monosemantic" labels are relative to the choice of elementary features — the author says so in a footnote, since a 45° rotation of the input basis exchanges the readings — so the ground truth the method is scored against is a choice, not a fact. The random-init control is the paper's most damaging number and it is reported as a size comparison only, because the Jaccard distance matches simplices by neuron index and cannot compare nets with independent parameters; a structure-level null (does the *filtration order* of an untrained net match its own inputs' geometry?) is therefore not run. Only one non-trivial network is used per architecture, with no seed variation on the trained side. And the input-set dependence they embrace is also the sharpest threat to the claim that filtration values mean anything: the same simplex at 0.31 in one context and 0.97 in another is a property of the distribution at least as much as of the network.

## What we take from it
- **Framing — the on-manifold ablation is the right default, and it is a design choice with a measurable consequence.** Suppressing activations directly leaves the set of reachable states and deforms a distributed code beyond recognition; suppressing *through the input* keeps every intermediate state a state the network could actually be in, which is why backward and within-layer relations are admissible at all. For our interventions this argues that every patching measurement we report should say which of the two it is, because a graph of off-manifold edits and a graph of on-manifold edits are descriptions of different objects. The construction also makes the cost explicit: the pseudoinverse solution is the minimum-norm input motion for a prescribed predictor velocity, so the "distance travelled in input space" is a free by-product and a natural second axis besides the fraction of activation removed.
- **Measure — a filtration turns "is there a link" into "how deeply must I intervene", and the pilot study behind it is the transferable part.** The B/ε/α/K sweep is exactly the kind of up-front identifiability work this folder has been demanding: at B = 512 every margin from 0.001 to 0.2 gives the same simplex set, the four corrections give the same sets, and the design floor comes from arithmetic on the smallest attainable p-value (2^−B) together with a power argument about zero-inflated change distributions. Anyone running a thresholded intervention battery can reuse the two inequalities directly: B ≥ log₂(m_d/α) for a dimension to be non-empty at all, and the ~29% fraction of responsive targets needed for the test to behave.
- **Warning — a network's description can be bigger, richer and less learned than its untrained control's, and the field's costliest habit is quoting size.** Untrained MLPs here reach higher dimension than the handcrafted reference and, on the same inputs, produce complexes larger than the trained nets' in five of twelve cases, with a per-seed spread from 8 to 419 simplices. This is the same defect as a feature dictionary scoring as well on a random model, and it means that any statistic of a complex — number of simplices, maximum dimension, membership count — must be printed against an init control *on the same inputs*, which the paper does and which costs three seeds.
- **Warning — the ground-truth arm is where a method looks best, and the trained arm is where it fails, so both must be reported.** The complexes recover the designed pathway perfectly and two-hot separation cleanly in the handcrafted nets, and separation goes negative on the trained nets; the author reports both. Read against this folder's transfer and distillation entries, the general pattern is that a structural description validated on a machine whose computation we chose is validated on the one case where we already know the answer — and the interesting question is whether it predicts anything on the machine we do not.
- **Warning — an input distribution is part of the estimand.** The random-input complex contains the Q₁ complex as a subcomplex and still sits 0.71 away from it because the shared simplices enter nine times later; the monosemantic random complex has no edges at all. A diffusion of responses across contexts is what that looks like — specific links survive, their depths do not — and it is the cheapest available demonstration that a context-free description of a network is not merely noisier than a context-specific one, it is a different object.
