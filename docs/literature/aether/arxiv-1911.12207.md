# Orthogonal Convolutional Neural Networks

- **Key:** arxiv-1911.12207
- **Authors / organisation:** Jiayun Wang, Yubei Chen, Rudrasis Chakraborty, Stella X. Yu; UC Berkeley / ICSI
- **Date:** 2019-11 (arXiv v3, 2020-04)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1911.12207
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (main paper plus supplementary sections A–E present in the HTML rendering)
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
They start from two complaints about trained CNNs: filters in deeper layers become strongly correlated, so a lot of the capacity is doing nothing new, and each layer, read as one linear operator, has a badly uneven gain curve, which is the multiplicative source of exploding and vanishing gradients. The remedy they want is not a new architecture but a regulariser that makes a convolutional layer behave like an orthogonal map, so that energy passes through each layer unchanged. The paper's real argument is that the orthogonality people were enforcing — on the reshaped kernel matrix — is the wrong object, and that the right object is the operator the layer actually applies to a flattened image. The larger goal is stability and feature diversity at no parameter cost, and they test the claim across classification, semi-supervised learning, retrieval, inpainting, generation and black-box attack cost.

## What they studied
Convolutional layers of standard backbones: ResNet18/34/50 and WideResNet28 on CIFAR-100, ResNet34 on ImageNet, AutoGAN's discriminator/generator on CIFAR-10, a deep-image-prior network for inpainting, and metric-learning and retrieval setups on CUB-200 and Cars196. The unit of analysis is the layer as a linear operator — the kernel viewed as a doubly block-Toeplitz (DBT) matrix mapping the flattened input image to the flattened output feature map — and the spectrum of that matrix is what they plot.

## How they did it
They write convolution as a matrix–vector product where the input and output tensors are left intact and the kernel is expanded into its DBT form, whose rows are individual filters placed at individual output positions. Orthogonality of that large sparse matrix is then checked only over the locally overlapping filter patches: rows at output positions further apart than the kernel width cannot interact, so the condition reduces to a self-convolution of the kernel with itself, padded and strided to isolate the overlapping region, equal to a tensor that is the identity in its centre block and zero elsewhere. Columns are handled the mirror way, using the transposed kernel on impulse inputs, with a special-case form for stride 1. They prove (and reproduce the proof in the supplement) that the row and column versions of this loss differ by an additive constant, so a single regulariser works whether the DBT matrix is wide or tall. The loss is added softly to the task loss with weight λ, keeping the architecture and the parameter count unchanged. They compare against a plain baseline, kernel-orthogonality regularisation and, on ImageNet, a hard Stiefel-manifold method and a spectral-restricted-isometry penalty, then run a spectrum analysis and a filter-similarity analysis on the trained networks, plus a λ sweep and a wall-clock table.

## What counts as an explanation here
A diagnostic story backed by spectra: the regulariser's effect is explained by the singular-value curve of the layer operator becoming flat (the paper says it "almost always stays at 1") and by the histogram of pairwise filter correlations shifting left and widening. The understanding is a property of the trained weights — where each layer sits in the space of linear operators — rather than a circuit, a feature dictionary or a surrogate model. It is partial by construction and explicitly probabilistic in practice, since the constraint is a penalty and not a parameterisation.

## How they judged success
Downstream task metrics: top-1 accuracy on CIFAR-100 for three ResNet widths and WideResNet28; top-1/top-5 error on ImageNet; top-1 accuracy against fraction of labelled data for semi-supervised CIFAR-100; kNN top-1/top-5 on CUB-200; PSNR on eleven standard inpainting images; Inception Score and FID for the GAN; black-box-attack time and query count to reach a 90% attack success rate; NMI/F1/Recall@k on Cars196. Mechanistic measures: the spectrum of one conv layer's DBT matrix (a 64×128×3×3 kernel at 16×16 spatial resolution), the off-diagonal histogram of the correlation matrix of guided back-propagation patterns per layer, and a network-dissection count of concept-aligned channels (mIoU at least 0.04 — the "unique detectors" criterion of that framework). Controls are the plain and kernel-orthogonal baselines; there are no seed-error bars except on the GAN numbers, and no ablation of the regulariser's own padding/stride implementation choices.

## What they found
The conv-orthogonality loss beats both baselines on every task reported. CIFAR-100 top-1 rises from 75.3 to 78.1 on ResNet18, 76.7 to 78.7 on ResNet34 and 78.5 to 79.5 on ResNet50, with the kernel-orthogonality regulariser landing between (76.5/77.5/78.8); WideResNet28 goes 77.0/79.3/80.1 for baseline/kernel/conv. On ImageNet with ResNet34, top-1 error falls from 26.70 to 25.87 and top-5 from 8.58 to 7.89, against 26.88 for the hard Stiefel method, 26.68 for kernel orthogonality and 26.10 for the spectral-isometry penalty. Semi-supervised gains sit at roughly 2.4–3.3 points over baseline across 10–80% labelled data and stay ahead of kernel orthogonality at every fraction. Retrieval on CUB-200 goes 25.1→27.0 (top-1) and 39.4→42.3 (top-5). Inpainting PSNR on the eleven-image table improves or ties on nearly every image against deep image prior with and without kernel orthogonality. GAN: IS 8.63 against 8.32 and FID 11.75 against 13.01. Attack cost rises about sevenfold in time and 1.7× in queries to reach a 90% success rate. The spectrum analysis shows the plain model's singular values falling off fast, kernel orthogonality slowing the fall, and conv orthogonality holding them near 1; filter correlation grows with depth in the plain model and is pushed down and spread out by the regulariser. The λ sweep peaks at 0.1, which is *not* the value used in the ImageNet experiments (0.01). Training cost rises 9% per epoch against 3% for kernel orthogonality; parameters and test time are unchanged.

## Limits and open problems they name
The constraint is soft: they get "near row-orthogonal" convolutions and never report the residual after training, so the spectrum claim is read off one layer's figure rather than measured as a distance from the orthogonal set. The robustness experiment measures attack cost at a fixed attack, not a certified radius, and the paper concedes the longer attack time may simply be the attacker working harder. The λ used in the main classification tables is not the λ their own sweep says is best, and nothing explains the mismatch. Semi-supervised comparisons are against the baseline only in the gains row, and no seed variation is reported outside the GAN numbers.

## What we take from it
A method and a warning. The method is the useful one for us: the honest object to constrain or to measure is the operator the layer applies, not the weight tensor, and the paper gives a cheap closed form for the operator's Gram matrix in terms of the kernel alone (self-convolution over the overlapping support), which is exactly the kind of quantity a weight-reading account can compute per layer on a trained checkpoint. The warning is the head's recurring one in a milder form than the parameterised papers: "orthogonality" here is a penalty, so the claim "this layer is orthogonal" is a claim about a residual nobody printed, and the mechanism is illustrated with one layer's spectrum rather than tested across layers. It also gives us a second sighting of a pattern worth copying — the paper's own λ sweep disagrees with the λ it used for its headline table.
