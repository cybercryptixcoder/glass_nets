# Combining Causal Models for More Accurate Abstractions of Neural Networks

- **Key:** arxiv-2503.11429
- **Authors / organisation:** Theodora-Mara Pîslar (University of Amsterdam; Pr(Ai)²R Group), Sara Magliacane, Atticus Geiger
- **Date:** 2025-03-14 (v1; published as CLeaR 2025, PMLR 275:114-138)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.11429
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

Causal abstraction gives a crisp definition of when a network implements an algorithm, and interchange intervention accuracy (IIA) gives a number for how well a proposed abstraction holds. The authors start from the observation that the number is usually mediocre, and that a mediocre number is not a mild verdict but an unreadable one: at 70% agreement, is the hypothesis mostly right, or is it an artefact that happens to survive on average? Their answer is not a better fitting method but a **different object**: let the hypothesis itself change with the input. A network need not move between computational stages in synchronised steps across its whole input space, so a single fixed high-level causal model is the wrong shape of hypothesis. The paper defines an operation that combines several simple high-level models into one whose intermediate mechanisms switch on and off depending on the input, and then chooses the switch regions by measurement. The second half of the idea is the trade-off that makes this an argument rather than a trick: a model with no internal structure is trivially faithful and explains nothing, so faithfulness alone rewards emptiness, and any framework that reports only faithfulness is scoring hypotheses on the wrong axis.

## What they studied

Two toy tasks on **GPT-2-small** (12 layers, width 768), fine-tuned to solve each task exactly, and the unit of analysis is the **intermediate variable of a high-level causal model aligned to a linear subspace of the residual stream**. (1) Arithmetic: prompt `X + Y + Z =` with X, Y, Z in 1..10, 1,000 distinct inputs. Five hypotheses are enumerated as high-level models — nothing summed yet (one variable per input, three models), one pair summed first (three models), all summed at once (the "trivial" single-intermediate model) — each with its own intermediate variable P. (2) Boolean logic: prompt `OP1 (OP2 (X) B OP3 (Y)) =` with two operators from {not, identity}, AND/OR, and boolean X, Y — 64 distinct inputs. Two algorithmic families are enumerated and broken into per-variable states: the direct evaluation order, and a De Morgan rewrite in which the outer operator is pushed onto the inner subexpressions (giving variables for the negated conjunction, the negated operands and their applicators). Alignments are found with Distributed Alignment Search through pyvene, one intervenable model per candidate variable per layer per subspace size.

## How they did it

1. Fine-tune GPT-2-small as a sequence classifier on each task (same hyperparameters for both, 2,560 training examples, 50 epochs, learning rate 2e-5).
2. For every candidate high-level model and every layer, train an intervenable model that searches for an orthogonal rotation aligning that model's intermediate variable to a k-dimensional subspace of the block output, optimising the DAS loss — cross-entropy between the network's output under a *distributed interchange intervention* on that subspace and the abstract model's output under the corresponding abstract swap. Subspace size sweep k ∈ {64, 128, 256} for all models, plus {4, 8, 16, 32} for two models; 216 intervenable models per causal model in the arithmetic task.
3. Score each alignment by IIA over pairs of inputs: the fraction of (base, source) pairs where the network's output under the subspace swap equals the abstract model's output under the abstract swap.
4. Define the **combine** operation: given several high-level models with matching inputs and outputs, a combined model has all their intermediate variables, and each intermediate mechanism is active exactly when the input lies in that model's partition cell, and undefined (null) otherwise. From a combined model, build **evaluation graphs** — one weighted graph per candidate model, nodes = inputs, edges = 1 when base→source and source→base interchanges both succeed, 0.5 when only one direction succeeds, and no edge when both fail.
5. Partition greedily: sort each graph's nodes by degree, add nodes to a subgraph while the subgraph's average edge weight stays above a faithfulness threshold λ, take the model whose subgraph is largest as the winner for those inputs, remove them, and repeat until the input space is exhausted or no model clears the threshold. Everything unassigned falls to the trivial model.
6. Sweep λ ∈ {1, 0.95, 0.9, 0.8} and report, for each threshold, the **strength** of the resulting combined model — the fraction of inputs assigned to a model with internal structure — against the strength each individual model achieves alone at that same threshold.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **causal model that is a piecewise function of the input**: a set of high-level candidates plus a partition of the input space saying which candidate's mechanism is in force where. It is a hypothesis about the network's *computational state as a function of input*, and its form is a causal model with conditionally activated intermediate variables, not a description, a dictionary or a circuit. It is explicitly partial and explicitly parameterised by the reader's tolerance: at λ = 1 it claims an exact abstraction on a subset, at lower λ it claims approximate abstraction over a larger subset. It predicts the outcome of interchange interventions on inputs in each cell, and it predicts that the same variable will not be found outside its cell.

## How they judged success

Two quantities, defined in the paper rather than inherited: **faithfulness** = IIA of the combined model's variable within its assigned cell, and **strength** = proportion of the input space not assigned to the trivial (structureless) model. The comparison is per threshold, combined versus uncombined, on the same layer and the same alignment subspace, so the only thing that varies is whether the hypothesis is allowed to switch with the input. Layer sweeps at 256 dimensions provide the motivation (where in depth each state lives), and the two tasks are reported separately. No held-out split is used: the evaluation graphs are computed over the full input space of each task and the partitions are fitted to those same graphs.

## What they found

Layer sweep, arithmetic: early layers (roughly through layer 4) match the "nothing summed" hypotheses, late layers (layer 11) match only the fully-summed model, and between them the partial-summation models are intermediate — the network's transition is gradual, not a sharp switch, which is the empirical reason a single fixed model reads as mediocre. The trivial all-summed model is perfect at every layer, so a 256-dimensional subspace of the block output carries the causal effect of inputs on outputs at all depths. The asymmetry among the three partial-summation models is read as an artefact of causal attention: when the model processes Y it can see X but not Z. At the chosen layers (layer 7 for arithmetic, layer 10 for boolean), the combined models are stronger at every faithfulness level for the pairs of hypotheses it makes sense to combine, and the full combination is strongest overall at the highest thresholds; at λ = 0.8 all variants converge to full strength, so the gain is concentrated exactly at the high-faithfulness end where the single models are worst. Boolean logic: combining a partial variable with a more complete one lifts the exactly-faithful region from at most half the input space for either model alone to three quarters for the pair, and one specific combination covers most of the space at full faithfulness where the parts cover less than half.

## Limits and open problems they name

The authors call both tasks proofs of concept, deliberately simple enough to enumerate candidate algorithms by hand, and name extension to complex tasks, integration with other interpretability methods, and better optimisation for the partition search as future work. The greedy partition is a heuristic with no optimality claim, and the paper does not compare it against an exhaustive or learned partition.

Limits the entry adds. First, the number of candidate models is small and hand-written: the arithmetic task has exactly five states and the boolean task two algorithm families, so "combined models beat single models" is measured inside a space the authors chose, and the trivial model is included as a candidate in every sweep — which is what makes strength meaningful, but also means the reported strength is partly a statement about the candidate set. Second, there is no held-out partition: evaluation graphs are computed over the entire input space and the greedy assignment is fitted to those same graphs, so the strength-at-threshold figures are in-sample by construction, and the entry's own check for whether approx-abstraction generalises to unseen inputs is absent. Third, each combined model simply reuses the alignments learned for the uncombined parts and never refits after combining, so the reported faithfulness is a lower bound shaped by alignments fitted under the wrong hypothesis. Fourth, both tasks let the model use a single intermediate variable per hypothesis, so "computational state" here is a choice among one-variable models rather than a test of how several variables interact inside a cell.

## What we take from it

A **framing** and a **measure**, both cheap. (1) The two-axis report is the transferable instrument: **every account we fit is published as (faithfulness, strength) at a stated tolerance**, never as a single accuracy, because a structureless account scores perfectly on the axis everyone reports. This is the same requirement `arxiv-2605.02234` reaches from the other side — its partition of the input space and its residual bucket are the strength axis in different clothing — and the most useful thing the two papers do together is give a name to the failure that the folder's middling scores keep exhibiting: an aggregate that mixes a trivially-true region with a genuinely-informative one. (2) The **evaluation graph** is a better object than a scalar battery average and costs nothing extra, because it is just the same intervention pairs laid out as a weighted graph over inputs — its edge weights are 1, 0.5 or 0 depending on whether the swap works in both directions, and the graph's density is the aggregate, so printing the graph is strictly more informative than printing the density. That bidirectional edge rule is a third independent arrival at the "score the swap both ways" discipline (`arxiv-2402.17700`'s Cause/Iso, `arxiv-2605.02234`'s interchange-consistent pairs). (3) A warning to keep: this paper's partitions are in-sample and its alignments are never refit, so its numbers demonstrate a hypothesis *shape*, not a validated account; for our batteries the corresponding rule is **fit the partition on one split of inputs and report strength-at-tolerance on another**, which is exactly the step the diagnosis paper adds and this one lacks. (4) The "network in different states for different inputs" idea is worth importing at the level of prompt families: our batteries should ask, for each account, which regions of the input space it claims (formats, templates, turn types, entities) and check that the claim holds outside the region it was fitted on.
