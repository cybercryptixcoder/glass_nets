# Palmprint Decomposer: Interpretable and Editable Factorization via Synthetic Learning and Instance-Wise Refinement

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.20913
- **Authors / organisation:** Licheng Yan, Yuqi Wang, Jingtao Wang, Bob Zhang, Shuyi Li (PAMI Research Group, University of Macau); Andrew Beng Jin Teoh (Yonsei University); Lu Leng (Nanchang Hangkong University); Ziyuan Yang (Sichuan University)
- **Date:** 2026-11-16
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20913
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Palmprint recognition models extract discriminative texture but never say how that texture sits on the palm's own colour and lighting, so there is no explicit handle for analysis or editing. The authors want an image-level factorisation of a single palmprint into four parts that can be changed one at a time and recombined by a written-down rule. The larger goal is an interpretable representation whose parts mean something outside the network — an editable physical story about how the observed image was formed. Their own framing is that reconstructing an image well does not prove the parts are separated, which makes the paper as much about disambiguating a factorisation as about producing one.

## What they studied
128×128 RGB palmprint regions of interest from seven public databases covering contact, contactless, mobile and multispectral capture (IITD, Tongji, PolyU, PolyU contact-free, a mobile set, XJTU-UP with four phone/illumination conditions, CASIA at six spectral bands), evaluated as fifteen dataset or subset groups; no real images are used for training. The unit of analysis is four image factors plus one scalar: a colour map, a light map, a structure map, and a global texture-strength scalar. "Interpretable" here means pixels and one number, not neurons or directions.

## How they did it
The composition rule is fixed and multiplicative: the base appearance is the colour map times one plus the light map, the texture modulation is the scalar times the structure map, and the image is the base appearance attenuated by the modulation, elementwise. Training data is generated by sampling those four factors directly — base colour and low-frequency lighting from a handful of low-order cosine modes, structure from multi-scale random curve families converted into a directional crease response — and composing them, so every training image arrives with its true factors as labels. A shared encoder feeds two deliberately asymmetric decoders: the base-appearance decoder works at half resolution and drops the finest skip connection so that fine creases cannot reach it, while the structure decoder keeps every skip and stays at full resolution. Four losses are summed: direct supervision of all four factors, image reconstruction through the composition rule, a consistency term that makes the predicted colour and light agree across paired synthetic images that differ only in texture, and a lighting term that refuses a nearly uniform light map when the input's own brightness varies. For real palmprints a per-image refinement then optimises the factors themselves through the same fixed rule, using low-resolution residual fields for colour and light (so refinement cannot sneak texture into the base), full resolution for structure, a scalar for strength, a reconstruction term and a directional-response overlap penalty between base appearance and input. Refinement is 100 optimiser steps per image on top of a single network forward pass.

## What counts as an explanation here
An explicit multiplicative decomposition into four editable maps plus a scalar, with a named composition rule that anyone can recompute. It is complete in structure (the whole image is accounted for by the rule) and deliberately interpretable at the level of pixels and one global number, so it predicts exactly the image it was fitted to and lets a user predict the effect of editing any one factor by hand. It makes no claim about a learned mechanism — the network is only a fast initialiser, and the interesting object is the factor set plus the rule.

## How they judged success
Three axes, each with its own metric. Reconstruction fidelity: PSNR, SSIM, a perceptual distance, and a texture-similarity score between reconstruction and input computed with a multi-order texture code. Separation: the same texture code comparing each factor — colour, light, and their product — against the *input*, so a lower score means less input-specific palmprint texture survived in the base appearance, read against the dataset's own same-identity and different-identity similarity levels as reference points. Preservation: equal error rate and a decidability index from genuine-versus-impostor distributions of texture scores computed on the structure map (and on the reconstruction), reported as deltas against the original images. Baselines are classical smoothing, a structure-from-texture decomposition, a crease-response map and a Vase–Osher-style oscillatory component, plus four ablations of their own design (no crease-response texture, full-resolution base decoder, no consistency loss, no lighting loss) and two refinement ablations (steps, overlap term). Editability is not benchmarked numerically in the main text; it is shown as a figure and, in the supplement, as cross-dataset factor exchange scored by histogram correlation to the donor appearance and texture similarity to the donor texture.

## What they found
Reconstruction across the fifteen groups lands at PSNR 42.62–59.39 dB with SSIM 0.974–0.999 and reconstruction-to-input texture similarity 0.96–0.99, after refinement but from a network that never saw a real palm. Base-appearance texture similarity to the input sits at 0.54–0.58 in thirteen of fifteen groups, close to the databases' different-identity similarities (0.54–0.56) and well below same-identity ones, which is the paper's evidence that the base appearance has stopped carrying identity. Verification on the structure map is close to the original images: equal error rate improves by 0.22 and 0.33 points on Tongji and the mobile set and on five of six spectral subsets, and degrades on one phone/illumination condition (up 0.68 points, decidability down 0.57). Against baselines on IITD and XJTU-UP, their base appearance retains far less input texture than smoothing or structure-from-texture (0.521 versus 0.910 and 0.788 on IITD; 0.516 versus 0.903 and 0.764 on XJTU-UP) at comparable texture verification, while their base appearance's intensity histogram agrees with the input *less* than the baselines' does. Ablations behave as the story predicts: restoring the full-resolution base decoder raises retained texture despite better SSIM, removing the consistency loss raises it from 0.51 to 0.66 on PolyU, and removing the lighting constraint pushes retained texture into the colour map (0.49→0.57 on IITD). Refinement is responsible for almost all of the reconstruction quality — PSNR climbs from 38.45 to 41.80 dB within twenty steps on IITD and 31.13 to 41.98 dB on PolyU — and it costs a small rise in retained base texture; dropping the overlap term would buy more reconstruction (54.12 versus 48.38 dB on PolyU) at the price of more leak. Factor exchange over forty-two ordered dataset pairs averages 0.71 histogram correlation to the appearance donor and 0.87 texture similarity to the texture donor across roughly 243,000 exchanged images.

## Limits and open problems they name
The composition rule is offered as a simple approximation and the authors name two consequences. On a phone/flash condition the separation and reconstruction both degrade, which they attribute to the gap between their formation model and real texture–appearance interactions. Refinement trades separation for reconstruction: retained texture in the base appearance rises after refinement on both datasets tested, and its effect on texture verification flips sign between the two datasets. Their own conclusion flags more expressive factor representations and formation models as future work. Not named but visible: the factor labels are the authors' own sampling process, so the network is being graded on reproducing a synthetic generator, and there is no ground-truth factor annotation on any real palm.

## What we take from it
Two usable instruments and one warning. (1) **A separation metric defined against the input rather than against a label**: "how much of the input's own identity-bearing texture is still inside the component that is supposed to be free of it" is computable for us with whatever similarity function the downstream task already uses, and needs no ground-truth decomposition — it needs only a reference pair of similarity levels (same-item versus different-item) to read the number against. That is directly applicable to any claim that a read-out has been stripped of a nuisance variable: report the nuisance's similarity to the raw input, beside the same and different-condition baselines. (2) **Ambiguity is removed by construction, not by loss weighting**: dropping the finest skip from one decoder path, and limiting per-image refinement to low-resolution residual fields, are architectural statements that a component *cannot* carry fine detail — and both ablations show the leak reappearing the moment the restriction is lifted. For us the analogue is a probe or read-out whose receptive path is restricted by design and whose restriction is ablated, rather than a penalty term whose weight is tuned. (3) **The warning is about the validation subject**: fidelity here is fidelity to a synthetic generator whose factors are the supervision, so the strongest numbers in the paper are about reproducing the authors' own sampling assumptions, and the only real-image evidence is indirect (similarity levels, verification deltas). Whenever we validate a read-out against labels our own pipeline produced, the same substitution applies and should be stated in the same breath as the number.
