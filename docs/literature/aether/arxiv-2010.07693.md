# Linking average- and worst-case perturbation robustness via class selectivity and dimensionality

- **Key:** arxiv-2010.07693
- **Authors / organisation:** Matthew L. Leavitt (Facebook AI Research, Menlo Park; work performed under the Facebook AI Residency), Ari S. Morcos (Facebook AI Research, Menlo Park)
- **Date:** 2021-03-29 (v2; v1 2020-10-14)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.07693
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

The workshop version of this work (already in this folder) established that a network's mean class selectivity can be turned up and down during training by a differentiable penalty, and that the two settings lose on one family of perturbations and win on another. This is the extended version, and its ambition is to explain *why* the two families disagree rather than only to record that they do. The organising idea is that the semantic content of a representation has a sparsity of its own, distinct from weight or activation sparsity: a network can pack class-discriminative meaning into a few sharply tuned units or smear it across many weakly tuned ones, and class selectivity is the handle that makes this measurable and controllable. The authors then ask whether that choice is the mechanism connecting the two robustness regimes, and they answer with two instruments borrowed from different traditions — the input–output Jacobian from sensitivity analysis, and principal-component dimensionality from the geometry literature — plus a within-unit, within-layer variability statistic that they introduce. The result is a coherent three-link story: selectivity sets dimensionality in early layers, dimensionality sets how many ways a perturbation can change the code, and that number sets which kind of perturbation is dangerous.

## What they studied

Three residual classifiers: ResNet18 and ResNet50 on Tiny ImageNet (64x64 inputs, with the first max-pool removed for that size), and ResNet20 on CIFAR10. The unit is a single channel at a ReLU — a sample's response is the mean over the whole feature map — and its class selectivity index is the gap between the largest class-conditional mean activation and the mean over the remaining classes, divided by their sum, in [0, 1]. The layer-level quantities are: the input–output Jacobian L2 norm; the coefficient of variation of the input-unit gradient, computed both across samples for one unit and across units within a layer; and the fraction of a layer's dimensions needed to explain 95% of activation variance (with 90% and 99% as replications), measured on clean activations and on clean-minus-perturbed difference matrices. Benchmarks: Tiny ImageNetC (15 corruption types, 5 severities), CIFAR10C (19 types, 5 severities), FGSM and PGD in the input-L-infinity ball at 16/255, PGD training as an adversarial-robustness intervention, AugMix as a corruption-robustness intervention, and ImageNet-A as a check on "natural" adversarial examples. The final logit layer is excluded from the regulariser and from every analysis.

## How they did it

The selectivity index is made differentiable and subtracted from the cross-entropy loss with a signed scale, averaged within a layer first and across layers second so that the channel-rich deep layers do not dominate. Negative scales push the network toward distributed semantic codes, positive scales toward sparse ones. Each hyperparameter setting gets 20 seeds (5 for ResNet50), every reported number is read off the epoch with the best clean validation accuracy, and error bars are bootstrapped 95% intervals. Corruption robustness is mean accuracy over all types and severities, plus a normalised version dividing by clean accuracy so a robustness gain cannot be a disguised clean-accuracy gain. Adversarial robustness is read off accuracy-versus-epsilon and accuracy-versus-steps curves. The Jacobian is computed as the L2 norm of the full input–output derivative. For the variability statistic, the input-unit gradient norm is computed per unit per image, its mean and standard deviation across images give a per-unit coefficient of variation, and the spread of those per-unit means gives a per-layer coefficient of variation. Dimensionality is PCA on each layer's activation matrix with a variance threshold, and separately the nearest-neighbour intrinsic-dimension estimator of Facco and colleagues (Levina–Bickel lineage) for the non-linear version. Two later experiments run the causal arrow in the opposite direction: AugMix training (a known corruption-robustness method) and PGD training (a known adversarial-robustness method), each checked for its side effect on mean selectivity. Gradient masking is ruled out by generating attacks with an unregularised replicate network and testing the regularised ones on those samples.

## What counts as an explanation here

A causal chain in geometry and statistics, not in features: a one-parameter property of units (selectivity) is manipulated during training, and the effect on robustness is explained by three intervening measurements — early-layer representation dimensionality, dimensionality of the perturbation-induced change, and variability of per-unit perturbability. Each link is a correlation over trained networks of a sweep, and the two strongest links are backed by a bidirectionality test (the robustness method moves the property back). Nothing here is a dictionary, a circuit, or a claim about what any unit means semantically; the paper is explicit that the difference-matrix dimensionality says how the perturbation acted rather than what the network computes. It is a partial explanation that predicts the *direction* of a trade-off from a mean selectivity number and predicts the layer where vulnerability is decided (early), but predicts no behaviour on a particular input.

## How they judged success

Metrics: mean corrupted accuracy and corrupted-over-clean normalised accuracy; per-corruption-type accuracy; adversarial accuracy curves over epsilon and over PGD steps; L2 input–output Jacobian norm; per-unit and per-layer coefficients of variation of the input-unit gradient; fraction-of-dimensionality per layer at three variance thresholds, linear and intrinsic; ImageNet-A accuracy on the 74 classes shared with Tiny ImageNet (2,957 downsampled images). Baselines and controls: the unregularised network at the same recipe is the reference for every sweep; clean-accuracy normalisation controls for the baseline-accuracy confound (with an explicit floor-effect caveat); 20 seeds with bootstrapped intervals; replication across three model/dataset pairs; attacks generated by an unregularised replicate as the gradient-masking control; PGD-training dead-unit counts removed before computing mean selectivity; and a three-way pre-registered prediction before the corruption sweep, where the authors say in advance whether selectivity should help average-case robustness, hurt it, or be orthogonal, and on what grounds. Oddly for so careful a paper, no statistical test is reported anywhere: every cell is a mean with an interval and the ordering is judged by eye.

## What they found

Average-case: lowering selectivity raises mean corrupted accuracy, peaking near a regularisation scale of −2.0 at 17% against 13.5% unregularised on Tiny ImageNetC with ResNet18 — a 3.5 percentage-point gain — while raising selectivity does nothing until about +1.0 and then costs accuracy. Lowering selectivity helped in all 15 Tiny ImageNetC corruption types for ResNet18, 14 of 15 for ResNet50, and 14 of 19 CIFAR10C types for ResNet20. Worst-case: the ordering reverses, with accuracy falling monotonically as selectivity rises under both FGSM and PGD in ResNet18 and ResNet50 at high perturbation strengths, and ResNet18's Jacobian norm is inversely proportional to selectivity (same direction in ResNet20 and ResNet50). Perturbability variability runs with selectivity rather than against it: high-selectivity networks show larger coefficient of variation both for a single unit across images and across units in a layer. Dimensionality: clean representation dimensionality is inversely proportional to selectivity in early ResNet18 layers up to about layer 9 and across all of ResNet20; change dimensionality is higher in low-selectivity networks for both perturbation families; corruption-induced change is more high-dimensional than the clean representation itself; and adversarial change is far more high-dimensional in early layers than corruption-induced change. Both robustness interventions move selectivity the way the regulariser does, so the relationships are bidirectional. Regularisation does not move ImageNet-A accuracy at all, for either model — the one benchmark where the knob is inert.

## Limits and open problems they name

The paper is explicit that its scope is CNNs on image classification and that it is unsure how far this generalises to other tasks. It calls scaling the selectivity penalty to datasets with many classes an open problem and defers to the earlier paper for that discussion. The mechanism attribution for the higher-dimensionality-of-adversarial-change claim is presented as intuition that targeted projection is happening, not as a measured projection. There is an unreported interaction the authors do not discuss: the gradient-masking control shows high-selectivity networks are *more* robust to attacks transferred from unregularised replicates, which is a transferability claim and should be reconciled with the single-network story (the paper asserts masking is not the explanation without quantifying what remains). Practical worries are named in passing — PGD training kills units, and dead units have selectivity zero by definition, so the mean-selectivity increase from adversarial training only becomes monotonic after they are removed from the denominator.

## What we take from it

Four things. (1) **The two-sided robustness battery is now supplied with a mechanism, and the mechanism is a dimensionality read-out** — which is the cheapest geometric statistic we can compute on any activation stack, needs no labels and no fit, and here carries the whole causal story. For our batteries this argues for reporting the fraction-of-dimensionality of (a) the clean representation and (b) the clean-minus-intervened difference matrix, per layer, on at least two variance thresholds, because the same direction of claim was made to hold at 90/95/99 per cent here. (2) **The coefficient of variation of a per-unit gradient is a ready-made instrument for "how reliably does this unit participate?"** — it turns a determinism question into a dispersion number, and its pair (across samples, across units) separates two failure modes our own read-outs conflate: a unit that participates erratically and a layer where participation is unevenly distributed. (3) **Bidirectionality as a check on a causal story**: instead of only running their intervention and reading the outcome, the authors take two independently motivated training methods, ask whether each moves the property back, and treat the answer as evidence the relation is not an artefact of one recipe. Our intervention reports should do the same whenever a known method exists that targets the same downstream quantity. (4) **The inert-benchmark finding is a reportable datum**: a knob that reorganises corruption and adversarial accuracy while leaving natural adversarial examples untouched is evidence that benchmark sensitivity is structured, not monotone in perturbation strength.
