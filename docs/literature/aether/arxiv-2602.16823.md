## What they wanted, and why

Circuit discovery produces subgraphs that supposedly carry a behaviour, but the claim that the "circuit computes the behaviour" is usually checked on finitely many inputs, and the value assigned to the patched components is usually a constant nobody defends. The authors' motivation is that a small perturbation can dissolve the agreement, so a circuit reported as 90%-faithful on a sample may be unfaithful almost everywhere — an awkward footing for using circuits in safety work. Their goal is to move circuit discovery onto the same footing as adversarial robustness verification: instead of testing faithfulness at points, certify it over a whole continuous region, with a machine-checkable proof. This is the first attempt in the folder to attach mathematical guarantees rather than empirical scores to a circuit, and the first to bring formal-explainability machinery (sufficient reasons, minimal hitting sets) into mechanistic interpretability.

## What they studied

Small vision and control networks taken from the neural-network-verification literature, at a granularity chosen per architecture: a fully connected MNIST network with 31 hidden neurons, a GTSRB CNN with 48 filters (16 + 32), a VNN-COMP ResNet2b for CIFAR-10 with 72 filters, and the NASA TaxiNet runway-regression CNN with 8 filters. The unit of analysis is a subgraph of the computation graph — nodes and edges at the chosen granularity — treated as a function candidate, with everything outside it either fixed to a constant (zero or mean) or replaced by activations transplanted from a reference input. There is no language model in the paper; the object is the circuit as a formal set plus a faithfulness predicate.

## How they did it

The building block is a siamese encoding. To certify input robustness, the circuit is duplicated and stacked with the full network behind a shared input layer, the duplicate's non-circuit components are pinned to a constant, and the output constraint requires the two logit vectors to stay within tolerance across an entire ℓ∞ ball around the input — one verification query returns a proof over the whole neighbourhood rather than a verdict on one point. To certify patching robustness, the two branches instead get disjoint input domains: the full network runs on a reference input, and its non-circuit activations are wired into the circuit branch, with the patching input ranging over its own continuous ball. A third encoding stacks three branches so input and patching robustness can be certified in one query. Discovery itself is ordinary greedy elimination — start from the whole graph, try removing a component, keep the removal only if the predicate still holds — with the certified predicate substituted for the sampled one; an exhaustive variant keeps making passes until no removal is possible, and a binary-search variant inherited from earlier work removes prefixes to get a weaker guarantee in logarithmic queries. For minimality they add a hitting-set construction: a "blocking set" is a subgraph whose removal breaks every faithful circuit, and under a monotone predicate the minimum hitting set of all blocking sets is a cardinally minimal circuit, so they enumerate candidate blocking sets of size at most three, compute the hitting set, and test whether it certifies.

## What counts as an explanation here

A subgraph plus a certificate. The explanation is a set of components and a proof that, across a specified continuous domain and under a specified range of patching values, running the subgraph and running the whole network agree to within tolerance. It is partial by construction and it is predictive in a precise but narrow sense: it predicts the model's logits on any input in the certified region, and it predicts the model's logits when the excluded components are replaced by any activation the patching domain admits. What it does not do is say what any component means, or why the remaining set is the set it is.

## How they judged success

The primary metric is certified robustness — the fraction of circuits for which a verification query confirms the bound holds everywhere in the region — reported against a sampling-based baseline that is judged on exactly the same metric afterward. Circuit size is reported alongside so robustness is not bought by simply keeping more components. They sweep the neighbourhood radius and the tolerance, use three different output metrics (gold-class logit difference, winner-class consistency against half the original margin, and a bound on the largest absolute deviation), and report timeout rates separately because verification queries are undecidable in general and unknown outcomes would otherwise be silently scored. Minimality is judged by comparing discovered circuit size against the hitting-set lower bound, and by the ordering of the four minimality notions on a six-node Boolean toy network. A per-component set analysis (intersection, both set differences, IoU, Dice, and the two asymmetric coverage ratios) checks whether the certified circuits contain the sampled ones or replace them.

## What they found

Certification is decisive and costs time, not size. On the main table the sampling-based circuits are 19.2% (MNIST), 46.5% (CIFAR-10), 27.6% (GTSRB) and 9.5% (TaxiNet) robust under the continuous criterion, while the certified circuits are 100% robust in every cell at sizes 15.8 vs 12.6 neurons, 19.2 vs 16.5 filters, 29.6 vs 28.9 filters and 6.8 vs 5.8 filters — a handful more components for a guarantee. Runtime rises by three to four orders of magnitude: 0.31 s to 612 s on MNIST, 0.23 s to 2,971 s on CIFAR-10. For patching, zero- and mean-patched circuits come out at 46.4%/33.3% (CIFAR-10), 58.0%/55.7% (MNIST), 38.0%/40.5% (GTSRB) and 57.1%/63.3% (TaxiNet), against 100% for the certified variants at nearly identical sizes and runtimes of 176–5,409 s; mean patching is not reliably better than zero patching, which is a direct empirical answer to the objection that mean-patching is the principled choice. Sweeping the patching radius shows the uncertified circuits collapsing — MNIST 87% at radius 0.005 down to 1.2% at 0.05, TaxiNet 93% down to 0% — while the certified ones stay at 100% with size growing from 11.3 to 23.2 neurons. Minimality behaves as the theory says: hitting-set size never exceeds circuit size in any run, some greedy circuits hit the bound exactly, and some hitting sets certify — but the search is capped at blocking sets of size three, so on the group sizes used the exact cardinality is sometimes established and sometimes only bracketed. Among the four minimality notions the toy network separates them cleanly (one node cardinally, two subset-, three locally-, five quasi-minimal), and the theory carries the sharper message: greedy elimination is only guaranteed subset-minimal when the predicate is monotone, monotonicity holds when the input domain sits inside the patching domain and the activation set is closed under concatenation, and without monotonicity there are infinitely many networks where greedy and binary search both return the whole graph while a two-node circuit is faithful — a ReLU( x ) − ReLU( x ) + x cancellation being the minimal counterexample. In the qualitative appendix the certified GTSRB circuit (26 channels) tracks the full model's Grad-CAM map and stays put under a 0.001 perturbation, while the larger sampled circuit (35 channels) already shifts its attention; the certified CIFAR circuit's extra first-layer filter is the only one responding positively in exactly the region where the perturbation was placed.

## Limits and open problems they name

The whole framework leans on the verifier, and the verifier does not scale to the models the rest of this folder studies — the authors are explicit that verification for state-of-the-art models remains limited and that this is a bet on the field's trajectory. They name only that. The other limits are mine. First, every robustness claim is bounded by a tolerance and a radius: "100% robust" means the logit difference stayed under a number chosen per dataset, and on TaxiNet that number is set to the model's own RMSE, so the certificate can be satisfied by a loose band. Second, the certified domain is a union of ℓ∞ balls around correctly-predicted test points, so nothing is claimed off-distribution, and a circuit can be certified on 100 balls while being wrong elsewhere. Third, the hitting-set minimality is computed over enumerated small blocking sets and the guarantee degrades exactly where the interesting circuits are large; no cardinality claim survives on a graph of tens of thousands of edges. Fourth, there is no semantic claim anywhere and no test that the certified circuit is the *mechanism* rather than a sufficient subgraph — the paper's own toy example makes the point that { v 1 } and { v 2 , v 4 } compute the same thing, so certification plus minimality still leaves the identification question open. Fifth, all four models are small CNNs/MLPs with piecewise-linear activations; the siamese encodings that make the certificates possible are precisely what becomes unavailable with softmax attention at scale.

## What we take from it

**A framing, and it is the important one.** "The circuit matches the model on my eval set" and "the circuit matches the model on a region" are different claims, and this paper shows the gap is not academic: the same greedy algorithm produces circuits that pass at 90% on samples and fail over the region, because sampled faithfulness is a point test of a property that is supposed to hold on a set. Any predictive white-box model of ours is scored against a patching abstraction, and the abstraction's fidelity is exactly the kind of property that should be quoted with an interval attached rather than a single number — even if we cannot certify, we can report the radius at which the claim survives.

**A measure worth importing in cheap form:** the radius-at-tolerance curve. Take a circuit (or a replacement model), sweep the perturbation radius, and report the largest radius at which the agreement still holds — the sampling-based column of this paper is that curve, and it is far more informative than a single faithfulness number because it exposes the cliff (TaxiNet: 93% at radius 0.005, 0% at 0.05).

**A warning about monoticity, which is the load-bearing assumption under every greedy circuit method in this folder.** Greedy elimination only yields subset-minimal circuits if faithfulness is monotone in the component set, and under patching it need not be: the paper's counterexample family is networks where two nodes cancel, so no single removal is survivable but the pair is, and greedy returns the entire graph. That explains a phenomenon this folder already records — circuits that keep components no ablation can justify — and it means a component that survives greedy elimination is evidence of a monotone-ish predicate, not evidence of necessity.

**A reusable algorithmic idea:** blocking-set duality. If you can cheaply produce subgraphs whose removal provably breaks the behaviour, the minimum hitting set of those is a lower bound on circuit size and sometimes an exact answer. That converts "our circuit has 26 units" into "no faithful circuit can have fewer than 19", which is a claim of a completely different kind, and it is a construction we could run with an uncertified predicate to get bounded claims at tractable cost.
