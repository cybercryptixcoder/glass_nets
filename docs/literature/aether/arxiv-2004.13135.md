# Local Lipschitz Bounds of Deep Neural Networks

- **Key:** arxiv-2004.13135
- **Authors / organisation:** Calypso Herrera, Florian Krach, Josef Teichmann; Department of Mathematics, ETH Zürich
- **Date:** 2023-02 (v3; v1 2020-04)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2004.13135
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Gradient-based training theory quotes convergence rates in terms of the Lipschitz constant of the loss gradient with respect to the *parameters* — that constant sets the admissible step size and the rate — yet nobody could say how large it is for an actual architecture. Existing estimates cover the network as a function of its *inputs*, which is the robustness question, not the optimisation question. The authors therefore ask for a closed-form, architecture-dependent bound on both the network map and its gradient, taken over the parameter space, and they want it bad enough to prove that no such bound can exist globally. The larger goal is to replace "some finite constant exists" in convergence analysis with an expression the analyst can actually compute from depth, width, activation-derivative bounds and weight norms.

## What they studied
Fully connected feed-forward networks of depth `m` with layer widths `ℓ_u`, activations assumed bounded with bounded first and second derivatives (sigmoid, tanh; ReLU via a smoothing argument). The unit of analysis is the parameter vector `Θ` — all weight matrices and biases flattened into one vector — and the objects bounded are the map `Θ ↦ N_Θ(ζ_x)` for a fixed sample, its gradient `∇_Θ N`, the expected loss `Φ` and its gradient. The parameter set `Ω` is assumed open, bounded in the max norm by `B_Ω`. A second, longer half generalises the whole thing to networks defined as solutions of controlled ODEs, covering neural ODEs, ODE-RNNs, neural SDEs and neural jump models.

## How they did it
They first set up the abstract quantities: a flattened Frobenius-type norm on lists of matrices, the layer recursion, the per-sample cost `φ(Θ,ζ) = g(N_Θ(ζ_x), ζ_y)` with `g` twice differentiable in its first argument and with bounded gradient and Hessian, and the population loss as an expectation. Then they prove a chain lemma (Lemma B.1) that propagates Lipschitz and uniform bounds through one affine-plus-activation block using only three activation scalars — `σ_max`, `σ'_max`, `σ''_max` — plus a weight-norm constant `D_u = sqrt(ℓ_{u-1} ℓ_u) B_Ω` and the input norm `S`. Theorem 3.1 iterates that lemma over layers to get recursive bounds on each layer's partial map and its gradient, then on the whole network; a corollary solves the recursions into closed-form expressions in depth, width, the activation scalars and `B_Ω`. Theorem 3.3 lifts the same constants through the loss by replacing the input norm with the random variable `S = ‖proj_x(Z)‖` and taking expectations, which is what makes the constants apply to the population loss rather than one sample. To show the bounds are not vacuous in the other direction they construct two adversarial parameter pairs by hand (Example 4.1, Example 4.2) with equal weights set to a common value and biases flipped, and read lower bounds off the resulting output and gradient distances. Finally they re-run the whole programme in the controlled-ODE language, where the chain rule becomes a linear ODE for `∂_θ X_t` and the bounds acquire exponential factors `exp(L_{V_x} B_υ)` in the control's total variation. The application section plugs the bound into Liu & Orabona's adaptive step size for SGD with clipping and reads a `C/sqrt(n)` stationarity rate with `n ≥ (C/δ)^2`.

## What counts as an explanation here
An explicit formula: a closed-form upper bound on a constant, derived from architecture and weight norms, plus a lower-bound construction showing the same expression cannot be improved to a global one. It is partial by design and its own authors say so — the bound is valid but too large to be used to set a step size, so its function is to license theoretical statements, not to guide training. It predicts nothing about behaviour; it predicts only the scale of the convergence-rate constant, and it predicts that scale is exponential in depth.

## How they judged success
There is no benchmark, no baseline comparison and almost no measurement. The correctness criterion is proof: each theorem is argued from a chain lemma with the three activation conditions made explicit. The only empirical check is a single MNIST experiment — networks of differing depth, every hidden layer 40 units, AdaGrad at a fixed learning rate, weight clipping to max-norm below 1, dropout 0.1, batch 500, 50k epochs — where the number of epochs to reach a gradient norm below δ = 0.08 is plotted against depth and a linear regression on the logarithms of the means is fit to argue the growth is exponential. Figure 2 shows the gradient norms during training beside the δ line. Nothing checks the value of the bound itself against a measured Lipschitz constant on any network.

## What they found
The network and its gradient are Lipschitz in the parameters with constants that factor into a per-layer recursion; in closed form the map's constant is dominated by `(ℓ² B_Ω)^{2(u-1)} (σ'_max)^{2u}`-type terms and the gradient's by factors of order `ℓ^{10u-9}`, i.e. exponential in depth and polynomial of high degree in width, in `σ'_max` and in `B_Ω`. Both are exponential in `m`, and the hand-built examples show the lower bounds are also exponential in `m`, so the exponential growth is a property of the object, not an artefact of loose algebra. Global bounds are impossible: if any single weight entry is unbounded, the constructed pair drives the output difference faster than any fixed constant times the parameter distance, so the same argument kills any distribution with unbounded support and finite moments — Gaussian initialisation included, since for every threshold the constant exceeds it with positive probability. Weakening the assumption to bounded weights alone (arbitrary biases) is permitted. For ReLU, only the boundedness constant of each layer changes, because ReLU is norm-nonexpansive, and an `ε` accounts for smoothing the kink; sharp ReLU genuinely destroys gradient Lipschitzness, since the derivative is discontinuous. The controlled-ODE version produces bounds with the same character, with the number of layers replaced by the total variation of the controls. In the MNIST check, the epochs to reach the tolerance grow exponentially with depth as predicted.

## Limits and open problems they name
They state plainly that the bounds are far too large to be used in practice, and that their purpose is theoretical convergence analysis rather than step-size setting. The maximum norm is chosen over the 2-norm because the 2-norm would require optimising over how weight mass is distributed across layers, which they defer to an appendix; since the two norms are equivalent only up to dimension-dependent constants, the choice is a real slack term they do not price. Convolutional layers are handled by a one-line embedding into fully connected layers with a corrected `D_u`, and every other layer type is left to future work. ReLU is covered only after smoothing, and the gap between the smoothed and true network is bounded by a constant the paper introduces but does not measure. The controlled-ODE half is a generalisation for its own sake, with no experiment attached.

## What we take from it
Two things, one methodological and one cautionary. The methodological one is the chain lemma: a Lipschitz bound for a composite computed layer by layer from three scalars per activation plus a weight-norm budget is exactly the shape of a compositional white-box account, and it is the cleanest available statement of what such an account needs to know about a unit — not its values, only the size of its derivative and second derivative. The cautionary one is the gap between the two constants the paper computes. It prints a bound and it prints a lower bound, in a single display each, and the ratio between them on a deep network is astronomical — the lower bound carries `ℓ^{m-1/2}` and the upper `ℓ^{2m}` times a `sqrt(m)`. So the paper knows both ends and never computes the ratio, which is run 648's complaint about product bounds arriving here from the opposite direction: there the paper could not measure the true constant, here it has a proof that the true constant lies in a wide interval and declines to report how wide. Rule for us: **when our account brackets a quantity from both sides, the width of the bracket is a headline number and not an afterthought, because a bracketed constant with a two-exponential spread is a statement about the algebra, not about the model.** Secondary and more useful: the parameter-space Lipschitz constant is a *different* object from the input-space one that every other paper in this sub-head computes, and the distinction is worth keeping explicit in our own write-up — the first governs optimisation, the second governs robustness, they are computed from the same weights, and a paper that says "the Lipschitz constant" without naming which is not saying enough.
