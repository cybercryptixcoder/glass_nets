# An Interpretability Illusion for BERT

- **Key:** arxiv-2104.07143
- **Authors / organisation:** Tolga Bolukbasi, Adam Pearce, Ann Yuan, Andy Coenen, Emily Reif, Fernanda Viégas, Martin Wattenberg — **Google Research, Cambridge, MA**. The seven authors are marked as equal contributors.
- **Date:** 2021-04 (v1 2021-04-14)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.07143
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

The paper takes the most common move in NLP interpretability — pick a neuron, sort a corpus by its activation, read the top sentences, name the shared property — and asks whether the names it produces are stable. They run the same model, unchanged, over four different corpora and find that a neuron's apparent concept is a different concept in each: one unit reads as song titles on questions, historical events on Wikipedia, and detective-novel action in book text. They call this an *interpretability illusion* and their target is methodological rather than technical: an interpretation that holds on one dataset and vanishes on another was never a fact about the model. The larger goal is a theory of *how* concepts can be laid out in an activation space at all, so that a claim like "this direction means X" can be distinguished from a claim like "sentences near this point are about X", and so that interpretability results carry a stated scope instead of an implicit one.

## What they studied

**BERT-base uncased** from HuggingFace, no fine-tuning and no dataset-specific modification. The unit of analysis is a *direction in the final-layer [CLS] embedding space* (768 dimensions): basis-vector "neurons" and random linear directions, plus, as a control, random sets of sentences. Four corpora — Quora Question Pairs (164,246 sentences), QNLI passages from Wikipedia (58,645), a random Wikipedia subset (203,736) and Toronto BookCorpus (198,085 sampled) — 624,712 sentences in total. Nothing is trained, ablated or steered; the whole paper is read-out plus human annotation of what the read-out shows.

## How they did it

Every sentence is encoded once and its final-layer [CLS] state stored. For a direction v, sentences are ranked by their projection score, the dot product of embedding with v, and the top ten are displayed; the same is done for random directions and for random sentence sets. Human annotators saw a set of ten sentences and recorded whether they shared a *pattern* — a lexical property such as containing variants of a phrase, or a structural one such as all having the same length — and which sentences carried it. Annotation was blind to the condition: annotators knew the source corpus but not whether the ten were top-activating, top-activating along a random direction, or randomly drawn. All told, 25 randomly selected neurons, 33 random directions and 29 random sentence sets were annotated, twice over. To explain the illusion they then test three hypotheses with separate instruments: a UMAP plot plus a linear SVM classifier over the sentence embeddings, to show the corpora occupy distinct regions and can be told apart; a *locality score*, comparing the distribution of pairwise distances among top-ten sentences against the distribution of distances between those sentences and their ten nearest neighbours in the original space (intersection-over-union between the two histograms, with random-sentence distances as the floor); and a token-frequency analysis, counting how often a token's rate rises or falls monotonically across activation quintiles for a given neuron/token pair. Two robustness probes follow: a count of duplicate sentences among the highest-activating ones, and a repetition of the whole analysis with the most distant 1% and 10% of sentences removed.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **named concept attached to a direction** — "neuron 221 means song titles" — offered as a description of what BERT encodes, and explicitly meant to be a *partial* one. The paper's structural contribution is a three-way classification of what such a name can possibly be attached to: a *global* concept, where moving along a fixed direction makes the concept more prevalent everywhere; a *dataset-level* concept, where the direction only orders sentences inside the region a corpus occupies; and a *local* concept, which exists only as a cluster and has no direction at all. The illusion is the failure to distinguish these: a top-activating list conflates whichever of the three happens to be present in that corpus. Predictively, the framework buys a checkable distinction — a global direction should survive a change of corpus, a dataset-level one must not, and a local one need not correspond to any direction — which is the only falsifiable claim the paper makes about its own interpretations.

## How they judged success

Three separate instruments, each with its own control. For the pattern claims: annotator agreement, a random-sentence baseline (to measure how much pattern the corpus gives away for free), and the count of distinct patterns per direction across the four corpora (mean 2.5). For the geometry: a linear classifier's ability to separate corpora, and the locality score with a paired comparison between directions annotated meaningful and meaningless, reported with per-dataset significance values. For the direction claims: monotonicity across activation quintiles against the analytic chance rate for five bins to be in order, with the token universe restricted to the 915 tokens occurring at least 100 times in every corpus. Everything is descriptive statistics over annotations; there is no held-out task, no intervention and no scoring of the concepts against any ground truth.

## What they found

The illusion is reproducible and it is not specific to neurons. Top-activating sets were annotated as meaningful far more often than random sets: across all corpora, 80% of neuron sets and 82% of random-direction sets carried a pattern against 48% of random sentence sets, and only 12% of neuron sets were annotated as pattern-free against 38% of random sets (the remainder being cases where the two annotators disagreed). The corpora do supply a topic bias that lifts random sets to that 48%, but the *same* direction produced different, and equally convincing, patterns on each corpus: 2.5 distinct patterns per neuron on average. Annotators were also far more confident about the top-activating sets than about random ones (8% split decisions against 38%), which is the illusion wearing the face of evidence. Crucially, random directions scored as well as neurons (mean patterns per direction 6.89 against 6.80), so the phenomenon is a property of the geometry, not of learned units. The corpora are geometrically separable — the SVM tells them apart and the UMAP plot shows clean dataset clusters — and only 38% of top-ten activation ranges overlap between a pair of corpora. Meaningful directions show measurably higher locality than meaningless ones (0.026 against 0.010 overall, p = 0.0004), which the authors read as the patterns coming primarily from a sentence's neighbourhood rather than from a direction. The monotonicity analysis finds real structure underneath: a quarter to a third of neuron/token pairs change token frequency monotonically within a corpus, but only 3-11% do so across three or four corpora together, and the tokens that survive that stricter test are mostly function words — pronouns, auxiliaries, articles. The outlier probe leaves the illusion intact: the most distant 1% of sentences absorb 48% of top activations and most of the annotated sentences recur across directions, yet removing the most distant 1% and even 10% does not remove the effect.

## Limits and open problems they name

One model, one layer, one pooling choice: the final layer's [CLS] token, un-normalised, with an appendix defence that normalising changes little. An informal look at layers 2 and 7 suggests the illusion appears there too, but the authors say a full analysis was out of scope. The annotation is small and human, and they report the weakness directly: annotators differ widely in how readily they find patterns (from 0.56 to 1.6 patterns per annotation across six annotators), so some of what was annotated is in the annotator rather than the sentences, and they count that as one of the three causes. Concept naming is by shared surface property, not by semantics, and no causal test is run on any named direction — nothing is ablated, steered or checked against a downstream task. Generalisation to other architectures, to token-level embeddings, and to non-text inputs is listed as future work. The paper's practical recommendation is narrow and clear: test hypotheses on more than one dataset and treat cross-corpus agreement as the minimum bar.

## What we take from it

- **A read-out has no meaning until its *scope* is stated, and the cheapest test of scope is a second corpus.** This is the data-side twin of `arxiv-1810.03292`'s randomisation test: there the model is destroyed and the account must move, here the model is untouched and only the probe set changes, and the account must *not* move. For our own accounts the translation is mechanical: fit the decoder, then refit its *evaluation* on a held-out corpus drawn from a different source, and report the concept labels from both side by side. A label that changes should be printed as two labels with a scope qualifier, not silently averaged.
- **Random directions are the control that makes a neuron claim meaningful, and here they match neurons almost exactly.** "Our dictionary found a curve detector" is unevidenced until the same pipeline on random directions yields the same rate of convincing names — the rate being the measured quantity, not the existence of one good example. This is the same discipline as the two-row floor in `arxiv-1806.10758` and the random-effect floor in `arxiv-2205.14140`, applied to read-out rather than to attribution.
- **Distinguish a global direction from a local cluster before interpreting anything, and one score does it.** The locality score — do the top-activated items sit near each other in the original space, relative to their distance from random items — separates meaningful from meaningless read-outs with a reported p-value at almost no cost. For us, a feature dictionary entry whose activating examples are one semantic neighbourhood is describing where those examples live, not what the model computes with them. Coreference `arxiv-1810.03292` (an account that is a function of the input), `arxiv-2410.13032` (the reference distribution that decides).
- **Cross-corpus monotonicity is a usable, cheap falsifier for "this direction encodes X".** Counting tokens whose frequency moves monotonically across activation quintiles, within and across corpora, gives a way to sort claims into per-corpus accidents (25-33% within, but only 3-11% across three or four) from candidates for genuine global structure (function words). The 1.7% chance rate for five ordered bins is the analytic floor any such count should be printed against.
