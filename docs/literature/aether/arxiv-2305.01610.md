# Finding Neurons in a Haystack: Case Studies with Sparse Probing

- **Key:** arxiv-2305.01610
- **Authors / organisation:** Wes Gurnee (MIT, correspondence wesg@mit.edu), Neel Nanda (Independent), Matthew Pauly (Harvard University), Katherine Harvey (Harvard University), Dmitrii Troitskii (Northeastern University), Dimitris Bertsimas (MIT)
- **Date:** 2023-05-02 (v2 2023-06-02)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.01610
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
The opening premise is that network units are dense linear algebra followed by a pointwise nonlinearity, which makes it natural that a layer computes with more features than it has dimensions — and that the field has been asserting this from toy models without establishing it at scale. The authors want to know whether superposition, monosemanticity and polysemanticity, all of which had been demonstrated mainly on synthetic tasks, actually appear in real pretrained transformers, and how a feature's *sparsity* of representation varies across layer, feature type and model size. Their framing commitment is that a probe is a localisation instrument and not an explanatory one: the paper's central claim is methodological, that constraining a probe to few units and then corroborating every interpretation with independent evidence is how neuron-level understanding has to be done. The larger goal is an "artificial neuroscience" — full model reverse-engineering as a natural-science programme rather than a single-method result — and the paper is explicit that superposition sits on the critical path to it, because superposition forces polysemantic units and polysemantic units block decomposition into independently meaningful parts.

## What they studied
Seven models from EleutherAI's Pythia suite, 70M to 6.9B parameters, same family, same data order, The Pile. The unit of analysis is the *neuron* in a strict sense they define: the post-GELU MLP activation, not the residual stream, not attention keys or values — precisely because the elementwise nonlinearity breaks rotational invariance and so gives that basis a reason to be meaningful, whereas a linear map's output does not. Over 100 binary features in ten collections (natural language of Europarl, programming language from GitHub, Pile data source, part-of-speech, dependency, morphology, plain-text features, 21 compound words, LaTeX features, Wikidata attributes of people). Probing datasets come with two to fourteen thousand sequences each and class ratios from 0.11 to 0.50.

## How they did it
A *k*-sparse probe is a linear classifier with at most *k* non-zero coefficients, so selecting which neurons to use is a cardinality-constrained combinatorial problem. They compare five scores — mean difference of class means, mutual information, absolute coefficient of an L1 probe, an F-statistic, and random — plus two selection procedures of their own: adaptive thresholding, which retrains a probe at each step keeping only the top-*k* coefficients from the previous step and so sweeps *k* from 256 down to 1 cheaply, and optimal sparse probing, a cardinality-constrained SVM solved to provable optimality by cutting planes but only tractable for small *k*. All probes are logistic regression on the *selected* neurons; all experiments filter to the top 1024 neurons by mean difference first, and classes are balanced. Evaluation is out-of-sample F1, with Matthews correlation as a check that gives largely the same ordering. Then the case studies, each of which is the real methodology: a compound-word probe asking for neurons that fire on XY but not on XZ or WY for any other words; a check of the same neurons over hundreds of millions of tokens and over per-token average activations rather than max-activating examples; neuron ablation during language modelling to measure loss impact; the product of the unembedding matrix with a neuron's output weight to see which tokens it promotes; and a basis-alignment control where the activation data is multiplied by a random Gaussian matrix and the identical sparse-selection pipeline is run in that rotated basis.

## What counts as an explanation here
A located unit plus a stated feature, with a confidence level attached — a probe reporting that neuron N at layer L carries feature F, graded by F1 and then refined by secondary analyses. It is explicitly partial and explicitly not causal: the authors say the output of a probing experiment licenses no strong conclusion without follow-up, and that what they offer is localisation with corroboration rather than a mechanism. The vocabulary they insist on is worth carrying: superposition (a global statement that more features than dimensions are represented), polysemanticity (a local statement about one neuron responding to unrelated things), and distributed representation (a feature carried by a linear combination of neurons rather than a basis element) are three different claims, and only the first is global. The paper's FAQ adds a fourth distinction they say is missing from the literature — *neuron* superposition is computational, because the nonlinearity is what allows the layer to compute with more features than it has, while residual-stream superposition is representational and only about storage.

## How they judged success
Out-of-sample F1 as the primary metric, chosen because the positive class is the asymmetric one; precision and recall read separately as a diagnostic of granularity mismatch (high recall with low precision suggests the unit represents something broader than the probe label, the reverse suggests something narrower), and never as a verdict. Controls and baselines: a random-neuron baseline, the random-rotation (non-privileged-basis) control, five competing selection scores compared head to head, and the logistic-loss-versus-*k* curve where a kink in the power law is read as an estimate of true sparsity. For the case studies the corroboration is a mixture of theoretical prediction (the weight-norm-times-bias fingerprint derived from the superposition construction in their Appendix C), ablation, and vocabulary analysis of the neuron's effect on output logits. They also record a decision rule they follow throughout: ablation is the test that separates "this neuron computes the feature" from "this neuron merely reads it".

## What they found
Sparse probing finds real structure. A single neuron in Pythia-1B is nearly perfect on the bigram "social security" against both of its halves, yet fires on a wide spread of unrelated *n*-grams once checked over 300 million tokens, and summing three such neurons separates the compound from everything else almost perfectly — offered as the first demonstration of neuron superposition in a real language model. Early-layer MLP neurons carry the fingerprint their construction predicts, large input weight norms against large negative biases, concentrated in the first quarter of the MLP layers. Context features behave the opposite way: a Go-specific `return` neuron (1B.L6.N3108) and similar language, programming-language and Pile-subset neurons look genuinely monosemantic, and ablating one costs 8% average loss per French sequence in the 70M model against 0.2% in the 6.9B model — the same neuron is a far smaller share of a much larger network. Sparsity rises with scale on average but not uniformly: programming-language and factual features get sparser, part-of-speech and compound words barely move, and some of the simplest text features are represented *more* sparsely in the smallest models. The two dynamics offered are the quantization model of scaling, where bigger models reach a longer tail of rarer features, and neuron splitting, where a coarser feature such as all-caps gets broken into separate units for abbreviations, constants and shouting, which reads as *less* sparsity. Methods comparison: mean difference, mutual information and optimal sparse probing all land within about a point of one another at every *k* (0.832 / 0.831 / 0.831 at *k*=1, rising to 0.890 / 0.886 / 0.902 at *k*=8), a random selection reaches 0.573 at *k*=1, and the more expensive methods cost from 32 to 53 seconds against a fraction of a second. Two negative results are printed as negatives: no sparse "falsehood" neuron was found in the counterfact dataset — performance kept climbing until about *k*=1024 and reached 90%, which they read as the probe detecting dissonance rather than a truth feature — and ablating the factual neurons had very limited effect, consistent with either computation upstream or redundancy.

## Limits and open problems they name
Probing inherits its whole failure list: no causal warrant, sensitivity to dataset construction and spurious correlates, inability to separate superposition from composition (a feature may be a conjunction of three coarser features that happen to need three neurons), inability to see features assembled across layers, and failure to find redundant neurons because the sparsest classifier picks one of an interchangeable set. The probe dataset's negative class is the hard part — the `is_politician` discussion shows how the choice of negatives silently decides which of `is_political`, `is_person` or `is_politician` you measure. Monosemanticity is asserted with an explicit disclaimer that it cannot be proved: doing so needs an ontology ("what is French?") and a search over billions of tokens for rare counterexamples, and the max-activating-example view is called an interpretability illusion because a wider corpus reorders it. Distributional statistics hide rare features — the `is_title` neuron at F1 above 0.95 turned out to be explaining section titles for less than half its activations. All seven models are one family on one corpus, the largest is more than an order of magnitude off the frontier, everything is binary or binarised, and the analysis is described as ad hoc and researcher-intensive.

## What we take from it
Four instruments and one warning. (1) *Sparsity as a hypothesis test rather than a hyperparameter*: sweep *k* and read the shape of the loss or F1 curve, since a kink where extra neurons stop paying is evidence about the true number of units a feature is carried by — a cheap, auditable estimate of a quantity our accounts otherwise assert. (2) *The random-basis control*: multiply the activation data by a random Gaussian matrix, run the identical selection pipeline, and compare. If the claim that units are meaningful is worth anything, the real basis has to beat the rotated one, and here it does on both F1 and logistic loss. This is a control we can run on any unit-level claim at essentially zero cost, and one almost nobody runs — the more so since even random neurons carry substantial signal (F1 0.573), which prices how much the true basis buys. (3) *Precision and recall as a granularity instrument*: report them separately and read the asymmetry as a statement about whether the unit is broader or narrower than our label, because it is the cheapest way to discover that our feature name is wrong. (4) *Separate reading from computing by ablation, and expect the read-only case to be common*: their factual neurons located cleanly and ablated to almost nothing, and their own interpretation is that either the computation happened earlier or the network ensembles. Any account we build that localises a feature must therefore state which of those two it cannot rule out. The warning: this paper is the folder's best illustration of the max-activating-example trap — a neuron that looks monosemantic on its top twenty examples can be polysemantic across a larger corpus, so the evidence for a unit account is the *distribution* over a much wider corpus, not the examples chosen to illustrate it.
