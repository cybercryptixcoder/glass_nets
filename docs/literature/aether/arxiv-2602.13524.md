## What they wanted, and why

A dozen papers now assume that the singular vectors of an attention head's query-key matrix stand in for the features that head computes with — several of them trace circuits on that assumption — and none of them says why the assumption should hold. The authors set out to close exactly that gap, asking under what conditions a feature and a singular vector of the QK matrix will point in the same direction, and how one could tell from outside a model whether that has happened. The framing is tightly scoped: features are taken to be linearly represented and tokens to be sums of features, and the head is taken to attend to a token pair because certain features are present in the two tokens. The larger goal is a cheaper route to feature identification than either probing (which shows what is decodable, not what is used) or sparse autoencoders (which are expensive and, the authors note, are built only from activations and never look at weights). If singular vectors really do track features, then identifying a feature reduces to projecting an activation into a basis that is computed once from the weights, on a single forward pass and per prompt.

## What they studied

Two substrates. First, an extended version of the toy autoencoder model of superposed features, to which they add one attention head: 20 features in 10 dimensions with a single large attention head as the default, with sweeps over feature probability, attention-loss weight, number of features, head dimension, context length and seed. Second, two real models — Pythia-160M across its training checkpoints and GPT-2-small — on the indirect object identification task, restricted to the attention heads and token pairs identified as circuit members in earlier work. The unit of analysis is the pair of weight matrices inside one head, read as a single bilinear form, and the objects compared are that form's singular vectors against the model's own feature dictionary.

## How they did it

Tokens are formed as a feature-strength vector multiplied by a learned dictionary, and reconstructed through a ReLU decoder, giving the standard superposition setup. Onto that they add a head producing a bilinear logit between a query token and each key token, followed by a softmax. The training target is specified at the logit level: a teacher assigns a target coefficient to each (feature-in-query, feature-in-key) pair, so a head can be told to attend when feature *i* is in the query and feature *j* is in the key, or when several such pairs are present with different strengths. The head is trained on cross-entropy between its softmax and the teacher's, and the dictionary is trained on reconstruction — a weighted sum of the two losses — so the features and the weights are learned together and can be compared directly. Because the features are columns of an observed matrix, the authors can measure the cosine between each feature and each singular vector, which no real-model experiment permits.

For the theory they pose a student-teacher problem over population quantities, with separate query-side and key-side feature sets and their Gram matrices, and solve for the loss-minimizing bilinear form. For real models, since alignment cannot be measured directly, they derive a prediction that can: if features align with singular vectors and non-interesting features are roughly orthogonal to interesting ones, then decomposing a head's relative attention in its own singular-vector basis should be *sparse* when a small number of features of interest is present, and unsurprisingly dense when none is. They then track a sparsity index and a reconstruction-count index during training in Pythia and measure the same reconstruction count in GPT-2, with a control that randomizes the singular-vector bases while holding the spectrum fixed.

## What counts as an explanation here

A weight-side account of a representational fact, and a deliberately partial one. The object explained is why a particular property of the weights holds — the alignment of singular vectors with feature directions — and the explanation is a theorem giving the closed form of the trained bilinear form, plus a companion theorem about why the features themselves move. It is not a circuit, not a dictionary, and not a replacement model; it says nothing about what any head computes beyond the fact that it screens for particular feature conjunctions. Its predictive content is narrow but real: the closed form names the exact direction the singular vectors take (the covariance-whitened features), and it yields a downstream, checkable prediction about how a head's attention decomposes. The authors are explicit that alignment is licensed rather than proven in real models — in a trained network one cannot confirm a singular vector matches a feature without already knowing that feature's representation.

## How they judged success

Evidence comes in four layers. In the toy model, success is visual and quantitative: cosine similarity between each feature and its claimed singular vector, and the singular-value spectrum, shown in full for the single-pair and multi-pair cases. Robustness is a sweep over six parameters — feature probability from 1 down to 0.02, attention-loss weight over two orders of magnitude, feature count from the hidden dimension to three times it, head dimension down to 4, several context lengths, and five seeds. The theory is judged by proof: uniqueness of the minimizer, a perturbation bound via Wedin's and Weyl's inequalities, and an orthogonality bound for the second feature pair. Anisotropy is calibrated against measurement rather than assumed away: they compute the deviation-from-isotropy operator for residual-stream autoencoder dictionaries on all twelve GPT-2-small layers, find its operator norm spanning 10 to 55, and then sweep the toy model across that same range, checking both raw alignment and a whitened variant. In real models, the metric is a sparsity index normalized to 1 for uniform contributions and 1/n for a single contribution, plus a reconstruction count, with a spectrum-preserving random-rotation control as the key null.

## What they found

When a head is trained to fire on one feature in the query and one in the key, the trained QK form is provably rank-1 with left and right singular vectors proportional to the covariance-whitened versions of those two features — the exact statement of the minimizer, and under isotropic features this reduces to alignment with the features themselves. Multiple feature pairs also align, one pair per singular value, with the largest singular value going to the highest-target-logit pair; the remaining features are pushed to be orthogonal to the aligned pair, which a third theorem derives as the consequence of penalizing interference. The two effects reinforce each other in training: singular vectors move into position first, at the pace set by their contribution to attention loss, and features then shift to become orthogonal, and the paper shows these two movements interleaved over training steps. Under anisotropy the mean cosine similarity stays above 0.75 even near the top of the GPT-2 range, and the theorem's bound is loose in practice; whitening the singular vectors by the feature covariance recovers a substantial part of the loss. The sparse-decomposition prediction holds in both substrates: in the toy model sparsity emerges only when a feature of interest is present and disappears when none is; in Pythia-160M the same index falls over training for the IOI circuit heads, typically with only a small handful of singular vectors needed to reconstruct relative attention; and in GPT-2 the reconstruction count is small for most IOI heads, varying by functional role. The random-rotation control removes the effect entirely, and the largest contributions frequently come from among the *smallest* singular values — which rules out the trivial explanation that sparsity is just a low-rank or ill-conditioned spectrum. Under rotary position encoding, alignment survives for position-independent features, and position-sensitive features are additionally allocated to the frequency band that matches their positional dependence.

## Limits and open problems they name

Directly examining features in real models is out of scope, so real-model support rests on a downstream prediction and on the interpretability and causal efficacy of singular-vector features reported elsewhere. The theory covers a single head; how singular vectors are allocated *across* heads, and whether heads coordinate their use of singular vectors, are named as open. When a head has more features of interest than dimensions, the preliminary appendix shows the lowest-logit features collapsing onto the smallest singular vector — so the model can confuse features from different pairs — and the authors call for much more work there. Feature anisotropy in real models might make some singular vectors track over-represented "cone" directions rather than features, and while their preliminary study is reassuring they do not settle it. RoPE is covered by a toy study rather than a theorem. And the sparse-decomposition prediction is indirect: it confirms that *something* consistent with alignment is happening, not which singular vector corresponds to which named feature.

## What we take from it

- **A method, and the cheapest one this folder has seen.** If a head's singular vectors are candidate features and the only computation needed is a projection into a basis read off the weights, then feature identification costs one SVD per head and one forward pass per prompt — no training. For our white-box model this is the strongest available argument that some feature attributions can be read off the weights directly rather than learned, and it is exactly the weight-side complement to the reconstruction-error surrogates this folder has been reading.
- **A framing.** The paper treats the toy model as a place where the unobservable quantity (the feature) is observable, derives a closed form there, and then converts the conclusion into a *prediction about an observable in the real model*. That is the cleanest template in this folder for turning an unfalsifiable representational assumption into a test — and it is the shape our model needs for every claim about internal directions.
- **A measure, and a warning about it.** The sparsity index is normalized so uniform contributions score 1 and a single contribution scores 1/n, which makes it comparable across layer and head. But the paper's own headline robustness numbers — above 0.75 mean cosine under strong anisotropy, 1-to-4 singular vectors reconstructing relative attention — are agreements about *which direction* and *how few*, with no statement of how much of the attention logit the aligned components actually carry. That is the same gap run 785's share table was written to close.
- **A control to copy.** Randomly rotating the singular-vector bases while preserving the spectrum is the right null for any claim of the form "these particular directions are special": it holds rank, conditioning and eigenvalue distribution fixed and varies only orientation. Every orientation-based claim our model makes should ship it.
