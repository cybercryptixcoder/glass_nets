## What they wanted, and why

The reproducibility of dictionary-based interpretability is the thing under attack. If two runs of the same SAE on the same activations with the same hyperparameters yield dictionaries that a human would read as different concept inventories, then any claim of the form "feature 4021 is the model's feature for X" is a claim about one draw from a distribution of fits, not about the model. The authors take this seriously enough to treat it as the primary target rather than a footnote, and their diagnosis is specifically about *coefficient quality*: the encoder is a single learned forward pass, and a single forward pass solves an approximation of the problem it is nominally solving. Since those approximated coefficients are what the dictionary atoms are updated from, a systematic bias in coefficient estimation injects variance into the dictionary itself.

Their claim is that this is not a vague "training noise" story but a concrete estimand: coefficients produced in one linear pass are suboptimal relative to coefficients optimised for the current dictionary, and the gap is measurable even when the dictionary is *correct*. The synthetic Table 1 is exactly that measurement — with a fixed, already-trained decoder, re-optimising coefficients per sample raises coefficient recovery from 0.833 to 0.975 (ReLU, 3K atoms), from 0.862 to 0.947 (TopK, 3K), and at 9K atoms from 0.555 to 0.672 (ReLU) and 0.048 to 0.048 (TopK, i.e. no gain where the dictionary itself failed). That last pair is the honest boundary of the argument: fixing coefficients cannot rescue a broken dictionary, so the paper then has to go and fix dictionary learning too.

## What they studied

Two experimental worlds. **Synthetic:** activations generated from a known ground-truth dictionary — d = 768, atoms drawn i.i.d. from an isotropic Gaussian and unit-normalised, coefficients log-normal with expectation 1, ~k = 60 active atoms per sample in the baseline — swept over dictionary width m/d ∈ {4×, 8×, 12×, 16×} (3K to 12K atoms) and atom-selection imbalance as a Zipf tail exponent β ∈ {0, 0.5, 0.7, 1.0}, giving 16 configurations. Each configuration: 10⁶ observations, five epochs, three seeds. **Real:** penultimate-layer activations from CLIP ViT-B/16 and DINOv2-B/14 (class and spatial tokens, one epoch over ~1.2M ImageNet images) and layer-9 activations from GPT-2 Small and Pythia-160M (500M FineWeb tokens, context 1024); all four have activation dimension 768. Sweep: expansion ∈ {4×, 8×, 16×} × target sparsity ℓ₀ ≈ {32, 64, 128} = nine settings per backbone, three seeds each, so 36 configurations across four backbones and 108 trained dictionaries plus baselines.

Baselines are the current zoo at matched settings: ReLU SAE, TopK, BatchTopK, Matryoshka, MP-SAE (matching pursuit), iSAE. Note what this lineup includes: MP-SAE is *also* iterative, so the paper's claim cannot be "iteration is what helps" — it has to be about which iterative procedure and what it optimises.

## How they did it

Replace the encoder with per-sample sparse coding, then patch the two failure modes that appear when you do that naively.

1. **Sparse coding instead of an encoder.** For each activation x, solve argmin over a ≥ 0 of ½‖x − W_dec a − b_dec‖² + λ‖a‖₁ with FISTA. Crucially, this happens *inside* the training loop, once per minibatch, under the current dictionary — so the dictionary is updated from coefficients that are actually optimal for it, not from an encoder's guess.
2. **Refitting.** An ℓ₁ penalty shrinks surviving coefficients, not just zeroes the others. So after FISTA identifies the support (which atoms are on), they re-solve *unpenalised* least squares restricted to that support, using projected gradient descent initialised at the penalised solution, constraining non-negativity and support membership. Magnitudes get corrected; the atom set is frozen. This is the standard post-selection refitting trick from sparse estimation, and the ablation shows it is not cosmetic: without it, sparse coding alone on Pythia-160M needs λ = 3.0 instead of 1.5 and ends with 9,767 dead atoms out of 12,288 and R² = 0.69.
3. **Co-active similarity regulariser.** Per-sample coding makes several atoms converge on nearly the same direction. Rather than penalising all pairwise atom similarity (which fights legitimate semantic proximity), they penalise only the *most similar pair among atoms active on the same sample*, with a margin μ so only near-duplicates beyond the margin are pushed: L_sim = mean over matched co-active pairs of (s − μ)². This is a hinge-like contrastive objective applied at the level of what fires together.

Training alternates: infer coefficients, refit them, freeze coefficients and matched pairs, then take the gradient step on W_dec and b_dec under L_rec + γ·L_sim. Gradients do not flow through the coding or the pair selection. Cost is real and honestly reported: fixed T = 30 FISTA and T_refit = 5 refit iterations; at 16× expansion, per-token inference is 8.22 µs vs 0.14 µs for ReLU and 0.39 for iSAE on Pythia (it is cheaper than MP-SAE, which grows with sparsity), and 64–75% of the training step is the sparse-coding itself, with refitting only ~16%.

**How consistency is measured:** decoder cosine similarity (DCS) between dictionaries after Hungarian matching, and mean coefficient correlation (MCC) under the same matching — computed *between independently trained seeds* rather than against ground truth, and averaged over seed pairs. Against ground truth the same two metrics measure recovery. The paper also adds a third quantity that most consistency papers omit: **usable atom fraction**, the share of atoms that are neither dead (never fire) nor duplicated (point in near-identical decoder directions), because a dictionary can look consistent simply by spending many atoms on the same direction.

## What counts as an explanation here

A dictionary plus a per-sample code, with a **stated claim about the dictionary's stability as an object**. The explanatory target has shifted from "what does feature j mean" to "would feature j exist at all if you reran this". That is a precondition for the first kind of claim rather than a substitute for it, and the paper is clear about it: stability is necessary for a dictionary to function as a public readout, not sufficient for it to be true.

What is genuinely new relative to a standard SAE paper is the recovery protocol. The synthetic world supplies a ground truth, the real world does not; so they first show that **cross-seed consistency tracks ground-truth recovery** in the synthetic sweep (dictionary consistency vs recovery: Pearson r = 0.90, Spearman ρ = 0.88; coefficient consistency vs coefficient recovery: r = 0.99, ρ = 0.98) and then, and only then, use consistency as the proxy on real activations. That is a cleaner instrumental-variable argument than most interpretability papers make, and the failure mode of the argument is worth stating: the correlation is measured across *methods at different failure levels*, so it establishes that consistency is informative when methods are broken; it does not establish that among two methods that are both near recovery, higher consistency means closer to the truth.

## How they judged success

Three tiers, in increasing order of how much they matter.

- **Recovery in the synthetic ground-truth world** (the strong test): DCS and MCC against the true dictionary after Hungarian matching, across all 16 (width × imbalance) configurations.
- **Consistency in the real world** (the proxy): cross-seed DCS and MCC, three seeds, nine settings per backbone, averaged across backbones — plus atom-level distributions (mean decoder cosine to matched atoms in the other two seeds) rather than only aggregate means.
- **Guard rails against gaming the proxy**: usable-atom fraction (dead + duplicate accounting), sparse-probing performance under matched budgets (so consistency is not bought by smearing information across atoms), reconstruction R², and an ablation that removes each component at matched ℓ₀.

The sparse-probing test is the one that keeps the paper honest: a method could look maximally consistent by learning a small, boring, high-variance-free set of atoms that carries little class information. They check the opposite: top-1 and top-5 latent probes on six vision datasets (AUPRC) and 113 binary SAE-Probes tasks (AUROC). ConSDL at 16× / ℓ₀ ≈ 128 sits at 0.52 (CLIP @1) vs 0.49 for MP-SAE and 0.46 for TopK, 0.73 (Pythia @1) vs 0.72 TopK, 0.74 (GPT-2 @1) tied with ReLU and iSAE — best or near-best in several columns, never a collapse. Interpretation: consistency did not cost information density.

## What they found

**Synthetic recovery is near-total, and the baselines' failure is configuration-dependent.** Across all 16 settings, ConSDL recovers ground truth at 0.92–1.00 DCS and 0.90–1.00 MCC, including the regimes where every baseline breaks. The breakdown is stark and worth recording because it explains contradictory literature: at β = 1.0 with 12K atoms, ReLU reaches R² = −0.30 and TopK DCS = 0.18/MCC = 0.10; at 3K atoms with β = 0, ReLU and TopK are essentially fine (DCS 0.98, MCC 0.83–0.86). So "SAEs recover known features" and "SAEs fail to recover known features" are both true, conditional on dictionary width and concept-frequency imbalance — the two axes that real foundation-model activations are extreme on. MP-SAE is the interesting baseline: it matches everyone at low imbalance and then collapses (12K/β = 1.0: DCS 0.20, MCC 0.06, 780 duplicate atoms).

**On real activations the gains are real but much smaller.** Averaged over four backbones: DCS 0.84 vs baseline average 0.74 (+17.6%) and MCC 0.78 vs 0.66 (+35.9%). Against the *strongest* single baseline the margin shrinks to +1.9% DCS and +13.3% MCC. Per-backbone DCS margins over best are +4.8% (CLIP), +1.6% (DINOv2), **−0.8% (Pythia)**, +2.3% (GPT-2) — i.e. on Pythia it does not beat the best baseline on dictionary consistency at all, tying MP-SAE and ReLU at 0.85. The consistent, and much larger, win is in coefficient consistency (MCC): +16.8%, +14.1%, +1.1%, +9.6% over best. That is the right shape for their theory (they fixed coefficient estimation), and it is a meaningfully weaker headline than the abstract's framing.

**Ablation: both components are load-bearing, and neither alone is enough.** On CLIP 16×/ℓ₀ ≈ 128: sparse coding alone gives DCS 0.84 but 9,270 duplicate atoms; + refitting gives DCS 0.85 with 5,564 duplicates; + similarity regulariser instead gives 56 duplicates but DCS 0.70; full gives DCS 0.78, 1 duplicate, zero dead. On Pythia: coding alone is broken (9,767 dead, DCS 0.29), refitting fixes it (DCS 0.84, 3,191 duplicates), full gives DCS 0.81, 0 duplicates, 0 dead. The tell is in Fig. 16 as described: deduplicating the +Refitting dictionary at threshold 0.6 removes 41% (CLIP) and 29% (Pythia) of atoms and drops DCS from 0.85 → 0.78 and 0.84 → 0.79. **So most of the apparent consistency advantage of the no-regulariser variant was near-duplicate atoms agreeing with themselves.** That single number is the paper's most useful contribution to method evaluation.

**Consistency extends to dictionary structure, and to finer granularity.** Clustering atoms by decoder direction and comparing cluster sizes across seeds: rank correlation 0.95 for ConSDL vs 0.22 for Matryoshka (CLIP 16×/128). Normalised centroid agreement 1.00, 0.96, 0.94, 0.88 at 192/48/12/3 atoms per cluster, with the gap to baselines widening as clusters get finer. On concept granularity: CLIP 16× yields ~9K atoms with cross-seed cosine > 0.7 vs 4K for ReLU; single ReLU atoms ("beer bottle", "Golden Retriever", "baseball", "plant") match *several* ConSDL atoms with decoder cosine 0.65–0.75 to the parent but ~0.5 pairwise between themselves, partially overlapping image-level activations (Jaccard 0.32–0.57). This is absorption/splitting seen from the healthy side — the split atoms are the consistent ones.

## Limits and open problems they name

Models are small by necessity (160M–90M visual backbones); the whole 3-seed × 9-setting × 7-method grid is why. No claim past billions of parameters or much larger dictionaries. Consistency on real activations (DCS ~0.84) is far below synthetic (up to 1.00), which they attribute to concept count, frequency imbalance, and — importantly — to real activations violating the "sparse linear combination of concept directions" assumption altogether, citing work suggesting some concepts have manifold rather than linear structure. ConSDL is therefore explicitly a better estimator *within* the linear-sparse frame, not a test of that frame. Training is 6–60× more expensive than a single-pass SAE depending on setting; they call for faster solvers.

Limits I am adding, marked as mine: (i) **Cross-seed consistency is validated as a proxy using exactly the correlation a broken baseline produces.** With methods spanning MCC 0.01–0.97 in the synthetic sweep, r = 0.99 is nearly inevitable; the informative test would be the correlation *restricted to the top quartile* of methods, where the discrimination actually matters for the real-activation claims — and that restricted correlation is not reported. (ii) **The real-world evaluation never checks what the atoms mean.** There is no human or automated labelling of ConSDL atoms' semantics; consistency is established, meaning is assumed. A dictionary of consistent nonsense scores perfectly here, and the sparse-probing test only rules out *class-relevant* nonsense. (iii) **The Pythia DCS tie is buried in Table 8's per-backbone rows** while the abstract quotes only the four-backbone average; a reader taking the abstract's 17.6% would overestimate the method's dictionary-level advantage on language models, where it is essentially zero against the best baseline. (iv) **The marginal cost of consistency is reported per-token but not per-experiment**, and the practical objection is real: the thing that made the previous generation usable at scale was that fitting a dictionary costs one encoder pass over the corpus. ConSDL makes every dictionary fit ~7–60× more expensive, which changes which research questions are affordable, not just how long they take. (v) **One hyperparameter set per configuration, tuned on the same setting it is reported on** — they show sensitivity across λ and T_refit (Fig. 17) which mitigates this, but the selected configuration is still the one highlighted. (vi) The refitting step is doing more work than its 16% cost share suggests; the paper demonstrates this rather than explaining it, and there is no analysis of *why* shrinking-biased coefficients destabilise the dictionary specifically — the mechanistic story ("suboptimal coefficients inject variance") is plausible and untested.

## What we take from it

**The deduplication check is a free audit we should apply to every consistency number in this folder.** Fig. 16's result — that removing 41% of atoms as near-duplicates collapses a method's DCS from 0.85 to 0.78 — is the cheapest available test for whether a reported consistency is real or is atoms agreeing with their own copies. Any entry in this folder that reports cross-seed or cross-method similarity should be asked, alongside it: how many of the agreeing units are duplicates, and does the number survive deduplication at a stated threshold? This is the same class of error as counting a feature twice because it split, which entry arxiv-2608.20134 warned about from the migration side.

**The "amortization gap" is a name for something we keep tripping over.** A learned encoder is an approximation to an argmin, and the approximation error is not noise — it is biased in the direction the sparsity penalty pushes. If we ever probe with a learned readout and then conclude something about the geometry of features, we are partly reading the readout's error. The two-step protocol (fix the dictionary, re-solve for coefficients, compare) is 20 lines of code and tells us whether a negative result is about the model or about our estimator. It also retro-explains the folder's recurring complaint that probes and dictionaries disagree: disagreements may be estimation artefacts, not model structure.

**Consistency and meaning are orthogonal and the field conflates them.** This paper's strongest evidence is that atoms recur across seeds (0.84 DCS, 0.95 cluster-size rank correlation), and its weakest link is that nobody checked what they say. Our own write-ups should be explicit about which of the two a given claim rests on. The useful formulation: a stable dictionary is a *precondition* for a semantic claim, and a semantic claim with no stability check is one seed deep.

**The configuration-dependence result is the most transferable finding, and it is not about their method.** SAE recovery is fine at 3K atoms and uniform concept frequencies, and catastrophic at 12K atoms with β = 1.0. Any claim of the form "SAEs do/do not recover ground-truth features" is under-specified without those two coordinates. When we meet a paper reporting SAE failure, the first question is now: at what width and what imbalance — and if the authors do not say, the failure may be a property of their sweep corner.

**A cheap architectural suggestion this enables:** since sparse coding's cost is dominated by a fixed 30 FISTA iterations, and since refitting (5 iterations, 16% of cost) accounts for most of the stability gain, the obvious next experiment — theirs to run, but cheap for anyone — is a hybrid where the encoder is trained amortized and then used only to *initialise* FISTA, cutting the iteration count. Listed here as an open door, not a result.
