## What they wanted, and why

Model diffing treats the difference between a base model and its fine-tuned descendant as the object of study, on the argument that fine-tuning changes far less than pre-training, so the difference should be much smaller than the model itself and therefore tractable. The crosscoder is the natural instrument: a single sparse dictionary shared across both models, with one decoder direction per latent per model, so a concept can be classified as shared, present only in the base, or newly present after tuning. The paper's problem is that the standard training loss makes that last class unreliable. Their goal is to show that the "emerged during fine-tuning" reading of a zero base-side direction is frequently a loss artifact, to supply a cheap measurement that catches it, and then to show what an artifact-free dictionary actually finds in Gemma 2 2B's chat tune. The framing that matters for us is that they are diagnosing a *measurement* that the whole subfield had been reading as a *finding*.

## What they studied

Two model pairs in the main text (Gemma 2 2B base and Gemma 2 2B-it), one layer (the middle layer, 13 of 26), and one layer of the residual stream as the unit of analysis, decomposed into 73,728 latents at expansion factor 32 — asserted to be about 100 active latents per token (L0) in both dictionary variants so the comparison is matched. Training activations were 100M tokens split between web text and chat data. The extension pools are wider than the main text: Llama 3.2 1B and Llama 3.1 8B chat pairs, plus two narrower tuning runs (a medical tune of Gemma 2 2B-it and an RL reasoning tune of a Qwen 1.5B), and a re-analysis of an independently trained L1 crosscoder from other authors. The unit of analysis is therefore a *pair* of decoder directions and their relative norms — a per-concept comparison between two models, not a single model's features.

## How they did it

Train a crosscoder on the concatenated activations of base and chat, so each latent has one shared activation and two model-specific decoder vectors; then read "is this concept new to the chat model" off the relative decoder norm difference, which they bin into base-only, shared and chat-only. The paper's contribution is to argue, from the loss, that a near-zero base decoder norm does not imply the concept is absent from the base model, and offers two mechanisms. *Complete Shrinkage*: because the L1 penalty is charged on the decoder norms themselves, a concept that fires on a minority of base tokens can have its base direction pushed to zero and be reconstructed only in the residual error. *Latent Decoupling*: a shared concept may be carried by one chat-only latent and by a *different* combination of base-only latents, which costs the same under this penalty, so the concept is present in both models but split across two latents that each look model-specific. Their appendix works the algebra for a toy case where a shared-plus-exclusive concept can be represented at equal loss either as three latents (shared, chat-only, base-only) or as two (chat-only and base-only), and only an L0-style penalty breaks the tie toward three. The diagnostic, *Latent Scaling*, projects the activations onto each candidate direction and solves for the single scalar that best reconstructs the other model's activations — a one-parameter least-squares fit with a closed form — then takes the ratio of that fitted scale in the base to the scale in the chat model. They compute the ratio once against the activations and once against the two terms of the decomposition (reconstruction and error), so that shrinkage and decoupling land in different columns: a spuriously chat-only latent that was shrunk shows its base-side information in the error term, and one that was decoupled shows it in the reconstruction. They then swap the sparsity mechanism for top-k selection over a batch and repeat everything. Causality is measured by building a hybrid: take the base activation at the chosen layer, add the chat decoder's contribution for a chosen set of latents, run the rest of the *chat* model's forward pass from there, and measure the KL divergence to the true chat distribution — so a set of latents is credited only insofar as reinstating it closes the behavioural gap, with the empty set and the full set as brackets and the residual-error arm as the honest ceiling. Ranked-by-chat-specificity halves are compared against each other, and one arm replaces activations only on chat template tokens.

## What counts as an explanation here

A model difference expressed as a sparse bidirectional dictionary: a list of concepts, each with a pair of directions and a verdict (base-only, shared, chat-only), plus a scalar per latent saying how much of it is genuinely confined to one model. It is partial and comparative rather than predictive of the whole model — the things predicted are (a) whether the fine-tuned model's behaviour can be reconstituted from the base plus a small set of directions, measured in KL, and (b) which latents are causally responsible for that gap. The strongest form of the claim in the paper is not the dictionary but the null it kills: "this concept is new to the chat model" is downgraded from a property of the network to a property of one loss function, and the paper shows the distinction is measurable with a single regression coefficient.

## How they judged success

Five instruments, nested. (1) The relative decoder norm, as the original method would use it. (2) The Latent Scaling ratio between models, with a matching ratio computed on the reconstruction term and on the error term, plus a Pearson correlation between the norm metric and the scaling metrics to show when the cheap metric tracks the expensive one and when it does not. (3) A KL-divergence causality score from the hybrid-patching construction, with empty-set and full-set brackets, a residual-error arm, top-half versus bottom-half of each ranking, and 95% confidence intervals over 512 chat interactions, reported separately over full responses and over the first nine response tokens. (4) Auto-interpretability: a language-model explanation and a detection score against *semantically similar* non-activating examples rather than random ones, which the authors note makes it a strictly harder test. (5) A matched-count comparison — take the same number of candidate chat-only latents from both architectures and count how many pass a range of thresholds — so the two architectures are compared at equal dictionary budget rather than at equal nominal strictness. The replications in the appendix reuse the whole pipeline on the Llama pairs and on the two narrow fine-tunes.

## What they found

The artifact is large and it is systematic. Under the norm metric the L1 crosscoder reports 3,176 chat-only latents against the top-k crosscoder's 134 on the same models and layer; of the L1 candidates, 2,132 (67.1%) fail the specificity test, and the corresponding counts are 1,210 of 2,442 (49.5%) on Llama 3.1 8B, 1,071 of 1,844 (58.1%) on Llama 3.2 1B, 58 of 59 (98.3%) on the Qwen reasoning tune and 235 of 246 (95.5%) on the medical tune, while the top-k crosscoder's false count is 1 of 134 on Gemma and zero on the two narrow tunes. Shrinkage is visible as error ratios for L1 chat-only latents reaching about 0.5, and decoupling as reconstruction-ratio distributions for chat-only and shared latents that overlap under L1 and are cleanly separated under top-k — with the appendix adding that pairs of chat-only and base-only L1 latents exist at cosine similarity 1, exactly the split the toy argument predicts. The cheap metric is therefore architecture-conditional: in the top-k dictionary the norm difference correlates with the scaling ratios at Pearson 0.73 (reconstruction side) and 0.87 (error side), and the top half by that metric cuts KL by about 50% on the first nine tokens against about 6% for the bottom half, while in the L1 dictionary the same ranking is uninformative or inverted. Causality also bounds the whole enterprise: replacing all latents lowers KL by about 59% over full responses and about 78% over the first nine tokens, and the residual-error arm beats the dictionary under full responses but does more than twice as badly on the early tokens — so the dictionary carries early-token framing better than long-generation content, and the reconstruction error is not a nuisance term. Late in the paper the authors find that 40% of the top-k chat-only latents fire predominantly on template tokens, and that patching only template-token positions reaches a KL of 0.239 (full response) and 0.507 (first nine tokens), equal to or slightly better than the best half of the ranked latents — most of what chat-tuning changes, on this measurement, is concentrated in the role markers rather than spread through content.

## Limits and open problems they name

One model family in the main text, one middle layer, small models, and hyperparameters tuned per model (the top-k variant needed different initialisation norms and a k-annealing schedule on the Llama 8B model to avoid dead latents) — the authors restrict their causal and interpretability claims to the setting they ran rather than generalising. They analysed only the chat-only class and left base-only and shared latents largely untouched. The residual error still carries a great deal of chat behaviour, so the dictionary is not a complete account even under its own best case. Most importantly, they state that no crosscoder can distinguish a latent genuinely learned during tuning from one that already existed and merely shifted its activation pattern, which is the distinction the "new concept" claim wants and does not get. They also raise the uncomfortable possibility that Latent Scaling alone finds chat-specific latents, which would make the dictionary optional for the classification question.

## What we take from it

- **A method: the one-scalar-projection test as a self-check on any "this thing exists in A and not in B" claim.** Their diagnostic is a single least-squares coefficient — how much of the other object's signal this direction can reconstruct when you are allowed to rescale it — and it costs nothing compared with training the dictionary. For us the transfer is direct: any direction, probe or feature we claim is present in one checkpoint and absent in another should be scored by its best-fit scale against the other checkpoint's activations, and the ratio printed. This is cheaper than a causal test and it catches the specific failure of a representation being present but unrepresentable by the arm we fitted.
- **A warning, and the sharpest one in the folder so far: the sparsity penalty itself manufactures the "emerged during fine-tuning" result.** The mechanism is not a data problem but a regulariser whose extra inductive bias is visible in one line of algebra — penalising the two model-specific norms with a sum of square roots charges a shared concept twice and a split concept once, so the dictionary prefers to split. Every architecture whose loss is charged per-model (crosscoders, and by extension any paired or comparative SAE) should be checked for this before its absent-side counts are reported. Three successive reductions in apparent novelty (3,176 → 1,132 false → 134) were all changes of the loss and the threshold, none of them changes to the model.
- **A measure: say explicitly which decision the read-out is asked to support.** Their norm metric is a perfectly good read-out under one architecture and "merely a training artifact" under another, with the *same* data, layer and latent budget. So a read-out's validity here is not a property of the quantity but of the pair (quantity, training procedure). Practically: whenever we report a statistic that was validated in someone else's setup — an L0, a reconstruction fidelity, a cosine — we should carry the same statistic measured on a deliberately mismatched control we train ourselves, rather than citing the validated number as though it travelled.
- **A framing for our own work: the base-plus-a-set construction is the read-out we want and mostly do not build.** Adding specific directions to the source model's activation and completing the forward pass in the target model turns "does this set of features account for the difference" into a KL number with an honest bracket — the full set is the ceiling, the residual error is the competitor, and the empty set is the floor. Two features of the design are worth copying verbatim: the residual-error arm, because within-dictionary reconstruction is not the same as complete reconstruction; and the split report over early versus late output tokens, because a dictionary can account for how a response is framed while accounting for almost nothing about its content, and a single averaged number hides the inversion.
- **A result to keep in mind when attributing chat behaviour to content features: the template tokens carry a surprising share of the change.** 40% of the chat-only latents fire mostly on role markers, and patching nothing but those positions matches the best half of the ranked latents. For us this is a caution about unit choice — if the behaviour we are trying to read out is largely located in a small set of positional/syntactic anchors, a dictionary fitted to understand content will show it as a diffuse property of many features while the anchors do the work.
