# Network Dissection: Quantifying Interpretability of Deep Visual Representations

- **Key:** arxiv-1704.05796
- **Authors / organisation:** David Bau, Bolei Zhou (equal contribution), Aditya Khosla, Aude Oliva, Antonio Torralba — CSAIL, MIT
- **Date:** 2017-04 (v1 2017-04-19; CVPR 2017 oral)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.05796
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Individual convolutional units in trained image networks had been observed to act like recognisable object, part and scene detectors even though nothing in training asked for that, which suggested networks might be spontaneously learning representations whose variables line up with human concepts. The authors wanted to find out whether that alignment is real and measurable, or whether it is an artefact of looking at whatever basis the network happened to learn — earlier work had claimed there is no meaningful difference between a single high-level unit and a random linear combination of units. To settle it they built an automatic, dataset-wide scoring procedure for unit-versus-concept alignment, and then used it as an instrument to compare architectures, training objectives and regularisers. The larger goal is a measurable notion of representation quality that is not just predictive accuracy, and their instrument is borrowed conceptually from single-neuron recording in neuroscience.

## What they studied
Units: every individual convolutional filter in every convolutional layer, scored one at a time; layer-level interpretability is the count of distinct concepts matched anywhere in that layer. Models: AlexNet, GoogLeNet, VGG-16 and ResNet-152, trained from scratch on ImageNet (objects, 1.2M images, 1000 classes), Places205 (scenes, 2.4M images, 205 categories), Places365 (scenes, 1.6M images, 365 categories) and a combined ImageNet-plus-Places365 hybrid, plus an untrained random-weight AlexNet and ten self-supervised AlexNet-based models (context prediction, jigsaw solving, ego-motion, learning-by-moving, video frame order, tracking, object-centric alignment, colorisation, cross-channel prediction, ambient-sound prediction). Data: a new pixel-labelled corpus assembled for the paper.

## How they did it
The scoring dataset, Broden, merges five densely annotated sources (ADE scenes and objects, Pascal-Context objects, Pascal-Part parts, OpenSurfaces materials, DTD textures) into concept classes normalised to single English words, drops classes with fewer than ten images, and adds eleven colour-name labels computed for every pixel; that yields 468 scene, 584 object, 234 part, 32 material, 47 texture and 11 colour classes. For a given network and unit, the activation map on every Broden image is upsampled to annotation resolution by bilinear interpolation anchored at receptive-field centres, and thresholded at a per-unit quantile chosen so that 0.5% of all spatial locations in the dataset exceed it; the resulting binary mask is treated as a segmentation prediction for every concept and scored by dataset-wide intersection-over-union, summed only over the images that carry at least one label of that concept's category. A unit counts as a detector for its best-scoring concept above an IoU of 0.04, and a layer's interpretability is the number of distinct concepts detected in it. To test the basis question they compose a Places205-trained AlexNet's conv5 with a uniformly drawn random rotation, applied either whole or at fractional powers along a geodesic from the identity, which changes the coordinates while leaving every downstream prediction identical because the next layer can absorb the inverse rotation. They also validate the labels against human raters on Mechanical Turk, sweep training iterations up to 2.4M, retrain from three random initialisations, and train variants with no dropout, with batch normalisation in every convolution, and with a widened conv5 (256 to 768 units) followed by global average pooling.

## What counts as an explanation here
A labelled dictionary of the form "unit *k* detects concept *c*", mined automatically from a pool of human-named concepts, plus a scalar score for how many such labels a whole layer supports. It is explicitly partial and explicitly not a claim about mechanism: the authors measure alignment between single variables and single concepts, say that emergent concepts often live in combinations of units in interior layers, and treat the unit-concept match as evidence of partial disentanglement rather than as a causal account. Prediction is not the point — the paper states that two representations can be equally predictive and unequally interpretable, and its whole set of results depends on that gap.

## How they judged success
The measure is IoU averaged over the dataset between a unit's thresholded activation mask and a concept's ground-truth mask, with the detector threshold (0.04) fixed and a sensitivity claim that relative orderings are stable across thresholds. Layer interpretability is the number of unique concept labels in a layer, reported both for unique concepts and for the total number of detector units. The key control is the rotation arm, where the rotated representation has exactly the same discriminative power, so any drop in detector count is attributable to basis and not to information. Human evaluation is a raters' yes/no judgement of whether a phrase describes the most-activating patches, separated into a set of units where raters agreed with an earlier hand-labelled set and a human-consistency baseline produced by a second rater group on those same hand labels. Architecture and training comparisons are across model families at the last convolutional layer, and the relationship to discriminative power is measured by a linear SVM on action40 with a fixed regulariser (C = 0.001) trained on each representation. Interpretability is also correlated against top-1 validation accuracy of the source classifiers.

## What they found
Humans rate the automatically assigned labels as descriptive on 37% of agreed-interpretable units in the first convolutional layer, rising through 56%, 54% and 59% in the next three, to 71% in the fifth — against a human-consistency baseline of 82%, 76%, 83%, 82% and 91% at the same layers, so the method is closest to human annotators where concepts are most nameable. A full random rotation of Places conv5 removes 80% of the unique detectors, and partial rotations of intermediate strength sit in between, which rejects the "any direction is as interpretable as a unit" hypothesis — interpretability is axis-aligned, earned by learning, and not implied by or required for predictive power. Layer structure matches intuition: colour and texture detectors dominate the first two layers and object and part detectors appear in the fifth; object and part detectors begin appearing at around 10,000 iterations of 256-image batches, with no evidence of units passing through texture or material phases first. Across architectures the ordering of interpretability is ResNet-152, then VGG-16, then GoogLeNet, then AlexNet, with the scene trained variant of each ahead of the object-trained one; across training objectives, Places365 gives the most unique detectors, while self-supervised models are heavy on textures and thin on objects — the colorisation model, trained on greyscale inputs, produces almost no colour detectors — which supports the authors' hypothesis that emergent units track what the training task requires. On training conditions: three retrainings from different random initialisations land at similar detector counts despite near-identical 50.0% top-1 accuracy, removing dropout (49.2% accuracy) yields more texture and fewer object detectors, and batch normalisation (50.5% accuracy, the highest of the group) reduces interpretability markedly, conjectured to be because whitening lets the network rotate intermediate bases freely. Widening conv5 to 768 units at a cost of about half a point of top-1 accuracy produces far more detectors, but widening further to 1024 and 2048 units does not add significantly more unique concepts. Object-detector count correlates positively with action40 transfer accuracy, though the best transfer representation is not the one with the most object detectors.

## Limits and open problems they name
The instrument is bounded by its concept list: a unit aligned with something Broden does not contain cannot score, and the authors say Broden will be extended. The scoring is restricted by construction to single-unit, single-concept alignment, so multi-unit concepts and distributed codes are outside its remit. The width study is called preliminary, and the saturation at wider layers is offered as ambiguous — either a capacity limit of the architecture or a limit on how many disentangled concepts help the scene task. Batch normalisation's interpretability loss is left without a remedy, and finding a way to keep its training benefits without the loss is named as future work. The basis-rotation result is reported for one layer of one model. There is no causal test of any label: no unit is ablated or steered to check that the named concept is what the network uses.

## What we take from it
A measurement template — a fixed concept pool, a per-unit threshold calibrated to a fixed activation quantile, and a single IoU-valued score computed over the whole probe set — that makes two accounts of the same architecture directly comparable, and the ancestor of the folder's coverage-over-a-concept-set idea. The rotation arm is the cheapest possible control on any basis-dependent claim and is one our own accounts have never run: re-express the representation in a random orthogonal basis, re-derive the account, and report how much survives; if the answer is "most of it", the account was reading a property of the model's information rather than of how it organised it. The training-condition results are a warning against treating accuracy as a proxy for structure — batch normalisation raises a model's accuracy and cuts its interpretability on the same data — so any account of what a network learned should be reported beside an equally-trained variant that differs in exactly one training choice. Most important for us: this paper defines interpretability as an axis-aligned property and scores it without ever intervening, which is precisely the diagnostic the folder's later entries on illusion and dormant directions show to be insufficient — a unit that segments a concept under a threshold need not be a unit the network uses when it predicts, so a Network-Dissection number is an upper bound on structure, not evidence of it.
