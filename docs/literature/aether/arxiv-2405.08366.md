## What they wanted, and why

Sparse autoencoders are the field's default tool for turning a model's activations into a list of nameable features, but nothing in that list can be checked against the truth, because the unknown "true" features are exactly what the SAE was trained to find. The authors want a *task-scoped* substitute: on a task whose inputs can be described by a handful of labelled attributes, one can hand-build a dictionary in which each feature corresponds one-to-one to a value of an attribute, and that dictionary can then be used as a reference point ("skyline", in their words) against which an unsupervised dictionary is priced. The larger goal is an evaluation that is objective enough to compare SAE variants, and specifically a *causal* one — does the dictionary let you edit the model's behaviour and predict what the edit does — rather than a counting exercise over descriptions. They are also after a diagnosis: if SAEs fail on a task, they want the failure to have a name and a mechanism rather than just a lower score.

## What they studied

GPT-2 Small on the indirect object identification (IOI) task, at all 26 attention-head locations of the published IOI circuit and at named cross-sections of that circuit (name-mover outputs, name-mover queries and keys at the final token, and the S-inhibition / duplicate-token / induction outputs). The unit of analysis is a *feature dictionary at one attention-head location*: decoder directions plus non-negative coefficients. Three dictionary families are compared — supervised dictionaries built from labelled attributes, SAEs trained on the task distribution, and SAEs trained on OpenWebText — plus a control family of task SAEs whose decoder directions are frozen at initialisation. A toy model (random isotropic features with matched norms) and a mixture-of-Gaussians toy model carry the two qualitative results.

## How they did it

Step one is to parameterise each IOI prompt by three attributes: the indirect-object name (216 single-token names), the subject name, and the position pattern (IO first, or second). Step two builds the supervised dictionary: subtract the empirical mean activation and add, per prompt, one vector per attribute value, each vector being the mean activation over prompts sharing that value. They compute these from 20,000 prompts and validate on 5,000 held out. Step three applies three tests to every dictionary, using the supervised one as the reference. **Test 1 (sufficiency / necessity)**: replace activations in a cross-section with their dictionary reconstructions and read the logit difference as a fraction of the clean value (sufficiency); or replace activations with the mean plus the reconstruction residue and compare the resulting drop to a plain mean ablation (necessity). **Test 2 (sparse controllability)**: for a prompt and a counterfactual prompt differing only in the attribute to be edited, greedily pick at most k features active in the reconstruction of the first to subtract and at most k features taken from the reconstruction of the second to add, minimising the L2 distance to the counterfactual activation; score an edit by agreement between the resulting next-token prediction and the prediction under the ground-truth intervention that patches the counterfactual activation in, and contextualise it by the fraction of reconstruction weight the edit removes (0 = the weight a supervised one-feature swap removes, 1 = the whole activation). This test deliberately never looks at a feature description. **Test 3 (interpretability)**: score every feature against candidate binary predicates over prompts (a name, a name-at-a-position, a name union, a gender label) by F1, keep the best, and then check the assignment causally by keeping only high-F1 features (sufficiency) or removing only high-F1 features (necessity), and by restricting test 2's edits to top-F1 features for the attribute. Task SAEs use a 16x expansion (1024 features over 64-dimensional head outputs), batch 1024, learning rate 1e-3, a sweep over L1 coefficients in {0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.3}, dead-neuron resets; full-distribution SAEs use 8192 features, 250M OpenWebText activations, batch 2048. Hyperparameters are chosen per location on the trade-off between active features per example and logit difference recovered against mean ablation.

## What counts as an explanation here

A feature dictionary: an over-complete set of directions, with an assigned human-readable predicate per feature where one scores well, plus a statement about what each feature *does* under intervention. The form is explicitly hybrid — a description (the F1-scored predicate) that is only credited after a causal check (subtract it, add it, see whether the behaviour moves as the description says). It is partial by construction and says so: the authors state that a dictionary captures the task-relevant slice of an activation, not the activation's full content, and that a supervised dictionary is not the only valid decomposition — they work through several alternative parameterisations (name coupled with position, per-gender vectors plus per-name corrections, pairs of names in a cycle, and a worst case with one feature per attribute combination) and note that some of these would pass their tests while looking nothing like the supervised one. Explanation and prediction are separated: at feature granularity the authors decompose attention scores into pairwise dot products between query and key features, and head-to-head composition into per-feature direct effects, which is the paper's clearest predictive object — a feature-level attribution graph in which most interactions are near zero.

## How they judged success

Sufficiency: logit difference under reconstruction, normalised by the un-intervened value. Necessity: a rescaled mismatch between the reconstruction-removal drop and the mean-ablation drop, so 1 means the reconstruction's residue is as task-irrelevant as the mean. Controllability: fraction of prompts where the edit predicts the same token as the ground-truth counterfactual patch, reported alongside the no-intervention value (because on some cross-sections doing nothing already scores high) and alongside edit weight. Interpretability: F1 against candidate predicates, with the stated guarantee that an F1 of a implies precision and recall both at least a/(2-a) (at 0.8, roughly 0.67), and a caveat that F1 ignores activation magnitude and is harsh on high-precision/low-recall features. Controls: the frozen-decoder SAE arm is the key one — same training data, same sparsity machinery, but random directions. Measures are single-task and mostly read off figures rather than tabulated.

## What they found

The supervised dictionaries pass all three tests: as few as 3 active features per activation reconstruct well, editing an attribute is a single one-feature swap, and edits agree with the ground-truth intervention more than 80% of the time on every cross-section except the subject attribute, where the no-intervention baseline already agrees often enough to compress the range. Task SAEs approximate worse but not catastrophically; full-distribution SAEs approximate notably worse and need far more features to edit (often 32 or more, and sometimes even 32 fails), with edit weight approaching the whole activation. Both SAE families do produce high-F1 interpretable features, and those features agree with the circuit roles reported by Wang et al. for every head; the authors add several observations the original circuit paper did not report (a layer-3 duplicate-token head carrying positional features, a single head, L7H3, holding most of the gender features, keys encoding position-plus-name where values encode the name). High-F1 feature sets are sufficiency- and necessity-useful at a threshold of 0.6, degrade at 0.7-0.8, and — a result they call remarkable — fixing only features above 0.8 while patching everything else from the counterfactual leaves the model predicting the original answer. The frozen-decoder control performs on par with the full-distribution SAEs, i.e. random directions are not obviously worse than internet-trained features for editing this task. Two qualitative failures: **feature occlusion** — at one head’s queries where both name attributes are causally live, SAEs find a feature for almost every indirect-object name but never more than 22 feature for subject names across an 8192-feature, 100,000-example, two-orders-of-magnitude sweep, and the number of subject features rises monotonically as the authors surgically subtract the higher-norm indirect-object features; **feature over-splitting** — a binary position attribute is split into ten or more features covering small, semantically uninterpretable subsets, which survives the seed and dataset change checks, and is reproduced in a two-Gaussian toy model where a many-feature SAE has lower total loss than the ideal two-feature solution for any L1 coefficient in the infinite-data limit.

## Limits and open problems they name

The central limit is the supervision itself: the attributes are a subjective choice, and a different but equally good parameterisation could make a good dictionary look bad ("judging a fish by its ability to climb a tree"). They mitigate this by preferring tests that are agnostic to the exact features, but do not eliminate it. Everything is one task and one model, and no exhaustive hyperparameter tuning was done for the SAEs, so the SAE numbers are not a state-of-the-art estimate. F1 as an interpretability score has the two stated defects. The occlusion and over-splitting results are demonstrated in toy models that only mimic the empirical setup, not proven to be the mechanism in the real model.

## What we take from it

- **Method — a skyline, not a baseline.** For any task we can describe with labelled attributes, build the trivial dictionary (conditional-mean directions per attribute value) first, and report every other read-out as a fraction of that skyline on sufficiency, edit success and F1. It is cheap, it is fully interpretable by construction, and it converts "our dictionary decodes X" into "our dictionary recovers N% of what a dictionary built from X would".
- **Measure — the separated edit axes.** Score an edit by *agreement with the ground-truth counterfactual prediction* and by *weight removed*, never by geometry alone. Our own encoder/decoder read-outs predict a direction; this pair is the test of whether the prediction is right at the right cost.
- **Warning — random directions are the control that killed the strongest SAE result here.** The frozen-decoder arm matched internet-trained SAEs at editing. Any claim that a learned dictionary beats nothing on a task needs the frozen/random arm, on the same sparsity budget, in the same table.
- **Warning — occlusion is the failure mode a dictionary over a causally live attribute will silently have.** A concept can be missing from the list not because it is absent but because a higher-norm neighbour absorbs the capacity; the check is whether the count of features for the missing attribute responds monotonically when you subtract the dominant one.
- **Framing — over-splitting means "how many features for this concept" is not a score.** It can lose to itself under the training objective; the diagnostic is stability of the split across seeds and datasets (present here) versus the ideal-sparse solution being strictly reachable (reproduced absent in a toy).
- **Shortcut — decompose the attention score into feature pairs.** Once query and key are dictionaries, the score is a sum of pairwise dot products, and the near-zero-ness of most of them is a sparsity claim you can print per head. This is the closest thing in the folder to a predictive object built purely out of dictionary entries.
