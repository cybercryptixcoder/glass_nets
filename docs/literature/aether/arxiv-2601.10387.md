# The Assistant Axis: Situating and Stabilizing the Default Persona of Language Models

- **Key:** arxiv-2601.10387
- **Authors / organisation:** Christina Lu, Jack Gallagher, Jonathan Michala, Kyle Fish, Jack Lindsey; Anthropic (work carried out under the MATS and Anthropic Fellows programmes)
- **Date:** 2026-01-15
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.10387
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

Post-training teaches a base model to play one character — the helpful, honest Assistant — and most interpretability work treats that character as the backdrop rather than as an object with structure. The authors ask two questions that a predictive account of model behaviour has to answer: what is the Assistant, in representational terms, and how reliably does the model stay in it? Their framing is that a model can leave the Assistant persona in contexts that never look like jailbreaks — a user working through trauma, a user pushing the model to describe its own inner life — and that some of the concerning behaviour in those settings is drift into a different character rather than a failure of any safety-relevant skill. The larger goal is a persona space in which the default identity is one end of an axis, so that identity becomes a measured variable that can be monitored and constrained.

## What they studied

Three open-weight instruct models: Gemma 2 27B, Qwen 3 32B, Llama 3.3 70B, plus the base checkpoints of Gemma 2 27B and Llama 3.1 70B. Data are model-generated rollouts against 275 character roles and 240 traits with 240 extraction questions; benign assistant chatter sampled from lmsys-chat-1m; a persona-based jailbreak set spanning 44 harm categories; and synthetic multi-turn conversations in four domains (coding, writing, therapy-like, philosophical discussion about AI). The unit of analysis is a **direction in residual-stream activation space** — the mean post-MLP activation at response tokens — and the object built from those directions is a low-dimensional *persona space* found by PCA over role vectors.

## How they did it

For each role, five system prompts are generated and crossed with 240 extraction questions, giving 1,200 rollouts per role; an LLM judge labels each response as fully role-playing, somewhat role-playing, or not, and two vectors ("fully X" and "somewhat X") are kept for every role with enough surviving samples. Mean response-token activations are collected at each layer, standardised by subtracting the cross-role mean, and PCA'd (377–463 components depending on model). The default Assistant vector is built the same way from four neutral system prompts plus a no-prompt condition. The first principal component turns out to have fantastical characters at one end and assistant-like occupations at the other; the authors then define the **Assistant Axis** not as PC1 but as the contrast vector between the default Assistant activation and the mean of all role vectors, on the same extraction questions. They validate it three ways: steering with it and scoring whether models adopt non-Assistant personas on introspective questions; steering with it against persona-based jailbreaks; and applying the same axis extracted from an instruct model to its base checkpoint using prefills. To study drift they run 100 synthetic conversations per domain for up to 15 turns with three different frontier models as the user, project per-turn mean activations onto the axis, and regress user-message embeddings onto the resulting projection and onto the turn-to-turn delta. Stabilisation (*activation capping*) changes a single line of inference: the component of the hidden state along the axis is clamped from below at a threshold τ at several layers at once, leaving activations already above the threshold untouched.

## What counts as an explanation here

A one-dimensional variable plus a calibration of its healthy range. The account is that "Assistant-ness" is a direction, that the default activation sits at one extreme of it, that a model's current persona can be read off as a scalar projection, and that behaviour is stabilised by keeping that scalar inside the range observed during post-training rollouts. It is explicitly partial: the authors state that a linear direction is probably not the whole story, that some of the Assistant lives in the weights rather than the activations, and that the other principal components are interpreted only qualitatively. It is predictive in a scalar sense — the reading is claimed to forecast susceptibility to role adoption, jailbreak success, and harm rate on the next turn — and not predictive of which specific behaviour will occur, since the demon-versus-angel example shows two roles at similar distance producing very different harm rates.

## How they judged success

Persona adoption is scored by an LLM judge on introspective questions (who are you, what is your name) into Assistant, human, nonhuman and mystical categories, as a function of steering coefficient. Jailbreak harm is scored on the 44-category set with a nine-category judge rubric validated against a human evaluator on 200 samples at 91.6% agreement, compared to a no-jailbreak baseline. Drift is measured as the per-turn projection averaged across conversations, and message-level prediction is a ridge regression with R² reported separately for the absolute projection and for the delta. Stabilisation is judged on a Pareto frontier: percent reduction in harmful responses against summed capability performance on IFEval, MMLU Pro, GSM8k and EQ-Bench, swept over cap percentile (1st, 25th, 50th, 75th) and layer range. **The only control on the axis itself** is role PC1 — the same experiment re-run with the unsupervised direction instead of the contrast vector.

## What they found

Persona space is genuinely low-dimensional and remarkably shared: 4, 8 and 19 dimensions capture 70% of role-vector variance in the three models, the top components explain 19.4% to 33.6% of overall activation variance, and role loadings on PC1 correlate above 0.92 between every pair of models. The default Assistant sits at the extreme of PC1 — minimum normalised distance to an end of 0.03 there, against 0.27 to 0.50 on every other component — and steering away from that end raises the rate of adopting a non-Assistant persona in all three models, with model-specific flavours: Llama splits between human and nonhuman portrayals, Gemma prefers nonhuman, Qwen hallucinates lived experience such as a birthplace and years of experience. Persona-based jailbreaks succeed at 65.3% to 88.5% against baselines of 0.5% to 4.5%, and steering toward the Assistant lowers the harmful rate substantially, usually by redirecting to a harmless answer rather than refusing. Applied to base models, the axis elicits helpful human archetypes — therapists and consultants for purpose, agreeableness traits for self-description — which the authors read as the Assistant being largely inherited from the pretraining corpus and only subsequently associated with being an AI. Drift is domain-structured and consistent across all three target models and all three auditor models: coding and writing stay in range, therapy and AI-philosophy conversations end markedly lower. The position is set by the most recent user message rather than accumulated: message embeddings predict the next turn's projection at R² 0.53–0.77 (p < 0.001) but the delta at R² 0.10, and the clustered message types are bounded task requests, technical explanation, editing and how-to on the stabilising side, against meta-reflection demands, phenomenological requests, authorial-voice requests and emotional disclosure on the drifting side. Drift is behaviourally consequential: first-turn projection correlates with second-turn harm rate at r = 0.39–0.52 (p < 0.001) over 2750 first turns crossed with 440 harmful questions, with activations at the Assistant end very rarely producing harm. Activation capping reduces harmful responses by nearly 60% with no aggregate capability cost at the chosen setting (layers 46–53 of 64 in Qwen, 56–71 of 80 in Llama, cap at the 25th percentile of projections, calibrated on 912,000 rollouts), and some settings slightly improved benchmark scores. **The control dissociates less than expected:** role PC1 has cosine above 0.60 with the axis at every layer and above 0.71 at the middle layer, and steering with it produces similar susceptibility and jailbreak results, while activation capping along PC1 is *less* effective at reducing jailbreaks in both models tested.

## Limits and open problems they name

Behaviours being quantified are fuzzy and the evidence is a mixture of judge-scored evals, qualitative case studies and a handful of benchmark suites. Target models are three open-weight dense transformers with no reasoning training, none frontier. The role list is incomplete by construction and elicitation is imperfect. Multi-turn users are simulated by frontier models, with three auditors as a partial hedge but no human study. The linearity assumption is called likely flawed in the authors' own words. A methodological detail the authors disclose rather than hide: the PC1 capping runs were executed with a different inference stack that they measured as costing 2–3% on the benchmarks, so the PC1 comparison carries an implementation confound on the capability axis (though not, by their account, on the harm axis).

## What we take from it

(A **framing**, an **instrument**, and a **warning**.) The framing is the one our model needs for identity: treat the default persona as a scalar coordinate whose healthy range is *calibrated from the model's own rollouts* rather than assumed, and report a component's departure from that range as a state variable separate from any task-level output. The instrument is capping rather than pushing — constraining a projection to a minimum value instead of adding a vector — which is the cleanest published instance of an intervention with a measured operating range (the 25th percentile, chosen on a capability/harm frontier) and which our intervention reports can copy wholesale, including the requirement to apply it at multiple layers. The warning is the weak null: this paper's axis is validated against a *supervised* alternative (PC1) that agrees with it, not against a content-free alternative, so the causal evidence shows the direction modulates persona, and does not show that it is the only or the best direction that does. Their drift decomposition is the piece I would reuse most: predicting the level from the last message at R² 0.53–0.77 while the delta is near-unpredictable at R² 0.10 says that a state variable can be highly predictable in absolute terms and almost uninformative about dynamics, which is exactly the shape our model's state estimates will take.
