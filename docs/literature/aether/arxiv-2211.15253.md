# Lipschitz constant estimation for 1D convolutional neural networks

- **Key:** arxiv-2211.15253
- **Authors / organisation:** Patricia Pauli, Dennis Gramlich, Frank Allgöwer; University of Stuttgart (Institute for Systems Theory and Automatic Control) and RWTH Aachen (Chair of Intelligent Control Systems)
- **Date:** 2022-11-28
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.15253
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Version read:** arXiv v1 (the abs page lists a later online date of 2023-06-20)
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

Lipschitz bounds for feed-forward networks had by this point been reduced to a semidefinite program via incremental quadratic constraints, but those programs were written for dense layers, and the standard practice when a convolutional network had to be certified was to unroll the convolution into a sparse Toeplitz matrix and hand the result to the dense solver. That unrolling multiplies the number of decision variables by the signal length and is described by the authors as highly redundant, so certification cost grew with input size even though the weights did not. The paper's aim is a bound whose cost depends on the channel counts and kernel widths and not at all on how long the signal is, obtained by treating each convolutional layer as a small discrete-time filter with internal state rather than as a large linear map. A second, narrower aim is coverage: pooling layers were simply dropped from previous analyses, and the paper wants at least the standard pooling operations inside the certified object. The larger goal is the ordinary one for this line of work — a cheap, sound robustness number that can be computed for realistically sized signal models.

## What they studied

One-dimensional convolutional networks built from three layer types: convolutional layers with element-wise slope-restricted activations, pooling layers (average and maximum, with kernel size equal to stride), and dense layers ending in a linear read-out. The unit of analysis is the layer as an input-output signal map, with the internal state of a convolutional layer carrying the kernel's recent input history. Per convolutional layer the state dimension is (kernel length minus one) times the input channel count, and the certificate variables attached to a convolutional layer are a positive definite storage matrix over that state, a positive diagonal multiplier over its output channels, and three small channel-sized matrices describing the layer's energy behaviour. Dense layers need only a dense multiplier and small channel-sized matrices; pooling layers contribute fixed matrices with no free variables except a positive semidefinite or positive diagonal weight.

## How they did it

Step one: write each layer as a map between consecutive signals and define the objects of interest on *differences* between two input-output pairs, so that everything downstream is about increments rather than absolute values. Step two: describe a convolutional layer as a finite-impulse-response filter in state-space form — a shift-structured transition matrix, an input matrix that injects the current input into the state, and an output matrix that reads the kernel taps off the state — with the activation applied after a direct feed-through of the current input. Because the state dimension depends only on kernel length and input channels, the layer's description is independent of signal length; the authors note that a non-causal convention (symmetric kernels) is converted into this causal one by re-indexing. Step three: certify each layer separately as incrementally dissipative, using a positive diagonal multiplier for the slope-restricted activation and a storage matrix over the state, and prove a conditional-understanding lemma: a storage matrix satisfying a three-by-three block inequality plus the activation's own inequality implies the layer's dissipativity. Step four: handle pools with their own inequalities derived by hand — for average pooling a contraction bound with constant one over the square root of the kernel size, with any positive semidefinite matrix allowed as its channel weight; for max pooling a unit-contraction bound with the channel weight forced to be positive diagonal. Step five: chain the layers by a block-tridiagonal matrix over the stacked signals whose first block carries the candidate squared bound and last block a unit term, and observe that the chained inequality holds if every layer satisfies its own incremental inequality. Step six: strip decision variables by parameterising the layers' matrices so that this block-tridiagonal matrix is exactly zero rather than merely positive semidefinite — which sets each layer's input-side weight to the negative of the previous layer's output-side weight, and, through the average-pooling rescaling, folds the pooling contraction constants into the scalar bound. Step seven: collect the result as one small LMI system per layer, convex in the candidate bound, and solve it as a minimisation with standard semidefinite tooling. Step eight: compare against the dense-family solvers (neuron-wise and layer-wise multiplier variants) and against the product of spectral norms, on a fully convolutional net swept over input length and on a ten-instance densely-and-pooled network.

## What counts as an explanation here

A certificate, not a description. The delivered object is a scalar bound together with a witness: per-layer storage matrices over the state, per-layer positive diagonal multipliers over the channels, and small channel-sized matrices carrying the layer's input-output energy relation. It is sound and partial — a sufficient condition, never advertised as exact. The authors make one explicit step toward interpretation, in a remark on what the small channel matrix contains: they state that these matrices carry information about the layer-wise worst-case gain and worst-case input direction, and that imposing a diagonal form after a max-pool layer destroys the direction information. Nothing is done with this observation; no matrix is printed, inspected, or correlated with anything. The explanation is thus complete with respect to soundness and empty with respect to the network's content.

## How they judged success

Accuracy of the bound and compute time, against two named baselines and one trivial one. The baselines are the dense-network solvers with neuron-wise and layer-wise multipliers, and the product of spectral norms; the accuracy measure is the resulting scalar (lower is better, since it is an upper bound and no lower bound is reported), and the cost measure is solver wall-clock. Experiment one is a three-layer fully convolutional net with channel widths fixed and kernel size three, swept over input length, plotted against the baselines; the paper reports that at small lengths the dense solvers are tighter and that its own method overtakes the layer-wise variant past roughly fifteen samples while its compute time stays flat. Experiment two is a small pooled classifier, for which ten random parameter draws are certified; the dense solvers cannot represent max pooling, so the comparison splits the network in two halves and multiplies the half-bounds. The reported average solve times are approximately three seconds for this method, about seven hundred and fifty for the split dense baseline, and about one second for the trivial bound, at an input length of one hundred and fifty. There is a stated failure of the baseline, not of the method: the dense route ran out of memory beyond that length. No ablation removes the state-space representation, the pooling constraints, or the zeroing parameterisation; no lower bound on the true constant is computed, so the tightness gap is never measured.

## What they found

The state-space route does what it was built to do on cost: the certificate size stops depending on signal length, and the measured solve times are flat over the sweep while the dense route climbs, with a memory failure at the larger lengths for the baseline. The price is accuracy at small length: for short signals the dense solvers give tighter numbers, so the paper's advantage is conditional on length and there is no crossover analysis beyond the single graph. On the pooled network the bound is described as comparable to the split dense baseline and clearly better than the trivial product of spectral norms — that is, the results confirm soundness and scalability and do not claim superiority of the bound itself. Pooling is included for the first time, and the paper's own construction explains the asymmetry: average pooling admits any positive semidefinite channel weight while max pooling admits only a diagonal one, which the authors trace to a general result that only linear operations preserve incremental positivity; they also note that an earlier paper proposed full matrices for a max-pooling-style constraint and that this was subsequently corrected by another paper of their own group.

## Limits and open problems they name

Two-dimensional convolutions are handled only by flattening them into one-dimensional ones, and directly exploiting two-dimensional structure is named as future work. The construction is restricted to the case where pooling kernel size equals stride (the paper says the general case is extendable but does not extend it), and to activations slope-restricted on the unit interval. The bound is one-sided: only an upper bound on the Lipschitz constant is produced and computed, and the comparisons rank upper bounds without a lower reference. The remark about lost direction information after max pooling is stated and left there, with no measurement of what the diagonal restriction costs in bound quality. Notably, the paper does not address the question its companion state-space literature raises — whether the bound is independent of the *realisation* chosen for the filter — since only one realisation (plus similarity transforms) is considered, and the realisation is used purely as a size-reduction device.

## What we take from it

Three things. First, a **method with a clean interface**: a bound whose certificate size is a function of channel counts, kernel width and depth only. For our account of a network this is the sharpest available statement that a layer's cost-bearing description is the kernel's state, not the activation map — the same observation the Roesser realisation makes in the 2-D case, arriving here in 1-D and with the pooling operations that the 2-D work leaves out. Second, a **warning about baseline accounting**: the paper's headline is a cost win measured against a baseline that ran out of memory, and its accuracy claim is "comparable"; a reader scanning the abstract would not learn that the dense route is tighter wherever both run. That is the interval problem again — a ranking between upper bounds with no lower end — and it argues that any bound we import must carry a computed lower reference on the same line. Third, a **framing we can borrow directly**: an LMI drawn from a *stacked* channel matrix whose entries the authors themselves say encode a per-layer worst-case gain and direction, discarded immediately. This is the third member of the family to produce an object of exactly the shape our white-box account wants — a small matrix over channels, computable from weights with no data, carrying a claim about which directions a layer amplifies — and the third to look at it only as a solver variable. The cheapest genuinely new experiment in this head remains reading one of these matrices back out of the solver and reporting its spectrum, and this paper supplies the cleanest specimen because its matrices are channel-sized rather than unit-sized, so they describe interactions between feature channels rather than between neurons.
