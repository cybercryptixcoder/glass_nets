## What they wanted, and why

The authors take as given that mechanistic interpretability has produced real findings but has no standard way to check whether a given study's claim is sound, and that this gap is what keeps the findings out of high-stakes deployment, where a regulator or an engineering team has to decide which of two incompatible explanations to believe. Their motivating anecdote is a pair of peer-reviewed papers offering competing accounts of the same mechanism in a group-operation network, which a third paper later reconciled by showing each was partly right and the two were simply not comparable because their methods differed. From that they argue the field needs a reviewing layer that runs continuously rather than at publication, that records the small results which never become papers, and that turns recurring good practice into guidelines backed by evidence rather than by authority. The stated aim is deliberately not to supply the guidelines: they want to convene the process that would produce them, and they say so twice, in the introduction and again in the limitations.

## What they studied

Nothing empirical — this is an agenda. The "objects" are research artefacts and the discourse around them: papers, replication and reproduction attempts, negative results, partial results, post-hoc critiques, forum threads, blog posts and social-media exchanges that carry methodological knowledge but live outside the paper record. The unit of analysis is the *claim* (and, one level down, the specific statement — a plot, a paragraph, an appendix table — that a claim depends on), not the model, the neuron or the circuit.

## How they did it

The paper is an argument plus worked illustrations. It first walks through the pitfalls of one concrete approach — activation patching for circuit discovery — to show that the pitfalls are technical and not merely sloppy practice: define the model as a graph, a circuit as a subgraph, the patch as substituting one component's activations from a clean run into a corrupted run, and the score as the change in a task metric; then note that a large score at every component in a candidate subgraph does not make the subgraph well-defined, since necessity has to be checked one component at a time, and that the corruption scheme can push representations off-distribution while the choice of metric (logits, probabilities, KL) can reorder which components look important. From there the method is design: three interlocking proposals — a collaborative platform where meta-results are filed, commented on and continued; a process by which community debate over proposed guideline pages hardens into expert-verified guidelines; and a source-based auditing layer that traces each claim to the evidence it rests on and propagates doubt when an assumption is later falsified. Engagement is addressed with incentive design borrowed from open-source and Q&A platforms (reviewer portfolios, partial-contribution credit, anonymity options). Two worked examples show the scheme in use: a nine-step audit of an activation-patching study in a clinical setting, and a ten-item checklist plus an implemented LLM-based auditor that scores a written circuit explanation for evidence of sufficiency, necessity, sparsity, robustness, non-cherry-picking, causal validation, null baselines, seed stability and pruning-tie exploration.

## What counts as an explanation here

An audit trail, not a model of the network. The understanding the paper wants is a claim whose evidential status is explicit and inspectable: what it depends on, which experiments were run, on which data, with which metric, and whether any of those links has since been weakened. It is explicitly partial and explicitly non-authoritative — the authors say guidelines must not be treated as a definitive test, and that the dependency graphs are "not exhaustive" scratchpads for a single debate. The nearest thing to a predictive object is the proposed probabilistic-logic layer, in which predicates over observations (a circuit meets a sufficiency threshold, an edge is in the circuit, an edge's removal causes a performance drop) feed latent predicates (this edge is critical, this circuit is approximately minimal) with soft truth values, so that a claim's confidence can be recomputed when evidence changes. That is a scoring harness over claims, not a white-box model of a network.

## How they judged success

There is no evaluation in the paper; the judgement is by argument and by the worked examples, which are stipulated rather than run. The two audits are constructed as plausible failure modes (in the clinical case, the study is sound on reproducibility, corruption and granularity but scores success by the probability of the urgent label, and recomputing with a logit difference between urgent and non-urgent makes several components' effects vanish or turn negative, so the central claim is judged not to hold). The LLM auditor is shown producing a scorecard on a toy indirect-object-identification explanation, passing eight items and failing two. Precedents are cited as indirect evidence that codified reporting standards work elsewhere: microarray minimum-information standards in biology, the GRADE certainty framework in clinical evidence, a high-integrity coding standard in safety-critical software engineering. The authors name the missing evaluation themselves: no user engagement has been gathered, and testing via surveys and workshops is deferred to future work.

## What they found

The substantive content is the pitfall taxonomy and the audit criteria, which are the reusable part. Two tables enumerate pitfalls that span all approaches (interpretability illusions, cherry-picking, missing sanity checks against random or scrambled baselines, no causal validation) and pitfalls specific to an approach (correlation mistaken for causation in probing, prompt-choice sensitivity in patching, feature absorption making a dictionary latent fail to fire on cases its label covers, out-of-distribution degradation in steering). The criteria for a circuit claim are stated using the causal-abstraction vocabulary: behaviour preservation within a stated tolerance, localisation (perturbing inside the circuit reproduces the effect, outside it does not), minimality at a preset tolerance, and robustness to distribution shift. The audit example's core finding is a metric-alignment failure: a component can look important under one output statistic and negligible under a signed alternative, and the paper's remedy is that the metric must be named in advance and paired with a sensitivity analysis over alternative metrics, corruptions, seeds and prompt distributions. A second, more interesting argument runs through the appendix on why verification is permissive: interpretability explanations need not be unique — several distinct circuits or abstractions can fit the same behaviour equally well — and causal abstraction as a criterion can become loose enough to accept a great many hypotheses, so auditing has to test the assumptions that make an explanation meaningful, not just whether a candidate mapping exists.

## Limits and open problems they name

The central limitation is that the whole construction is conditional on participation that has not happened: the platform needs an active community, incentives are proposed rather than demonstrated, and the authors concede they have gathered no user engagement. They also acknowledge that guidelines can become a false guarantee if passing a checklist is mistaken for rigour, which is why every occurrence insists the guidelines are one dimension among several. On the regulatory side they list what they do not solve: governance of guideline development, the tension between minimal requirements and methodological flexibility, enforcement without stifling innovation, and verification at frontier-model scale where exhaustive testing is computationally prohibitive. They also concede that the taxonomy is high level — Table 2's entries are called well-known, with finer distinctions left to the community.

## What we take from it

Four things transfer directly to our own write-ups, and one warns against something we are already doing.

1. **The claim-dependency form is a report template, not just a platform feature.** The paper's proposal for a claim graph — each claim linked to the specific statement (a plot, a table cell, an assumption) it rests on, each edge marked weak or strong, and the graph re-evaluated when an ancestor is falsified — is a shape our effect tables can adopt at zero infrastructure cost. Concretely: every number we publish names the artefact it came from, and any claim that depends on an assumption (this corruption is in-distribution, this metric is the right one, this component set is minimal) is drawn with that assumption as a visible parent node. The payoff the paper names is the one we have seen in our own entries: a dependency you never unfurl is a dependency you never test.

2. **Metric alignment is a first-class audit step and it is the cheapest one to run.** The clinical example's failure is that a component's causal influence was measured in the wrong currency, and the diagnostic is simply to recompute the same intervention under a signed metric and a second metric and report both. That is one extra column on a table we already build, and the case where the two disagree is exactly the case worth printing.

3. **Minimality needs a stated tolerance and a tie-break policy.** The paper's minimal-circuit checklist treats seed stability and greedy-pruning tie exploration as standard items, and its toy scorecard fails precisely on those two. This matches what our circuit entries have been circling: a component set is one of several equally plausible solutions until the search's ties are explored, and a minimality claim without a seed arm is not yet a claim about the model.

4. **Non-identifiability is the frame our accounts should be written in.** If several circuits can fit the same behaviour, then an account's value is not that it is the explanation but that it is a member of an equivalence class whose assumptions are stated. The paper's demand — quantify uncertainty and test against explicit alternatives — is the same demand the folder's probe and control-task entries have reached from three other directions, and it suggests a required row: name the rival explanations our account has not excluded.

5. **A warning:** the paper is a compilation of pitfalls already documented elsewhere, citing the interpretability-illusion work, the absorption analysis, the activation-patching best-practices paper, the Hydra-effect and SAE-stability critiques. Nearly every guideline in it is a restatement of a failure already published in a specific paper. Reading the agenda is not a substitute for reading the seven papers it compresses; the taxonomy is useful as an index, and nothing more.
