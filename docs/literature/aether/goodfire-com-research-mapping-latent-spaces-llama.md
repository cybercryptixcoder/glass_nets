# Mapping the Latent Space of Llama 3.3 70B

- **Key:** goodfire-com-research-mapping-latent-spaces-llama
- **Authors / organisation:** Thomas McGrath, Daniel Balsam, Liv Gorton, Murat Cubuktepe, Myra Deng, Nam Nguyen, Akshaj Jain, Thariq Shihipar, Eric Ho; Goodfire
- **Date:** 2024-12-23 (with a February 2026 update noting that the SAE demo interface and API were deprecated)
- **Link:** https://www.goodfire.com/research/mapping-latent-spaces-llama
- **Kind:** blog/research post (release announcement for an SAE-on-70B API, plus a short exploratory tour of the feature space)
- **Read from:** full blog post (all sections: feature map, feature examples, steering, methods, moderation, limitations; the interactive UMAP explorer and the steering plots were not inspected beyond their described axes and the numbers stated in prose; no figures read directly)
- **Relevance to us:** 1 (background)

## What they wanted, and why
The goal was access rather than a question: train sparse autoencoders on a large open-weights model and hand the interpreted model to outsiders through an API, so that interpretability work could proceed on a model strong enough to be interesting rather than on a toy. The authors argue that the most capable openly available model with interpretability tooling is a bottleneck for both research and products, and this post is the release plus a first qualitative survey of what the dictionary contains. A secondary aim is to show that SAE-based steering can be made robust enough to be a usable primitive, with convenience layers on top of it. The post is explicit that it is an exploration and that a follow-up on steering methodology was promised for the new year.

## What they studied
Llama 3.3 70B is the main subject, with the earlier Llama 3.1 8B SAEs as the reference point; features are taken at one intermediate layer. The unit of analysis is the SAE latent: a single dictionary direction with a scalar activation, usable both as a classifier and as a steering handle. Data for the dictionary is LMSYS chat data, so the units studied are conversational features learned from chat traffic rather than from a broad pretraining mixture. A single feature map is built over the whole dictionary, so the post also treats the *layout* of features in the dictionary as an object worth looking at.

## How they did it
Training follows the group's earlier SAE recipe on smaller models, which the post defers to rather than restates, and the authors add two observations that shape the rest: they find narrower and sparser dictionaries than convention work better for steering, and they flag that this preference likely trades off against classifier performance. To read the dictionary they embed all latents with UMAP and render the result as an interactive map, then inspect local clusters by hand and give examples of clusters that look coherent or interesting. For steering they expose two interfaces: a direct call that sets one latent's value to a chosen magnitude, and an automatic routine that searches for latents to elicit a stated behaviour and sets their weights. Steering strength is swept and each response is scored for coherence and for whether the behaviour was achieved, using another language model as the judge on a 0-to-100 scale. They also apply a moderation pass over the dictionary before release, and describe the removal rule and the residual risks in their own terms.

## What counts as an explanation here
The explanation is a feature dictionary plus an interface: a labelled dictionary over one layer of one model, browsable as a map, with the argument that being able to steer a feature is a kind of proof that the description means something. It is partial by the authors' own statement — the dictionaries capture a fraction of the model's computation — and it is not meant to predict outputs. The closest thing to a predictive object is the steering sweep: a curve of judged behaviour against injected magnitude, which is a prediction about the effect of one intervention at one layer, not an account of a mechanism. There is no circuit, no attribution graph, and no attempt to compose features into a computation.

## How they judged success
Success criteria are mostly release-level and qualitative. Steering is graded with a model judge on coherence and behaviour, plotted against strength, and the post reads the shape of the curve rather than a single score. Feature coherence is judged by eye from cluster inspection, and the post is careful to say that apparent clusters of behaviour-sensitive latents have not been verified to have distinct effects. There is no baseline dictionary, no held-out probe set, no causal certificate for any individual feature, and no aggregate faithfulness metric. One incidental measurement is the moderation figure: a stated fraction of features removed, which the post reports alongside the caveat that dead features are counted in it.

## What they found
The dictionary contains a broad range of concepts for something trained only on chat data, with clusters that look like behavioural distinctions, biomedical knowledge, physics, programming, name-related abstractions such as placeholders and citations, and a large family of phonetic and character-level features. Latents tied to special formatting tokens and to strings repeated in every conversation (a knowledge-cutoff date, for instance) sit as isolated points or small islands away from the central mass of the map; the authors offer two non-exclusive explanations, that special tokens carry very large magnitudes, or that frequent repetition pushed those features toward memorised representations, citing the superposition-and-memorisation work for the second. Steering shows the pattern the authors say practitioners will recognise: the judged behaviour rises sharply around a mid-range strength where the style fully changes, but factual degradation appears at lower strength than the behaviour score suggests, and at the high end the responses are judged well by the automatable metric while being factually wrong, with magnitudes converted into nautical units. That last observation is presented as a pitfall of model-judged steering evaluation rather than as a finding about the model. Moderation removed roughly 30% and 3.5% of features from the Llama 3.1 8B and Llama 3.3 70B dictionaries respectively, numbers that include dead features.

## Limits and open problems they name
The central stated tension is that steering and classification want different dictionaries: steering likes narrow, sparse dictionaries near the middle of the network, classification likely wants broad dictionaries early or late, and the authors suggest crosscoders for cross-layer features together with flexible decoding schemes as a possible reconciliation. They also state the general caveat that their dictionaries cover only part of the computation, both because the training data is limited and because SAEs do not yet reconstruct activations comprehensively, and they note that early evidence does not point to incomplete reconstruction being merely a question of scale. The mechanism by which steering damages factual recall is named as unknown and interesting. The post also concedes that steering evaluation still needs a strong qualitative foundation, since the automated judge scored a factually-broken region highly. A February 2026 update notes the demo interface and API were deprecated.

## What we take from it
A warning and a shortcut, in that order. The warning is the clean worked example of a model-judged behaviour score rising while the underlying capability degrades: the behaviour metric and the factual metric are not monotone together across steering strength, so a single judge score is not a reliable readout of an intervention's effect. For us this is the concrete form of a rule we already hold — every steering result is reported with at least one metric the judge was not asked about, and a high score in a region the authors themselves call broken is treated as evidence that the evaluation is too narrow, not that the intervention works. The shortcut is the isolated-island observation: features tied to strings that appear in every conversation are pushed apart from the main body of the dictionary, which is a cheap diagnostic to run before trusting any feature we interpret on chat-formatted data — check whether a latent we care about is a formatting or boilerplate artefact before treating its cluster position as meaningful. Read as background: this is a release announcement, and it is useful to us for the eval design and the artefact warning rather than for any methodological claim.
