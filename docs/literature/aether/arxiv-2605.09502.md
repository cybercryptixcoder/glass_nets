# Hidden Error Awareness in Chain-of-Thought Reasoning: The Signal Is Diagnostic, Not Causal

- **Key:** arxiv-2605.09502
- **Authors / organisation:** Aojie Yuan, Zhiyuan Julian Su, Haiyue Zhang, Yi Nian, Yue Zhao; University of Southern California (Los Angeles)
- **Date:** 2026-05-10 (v1, the only version)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.09502
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (Sections 1-8 plus Appendices A-G; figures not inspected)
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

Chain-of-thought prompting is used as if the written reasoning were a window onto the model's computation. Work that attacked that assumption before this paper worked on the text: it showed explanations bend to irrelevant cues, or can be truncated with the answer intact. What was missing was a comparison between what the model writes and what it holds internally during the same forward pass. The authors' question is therefore direct: if you read the hidden state during a reasoning trace, can you tell whether the trace will end in a wrong answer, and can you tell earlier than the text does? The larger goal is a diagnostic instrument for reasoning, and then the papers' real target is the step after diagnosis - whether that instrument can be used to repair what it detects. They frame the answer as a boundary statement for mechanistic interpretability: there are representations you can edit and representations you can only observe.

## What they studied

Nine models across three families, spanning 1.5B to 72B parameters: Qwen2.5 at 1.5B, 3B, 7B, 14B, 32B and 72B, Llama-3.1-8B and Phi-3.5-mini, plus DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B as an RL-distilled reasoning model. The benchmark is MATH-500, with one cross-domain check on ARC-Challenge. The unit of analysis is the residual-stream hidden state at a chosen layer, read at the last token of a generated CoT trace (and, for the early-signal experiments, at the end of the first numbered step). Labels are trace-level: the trace is wrong if its parsed final answer differs from the reference. The intervention unit is the same vector - the probe's weight direction, subtracted from or blended into hidden states during generation.

## How they did it

One greedy CoT is generated per problem, numbered step by step, ending in an explicit answer line; the answer is parsed and compared with the reference to give the label. Hidden states are extracted at every layer from a single forward pass over prompt-plus-trace, the last-token position is taken, features are standardized per dimension, and an L2-regularized logistic regression is fitted per layer; the layer is chosen by 5-fold cross-validated AUROC and the probe is then refit on all training data at that layer. Probe training uses 100 MATH-500 problems disjoint from every evaluation set; evaluation uses 200 separate problems. Against this they run baselines on the same data: self-consistency with five samples, CCS from the same hidden states, the probability of the token `True` under a prompting wrapper, verbalized confidence on a 1-5 scale, and the sequence log-probability of the trace. To separate trace quality from problem difficulty, they sample five traces at temperature 0.7 for 50 problems and compare correct and wrong traces on the same problem with a t-test and Cohen's d. To test surface visibility they train a TF-IDF plus logistic-regression classifier on the first step's text and subtract its AUROC from the hidden-state probe's. Four interventions follow: subtracting the probe direction at several strengths during generation; best-of-N selection by lowest probe score against random selection, majority vote and an oracle; retry-based self-correction with probe-triggered and oracle-triggered versions; and activation patching that replaces or blends correct-trace hidden states into a wrong-trace generation, plus a pure subtraction variant. The appendix adds per-layer tables, probe-architecture and training-size ablations, two example traces, and the ARC transfer test.

## What counts as an explanation here

A single direction in hidden-state space, identified with the logistic probe's weight vector, plus the layer at which it is read. The claim is not that the direction is a mechanism for error; the paper's central contribution is the opposite reading, that the direction is a summary statistic of how the computation is going and is not a lever. Completeness is explicitly not attempted: nothing is claimed about which components produce the errors, and the discussion says reasoning quality arises from distributed multi-layer computation rather than from any single editable feature. What the direction is allowed to predict is bounded in one respect - it predicts the outcome of a trace - and denied in another - it does not predict what will happen if you interfere with it.

## How they judged success

AUROC for error detection, cross-validated and held out; the same metric for the text-surface classifier to define a concealment gap (hidden minus text AUROC); a within-problem paired comparison with t-test p-values and Cohen's d to rule out difficulty as the driver; gains and losses in final-answer accuracy for each of the four interventions, with oracle bounds to show that correct traces exist to be selected or recovered; and the fraction of wrong traces lying in a region defined in advance as high confidence (at least 4 of 5) together with high probe score (above 0.5). No new training of the models occurs anywhere: every intervention acts at inference time.

## What they found

The probe separates correct from wrong traces at 0.95 AUROC for Qwen2.5-3B (the authors' main workhorse), and above 0.9 for five of the nine models, including 0.977 at 72B; the middle of the size range is where it sags, at 0.669 for Qwen2.5-7B, 0.762 at 14B and 0.703 for Llama-3.1-8B, and the reasoning-distilled model sits at 0.852. The sags are explained in the discussion as fewer, less systematic errors in intermediate models - harder to separate linearly - rather than as a scaling limit. Against baselines on 200 held-out problems the probe (0.953) beats self-consistency (0.823), CCS (0.718), the `True`-token probability (0.721), verbalized confidence (0.674) and sequence log-probability (0.676); a linear probe also matches an MLP (0.944) and beats a random forest (0.893), and 20 training problems already reach 0.956. The within-problem control works as intended for three of four models measured, with wrong traces scoring higher at p below 0.05 and d from 0.55 to 0.69; the 14B model is the exception at d = 0.13. The signal is early: the first step alone reaches 0.787, which the paper calls 98% of full-trace performance against that table's own 0.800 full-trace figure, and the step-by-step trajectories differ in shape between models - 3B separates most at step 1, 7B builds separation as the trace goes on. Concealment is the headline: wrong traces verbalize 4.55 out of 5 against 4.87 for correct ones, the first-step text classifier reaches only 0.590 against the hidden probe's 0.787 (a gap of 0.197), and surface statistics do not differ significantly (length p = 0.211, number density p = 0.726). 65% of wrong traces fall in the high-confidence, high-probe-score region. Then every intervention fails. Subtracting the error direction moves 3B accuracy by at most +0.03 and costs 0.06 at the strongest setting, while 7B gains 0.04 there. Probe-guided best-of-12 reaches 0.62 on 3B against 0.75 for majority vote and 0.88 for the oracle. Always retrying costs both models 3 points; even an oracle-triggered retry gains 1. Blending correct hidden states in at half strength takes 3B to 0.00 accuracy and 7B to 0.07, and replacing them outright drops 3B from 0.65 to 0.35. Only the subtraction variant escapes, lifting 7B from 0.33 to 0.47. Cross-domain, a MATH-trained probe transfers to ARC at 0.54-0.55 while a natively trained one reaches 0.63-0.69, which the authors read as error directions being domain-specific.

## Limits and open problems they name

The benchmark is essentially MATH-500, with ARC as a single check; the intervention conclusions therefore hold for short mathematical traces on 1.5B-72B open models. Nothing is trained: fine-tuning on probe signals, or reinforcement learning from probe feedback, is named as untested and as possibly the way to close the diagnostic-causal gap. The reasoning-model difficulty control is underpowered (14 mixed-outcome problems, p = 0.447, d = -0.30), so the RL-model trace-level claim rests on the AUROC rather than on the paired test. The authors also note that the intermediate-scale dip has only a qualitative explanation. Beyond what they say: the probes are trained on 100 problems and their confidence intervals appear only in the early-signal table, and no per-layer interval is given for the headline per-model AUROCs, so the comparison between a 0.918 cross-validated value and a 0.724 held-out value for the 1.5B model - a large drop the paper does not discuss - is exactly the kind of cell that would need an interval to interpret; the two example traces are chosen to illustrate, not to estimate.

## What we take from it

Three things. (1) A reusable measurement: the concealment gap, defined as the hidden-state readout's AUROC minus a text-surface classifier's AUROC on the same inputs, is the cheapest available test of whether a readout sees something the output channel does not, and it costs one TF-IDF baseline. (2) A warning about the folder's own instruments: this paper's probe is excellent at predicting an outcome and useless as a lever, and the failure is visible only because all four interventions were run on problems where both outcomes occur - so a white-box model that reports a predictor without an intervention table is reporting exactly the half of the picture that this paper shows can be true while the other half is false. (3) Two patterns recorded below: the oracle bound as the test of whether selection or signal is the bottleneck, and the monotonicity failure in the patching table that shows a diagnostic readout's causal effect need not be linear in it.
