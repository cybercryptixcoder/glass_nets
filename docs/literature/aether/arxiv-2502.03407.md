# Detecting Strategic Deception Using Linear Probes

- **Key:** arxiv-2502.03407
- **Authors / organisation:** Nicholas Goldowsky-Dill, Bilal Chughtai, Stefan Heimersheim, Marius Hobbhahn; Apollo Research
- **Date:** 2025-02-05
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.03407
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (v1 HTML: main text §§1–5 including limitations, Tables 1–3, Appendices A–F read in full, Appendix G sampled; all figures are images and were not inspected)
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Output monitoring is structurally insufficient against a model that is trying to look honest: a scheming model can produce a benign transcript while its reasoning is misaligned, so the thing to watch is the internals rather than the text. The authors ask a deliberately deflationary version of that hope — can a *linear* probe trained on data that contains no real deception fire on the model's own on-policy lies, in settings built by other labs where the lies are strategic and multi-turn. They frame the target as a component of AI control rather than a proof: the models they test are not capable of the sophisticated deception that motivates the work, and their transcripts are readable to a human, so the exercise is a benchmark of white-box monitors in easy conditions, with the hard conditions named as future work. The larger goal is a monitor that can sit on a deployment distribution where almost nothing is deceptive and a single false alarm is costly.

## What they studied
**Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct** (80 layers), probed in the residual stream **after layer 22** of 80 — a site chosen on the predecessor Llama-3.1-70B and carried over. The unit of analysis is the **per-token residual-stream activation**, collapsed to one score per response; the secondary unit is a sparse-autoencoder feature vector from a public Llama-3.3-70B SAE. Training data: the **Instructed-Pairs** contrastive set (pairs of prompts differing only in whether the assistant is told to pretend to be honest or deceptive, with a true fact inserted as the response and activations taken with the last five tokens cut so the fact can be completed either way) and, as an alternative, responses the model wrote itself in **roleplaying** scenarios where lying is instrumentally useful. Evaluation data are all sampled on-policy: **Roleplaying** (371 responses, judged low-realism), **Insider Trading Report** (1,329, high-realism, filtered to the 25% of runs where the model actually trades on the tip), **Insider Trading Confirmation** (656, following up only the deceptive and ambiguous reports), **Sandbagging** (995, asked to underperform on WMDP while answering MMLU normally), and a fourth setting (**AI Audit**) that the authors exclude from main results because they could not establish that the model was knowingly deceptive rather than confused. Control distribution: 9,994 responses to **Alpaca** queries, chosen because they are unlikely to concern deception. Ground-truth labels are **GPT-4o** judgements with a per-dataset grading prompt and few-shot examples; Sandbagging is labelled programmatically from the model's own step-by-step reasoning.

## How they did it
Stage 1, fit the probe: flatten activations across samples and positions, normalise to zero mean and unit variance, fit **L2-regularised logistic regression** (λ = 10), and treat the weights as a direction. Stage 2, score a response: apply the training normalisation to the new activations, project onto the direction at every token, and take the **mean over all response tokens** as the response-level deception score. Stage 3, calibrate: compute the score distribution on the Alpaca controls and set the operating threshold at its **99th percentile**, so the false-positive rate on unrelated chat is fixed at 1% by construction, then report what fraction of deceptive responses in each evaluation set clears that bar. Stage 4, compare families, all retrained on 80–90% of Roleplaying so they are directly comparable: the same probe on the Roleplaying set itself, on **SAE features** rather than raw activations, on the activation after a scripted follow-up exchange (user: was your last response deceptive? model: No), and on the yes/no pair from an earlier sleeper-agent result, plus a **black-box baseline** in which GPT-4o reads the complete transcript on a seven-point scale with one universal prompt. Stage 5, sweep the hyperparameters — fitting procedure (logistic regression, difference of means, LDA, LAT), layer, regularisation strength, aggregation rule (mean, maximum, ReLU-thresholded mean), and last-token-only probing — against representative validation sets. A re-analysis reclassifies all "ambiguous" grades as honest or deceptive to bound what excluding the hard cases buys.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **direction**, nothing more: one linear functional of the residual stream, used as a scalar monitor rather than as a story about mechanism. The paper is explicit that the probe carries no account of *how* the model deceives; its content is inferred only by looking at which tokens fire and which do not, and the authors read it as tracking something broader than "this response is deceptive" — plausibly "this scenario makes deception advantageous". The entry worth carrying is therefore not the direction but the *validation grammar* around it: what a read-out has to do before anyone is allowed to call it a monitor.

## How they judged success
Two metrics, on two different pairings, and the paper treats them as different claims. **AUROC** between deceptive and honest responses *within* a dataset measures separation; **recall at a 1% false-positive operating point** between deceptive and control responses measures whether the monitor is usable on a stream of mostly-innocent traffic. The threshold for the second is the 99th percentile of the Alpaca score distribution — a number fixed before the evaluation sets are touched — and is reported alongside its sensitivity to method choice (the layer sweep). Supporting evidence: per-token activation traces read qualitatively for failure patterns, the three-way honest/ambiguous/deceptive grading, a second roleplaying dataset from another lab with matched deceptive and non-deceptive prompt variants, a 0.1% FPR operating point, and the ambiguous-reclassification table.

## What they found
The mainline probe separates deceptive from honest responses at **AUROC 0.964 (Roleplaying), 0.999 (Insider Trading Report), 0.974 (Confirmation) and 0.983 (Sandbagging)**. On the control-relative task it is stronger still — AUROC above 0.998 everywhere — and at a 1% FPR threshold it catches **95–99% of deceptive responses**, with the worst cell being Roleplaying at 96% in the discussion (the paper's own summary says 4% of deceptive Roleplaying responses are missed). The follow-up-question probe is the best of all on honest-versus-deceptive separation (near-perfect on every dataset) and reaches **>99% recall at 1% FPR**, but it rates every Sandbagging response as highly deceptive, including the honest ones. Training on the model's own roleplaying responses separates them perfectly in-distribution yet generalises worse to Sandbagging and much worse against controls — the authors' explanation is that a probe trained where deception is always incentivised never learns the scenario-level feature that makes the Instructed-Pairs probe good at saying "nothing here is deception-related". The SAE-feature probe is worse on every dataset and rates many deceptive prompts below many control prompts. The yes/no-difference probe fires in the **opposite** direction from the published sleeper-agent result — admitting deception correlates with honesty in these datasets — because several of the honest responses are confessions of past misconduct. The black-box baseline is roughly on par with the probes. Three failure modes are documented with per-token traces: **spurious correlation** with wrongdoing (an honest confession of having exaggerated a role scores at the 100th control percentile), **aggregation failure** (a response deceptive in its first half and contrite in the second averages below threshold even though the deceptive tokens score at the 98.8th percentile), and a residual category the authors simply cannot explain. Reclassifying ambiguous grades drops Roleplaying AUROC from 0.964 to 0.913 and recall from 0.998 to 0.956 while Insider Trading and Sandbagging barely move. The two highest-scoring control responses, of 9,994, are both answers to prompts asking the model to *produce* something false.

## Limits and open problems they name
The settings are easy: deception is blatant, often stated outright in the chain-of-thought, and the probe is never actually evaluated on chain-of-thought tokens, so whether it survives subtle deception is untested. Ground truth is a GPT-4o grade, and the authors concede that excluding ambiguous cases may flatter the probe; they also had to drop one evaluation setting entirely because they could not tell deception from confusion. Capability was a limiting factor — Llama-3.3-70B was not credibly deceptive in the audit scenario. Recall at 1% FPR proved very sensitive to method: probing two layers later drove recall to nearly zero on both Insider Trading sets while leaving the honest-versus-deceptive AUROC decent, and adjacent layers differ substantially. Single model, single site, mean aggregation, and a control corpus chosen for being unambiguously non-deceptive rather than deployment-realistic; the authors also name the untested cases, sandbagging and alignment-faking without chain-of-thought, where only a white-box monitor could work at all.

## What we take from it
Several things, and one warning. **A monitor's operating point belongs in the design, not the appendix**: fixing the threshold on a control corpus before touching evaluation data converts "the probe separates the classes" into "this monitor would have caught X% of lies at a 1%-of-traffic false-alarm budget", and that is the form our own read-out claims should take. **Decouple the two AUROCs** — within-dataset separation and control-relative recall are different claims and here they diverge exactly where it matters. **A probe that fires on honest responses in deception-relevant contexts is a scenario detector, not a state detector**, and the diagnostic for it is already in the paper: compare honest responses to incentivised prompts against honest responses to incentive-free prompts. On the aggregation side, the per-token trace table is the cheapest failure taxonomy in the folder — spurious correlate, aggregation artefact, unexplained — and any per-response scalar we build should be stress-tested with a partially-signal example of that shape. The layer sweep is a standing warning against reading a single-site number: two layers of shift annihilated recall while the "accuracy" metric barely noticed.
