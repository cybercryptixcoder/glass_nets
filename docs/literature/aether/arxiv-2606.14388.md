# A Low-Rank Subspace Analysis of LLM Interventions

- **Key:** arxiv-2606.14388
- **Authors / organisation:** Angira Sharma, Christian Schroeder de Witt, Philip Torr, Anisoara Calinescu, Jialin Yu; University of Oxford (all authors)
- **Date:** 2026-06-12
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.14388
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

Post-hoc interventions on model behaviour — activation steering, ablating a refusal direction, fine-tuning on one narrow objective — are effective on their target and unreliable off it: editing one behaviour moves others, and there has been no diagnostic that says in advance which ones will move. The authors want the geometry behind that spillover, so that intervention design can predict collateral rather than discover it. Their larger goal is a picture in which behaviours are not independent control handles but interacting regions of activation space, and in which "this edit will be local" becomes a claim you can check before running the edit. The question is deliberately representational: what property of a behaviour's *subspace* predicts how far its perturbation travels.

## What they studied

Six instruction-tuned open-weight models spanning 7B to 70B: Gemma-2-9B-IT, Llama-3-8B-Instruct, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3 in the main experiments, plus Gemma-3-27B-IT, Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct and Mistral-Small-24B-Instruct-2501 for scale checks. Two behaviours, refusal and sycophancy. Refusal is studied on a synthetic five-category dataset (Harmful, Copyright, NSFW, Deceptive/Malicious, Against Policy, 100 generated requests each) and on the eight-category jailbreak dataset of Arditi et al.; sycophancy uses a synthetic five-category dataset (Geography, History, Math, Science, Subjective). The unit of analysis is a **low-rank subspace** of residual-stream activation space, rank 2 by default, extracted from matched contrastive prompt pairs at the last token; a separate rank-2 "decision" subspace captures the refuse/comply axis.

## How they did it

For each category, matched prompts are written in three variants — one instructing the model to comply, one instructing it to refuse, one neutral evaluation prompt — and the contrast vector between the comply- and refuse-instructed last-token activations is computed per item (the Arditi recipe). Contrasts are centred and decomposed by SVD. A pooled PCA over all categories is then ranked by the absolute correlation between each component's scores and the scalar decision margin (log-prob of REFUSE minus log-prob of COMPLY on the eval prompt); the top two of those components are the decision subspace, and they are projected out of every contrast before anything else is measured. The residualised contrasts are re-decomposed and the top two components form the category's subspace basis. Subspace-pair similarity is the mean squared cosine of principal angles. The intervention is a projection: the category basis is taken from the *raw* (pre-residualisation) contrasts, the activation is mapped through an identity minus a scaled projector, and the change in the target category's decision margin is measured on held-out test prompts. The whole cross-effect matrix is therefore a table of held-out margin shifts, and the geometric quantities are then correlated with that table. Controls: a random orthonormal rank-2 subspace matched in dimension (three seeds across five sources per layer, 15 draws), rank sweeps (1, 2, 3), decision-rank sweeps (1 to 4), bootstrap refits of the entire pipeline, and a shared/private decomposition that splits an overlapping pair into directions both categories share versus directions private to one.

## What counts as an explanation here

A geometric one, and explicitly a partial one. The account is: behaviours occupy low-rank subspaces; spillover happens where subspaces overlap (the substrate) and is sized and directed by how strongly the source subspace is coupled to the decision axis (the gate). Nothing is claimed about mechanism — the authors say outright that this should not be read as identifying full semantic mechanisms, and the PCs are described as a low-rank estimator rather than as mechanisms. The form is predictive in a weak, ordering sense: overlap tells you *which* pairs can interact, decision coupling tells you how much and in which direction, and the two together pick out the regime where effects are large. It does not predict a number for a given pair.

## How they judged success

Self-effect on the source category (diagonal of the cross-effect matrix) as a sanity check that the extracted subspace is behaviourally live; cross-effect magnitudes off-diagonal; selectivity, defined as the ratio of mean self-effect to mean collateral effect; a random-subspace null with the real/null ratio reported per model and per layer; bootstrap subspace stability compared to the chance overlap for random rank-2 spaces (r/D); Pearson and Spearman correlations between residualised overlap and absolute cross-effect across directed pairs over the last twelve layers, with bootstrap confidence intervals; and an interaction table stratifying pairs by overlap bins and decision-angle tertiles. No significance tests beyond the confidence intervals.

## What they found

Raw overlap between category subspaces is high and almost uninformative — it is dominated by shared alignment to the decision axis. After residualising out the two decision components, overlap becomes heterogeneous and the cross-effect matrices show real structure: in Gemma-2-9B, projecting out the Against Policy subspace lowers every other category's refusal margin by roughly 6.7 to 8.7 log-prob units, and the Deceptive/Malicious subspace lowers them by roughly 6.99 to 7.86, while projecting out Copyright or NSFW *raises* refusal across the board (+2.85 to +3.84 from Copyright) — the authors read the positive cases as removal of fine-grained discriminators, leaving the model conservative by default. Asymmetry is the headline: ablating Against Policy drops Harmful refusal by 6.71 while ablating Harmful drops Against Policy by only 0.21, and the same direction-dependence shows in the Llama and Mistral matrices. Overlap alone does not order effect size: Gemma's Deceptive/Malicious–NSFW pair has both high overlap (0.69) and large effects (≈7.55), but Harmful–Copyright has low overlap (0.20) and weak effects (≈0.23) *and the aggregate correlation on that model is only r = +0.164* — the only model with a clearly positive relation is Llama (r = +0.430, Pearson, 95% CI [+0.33, +0.53]; Spearman +0.464), Gemma's +0.164 has a CI reaching down to +0.03, and the pooled unnormalised correlation (+0.050) spans zero, which is why the authors also report a pooled row normalised across models (+0.230 Pearson / +0.237 Spearman). Decision coupling is the second factor: effect magnitude rises as the angle between the source subspace and the decision subspace falls, overlap predicts effect size mainly in the low-angle tertile, and the joint table shows strong effects almost only in the high-overlap, low-angle corner. Rank matters as a trade: rank 1 gives mean self-effect 2.1 with collateral 1.2 (selectivity 1.75, but a weak intervention), rank 2 gives 4.3 / 3.8 (1.13), rank 3 gives 4.2 / 5.1 (0.82) — more dimensions buy no extra reach on the target and only more spill. The random-subspace null is unhelpful as a floor: real effects are 147× to 319× larger than a random rank-2 projection on the layer-averaged table. The most important single table for us is that the per-source *maximum* collateral equals or exceeds the self-effect in all three models (Llama self 4.06 against max collateral 4.61, Gemma 5.03 against 5.11, Mistral 3.11 against 3.20), i.e. there is always at least one other category damaged more than the one being edited. Subspaces themselves are stable across bootstrap refits (0.686 Llama, 0.402 Gemma, 0.331 Mistral, 663× to 1464× above the random floor), and the shared/private split shows overlap is often genuine sharing: when both principal directions are shared (Gemma Deceptive/Malicious–NSFW), the shared and the private projections cost about the same amount (−1.26 / −1.18 shared versus −1.14, −1.13 / −1.13, −1.12 private), which the authors offer as the reason orthogonality constraints between steering vectors fail to remove side effects.

## Limits and open problems they name

Linear low-rank structure only; non-linear or higher-dimensional routes to the same interference are declared out of scope. Estimates are data-dependent — the exact geometry moves with prompt distribution and category definitions, and the authors cite the known unreliability of steering vectors — even though the qualitative pattern holds. The overlap–effect relation is architecture-dependent: Mistral shows weak stratification even among aligned pairs, the 24B Mistral checkpoint inverts the trend outright, Gemma-3-27B reaches only r ≈ 0.21, so alignment is described as necessary but not sufficient. The analysis is pooled over an interaction regime of layers rather than optimised per category. Future work is an intervention that respects both geometry terms, and a test of whether the shared structure comes from shared semantics or from architectural constraint.

## What we take from it

(A **warning** and a **measure**.) The warning is sharper than the method: an ablation that looks targeted is, on this evidence, never targeted — every model in the table has a category whose collateral exceeds the target's self-effect — so any claim our white-box model makes about editing one behaviour in isolation needs the full cross-effect matrix printed, not the diagonal. The measure is the residualisation step: before comparing two components' subspaces, project out the directions most correlated with the output decision, because raw overlap is almost entirely decision alignment and will make everything look alike. Two smaller instruments are worth copying: the rank trade (selectivity falling from 1.75 to 1.13 to 0.82 as rank rises while self-effect saturates) is a concrete argument for reporting selectivity beside effect size for any subspace we fit, and the shared/private decomposition — project the shared directions, then the private ones, and compare — is the cheapest test of whether two of our components share a mechanism or merely co-fire. The null discipline is the thing to refuse: a random-subspace baseline reporting a 147×–319× gap certifies a projection did something, and says nothing about whether the extracted subspace is the right object, which is why this entry's headline correlations sit at 0.16–0.46 on exactly the models where the null looked impressive.
