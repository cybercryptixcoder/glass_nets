# Regularisation of Neural Networks by Enforcing Lipschitz Continuity

- **Key:** arxiv-1804.04368
- **Authors / organisation:** Henry Gouk, Eibe Frank, Bernhard Pfahringer (Department of Computer Science); Michael J. Cree (School of Engineering) — University of Waikato, Hamilton, New Zealand
- **Date:** 2018-09-14 (v2; preprint, stat.ML)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1804.04368
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
The premise is that a function which changes slowly is a simpler function, and simpler functions generalise better. The authors want to make that intuition operational: if the steepness of a network's input-to-output map can be measured exactly and then capped during training, the cap becomes a regulariser with a single meaningful dial instead of a stack of heuristics. They are reacting to the state of practice — dropout and batch normalisation work but are justified mechanically rather than theoretically, and plain weight decay is known to be weaker than either — so the pitch is a regulariser that arrives with a derivation. A second, quieter motive is bookkeeping: several then-current generalisation bounds contain the very quantity they compute, so producing a trustworthy estimate of it is useful even to readers who never adopt the training procedure. The paper is therefore half a measurement paper (how steep is this network, exactly) and half a training paper (how do you keep it below a number you choose).

## What they studied
Feed-forward networks assembled from the layer types that were standard at the time: dense affine maps, convolutions, pooling, ReLU-type activations, softmax, batch normalisation, and residual blocks. Nothing is restricted to rectifiers — the treatment is per layer type, so mixed stacks are covered, and the analysis is done separately for the three vector norms `ℓ1`, `ℓ2` and `ℓ∞` on both input and output. The unit of analysis is the **weight matrix of a single layer**; the network's steepness is then assembled from those per-layer numbers. Experiments span three architecture families (two-hidden-layer MLPs with a thousand units per layer, a VGG-style convolutional stack, and wide residual networks) on a one-dimensional synthetic target, CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, SVHN, and ten tabular classification sets from the UCI repository.

## How they did it
They build the upper bound bottom-up. Because a composition of steepness-`k1` and steepness-`k2` maps is at worst `k1*k2`-steep, and the same is true across a chain of layers, the whole network is bounded by the product of its layers' constants — with the explicit caveat that the product is not usually the true constant even when each factor is individually exact. For an affine layer the exact constant under a given norm is the operator norm of the weight matrix, so the paper reduces to computing matrix norms: `ℓ1` is the largest absolute column sum, `ℓ∞` the largest absolute row sum, `ℓ2` the largest singular value. Pooling, ReLU and softmax all come out at or below one and are simply dropped from the product. Residual blocks are handled by subadditivity of norms — a skip plus a sub-network is bounded by one plus the sub-network's product. The two pieces of original work are both about layers that resist that clean treatment. For convolution they write the true linear operator explicitly as a block matrix whose blocks are circulant matrices of the individual filters, show that the `ℓ1`/`ℓ∞` norms of that matrix are obtainable in time linear in the number of weights, and then point out that the shortcut everyone else used — flattening the filter tensor into a matrix and taking its largest singular value — is not the norm of that operator, exhibiting cases where the two "differ greatly." For the spectral norm they adapt the power method so that each iteration is one forward convolution and one transposed convolution instead of a matrix times a vector, which keeps the cost near that of a single-sample forward/backward pass and automatically respects padding and stride. Training then becomes projected stochastic gradient: after each update, every weight matrix (and the scale vector of any batch-normalisation layer) is projected back onto the feasible set by rescaling it down to a single hyper-parameter `λ` if it exceeds it, so the whole network ends up bounded by `λ` raised to the depth. The paper is careful that the `ℓ2` case is approximate: a truncated power iteration can only underestimate a singular value, so the `ℓ2` constraint is applied to an estimate that is never above the truth, whereas `ℓ1`/`ℓ∞` are exact. Evaluation is by held-out accuracy: single-method comparisons against no regularisation, spectral decay as a competing spectral-norm method, dropout, and batch normalisation, plus each pairing and the full stack, at fixed schedules; on the tabular data it is two repetitions of five-fold cross-validated accuracy with a significance test designed for the overlap in that protocol, summarised in a rank diagram; a `λ` sweep on one architecture checks the dial's shape; and a plot of per-layer operator norms for the trained networks asks whether the other regularisers had been constraining steepness all along without saying so.

## What counts as an explanation here
A single scalar per layer and a product for the network: the explanation of "how steep is this model" is a number, computed from weights and nothing else, with a stated bound status (exact for two of the norms, an underestimate-based estimate for the third). It is a bound on the whole input-to-output map, so it is trivially transferable and worthless for saying *where* a network is steep or *why*. As a training intervention it is a constraint, so what the resulting model is certified to satisfy is exactly one inequality, and the paper is explicit that the inequality holds for the product bound and not for the model's true constant — meaning the certificate is on the analytic object, not on the network's behaviour. There is also a diagnostic product that is not presented as such: the per-layer norm plot for models trained by other means is a cheap, weight-only description of a model's steepness profile.

## How they judged success
Almost entirely by held-out classification accuracy against named baselines at matched schedules — no tightness metric, no measured-versus-true-constant comparison anywhere. The specific instruments: top-1 test accuracy on five image datasets for sixteen configurations, a significance-marked accuracy table plus a critical-difference rank diagram on ten tabular datasets, a validation-accuracy-versus-`λ` sweep, per-layer operator-norm plots, learning curves, and an assertion in prose that the extra cost per minibatch is small (exact linear cost for two norms, roughly one sample's forward-plus-backward pass for the third) with "a few percent" claimed for typical architectures. Notably absent: any report of how loose the product bound is relative to the true constant, any measurement of the trained networks' actual steepness under the norm they were constrained in, and any wall-clock table.

## What they found
Combined with batch normalisation the constraint is the strongest single addition on the image tasks: on VGG-style CIFAR-10 the unregularised baseline reaches 88.39%, the constraint alone 88.98%, batch normalisation 90.45%, and the pair 93.25%; on CIFAR-100 the same pairing moves a 56.14% baseline to 70.19%; on SVHN the VGG model goes from 96.95% to 98.03%. On the residual networks the method is roughly level with dropout rather than ahead (CIFAR-10: 95.64% for both), and on SVHN the residual-network gains are described as very small. On the tabular sets the `ℓ∞` variant with batch normalisation took the best mean accuracy on seven of ten datasets and the best average rank (2.6 against 5.9 for batch normalisation alone), but the paper says outright that the evidence does not establish a significant win over batch normalisation itself. The competing spectral-norm method was consistently worse than the `ℓ2` variant, and on the residual SVHN run it failed to converge (9.09% against 97.92%). Dropout is a reliable indicator of a significant degradation on the tabular sets, with the two synthetic distributions as the exception. The dial behaves as advertised only for `ℓ2`: too small underfits (even `λ=1` on a target that is itself unity-steep fails to capture the trend), larger improves, too large overfits. Norm choice split by data type — `ℓ∞` best on tabular, `ℓ2` best on natural images — and the paper notes the same `λ` value gives visibly different fits under different norms even in one input dimension, which it attributes to the feasible weight sets differing and thereby changing the optimisation path. The per-layer plots show unconstrained networks with much larger operator norms, dropout shaving them somewhat, and — the honest detail — that some layers constrained under `ℓ2` finished above the chosen `λ` once the norms were computed properly, because training had used a single power iteration. The batch-normalised networks were left off those plots because their per-layer norms varied by more than an order of magnitude in both directions.

## Limits and open problems they name
That last point is the general one they state: for `ℓ2` the constraint is only approximate, so anyone needing a guaranteed cap should use `ℓ1` or `ℓ∞`. They flag the uniform-`λ` assumption as possibly inappropriate and propose per-layer tuning; they note the treatment of recurrent networks as desirable but not attempted; they suggest extending to discriminator networks. What they do not name, and what matters more for us: the product bound is never compared to a true constant on any network of interesting size, so the "how steep is it really" question the paper opens with is answered only in the direction of an upper bound; the paper itself concedes that the product of per-layer constants is generally not the constant of the composition, which means the certificate can be far above the behaviour; accuracy is the only outcome measured, so the mechanism by which the constraint helps is inferred from correlations (the norm plots) rather than demonstrated; and the training procedure's own convergence dependence on the learning-rate schedule is visible in the curves without being analysed.

## What we take from it
A method, a measure and a warning, and the warning is the sharpest.
- **Measure:** the per-layer operator-norm *profile* of a trained network is the cheapest weight-only description of steepness we can produce, and this paper's plots are evidence that such a profile discriminates between training procedures — which makes it a natural first-class readout for our white-box account, printable before any probing.
- **Method:** the projected-gradient recipe is the reference way to *make* a bound hold rather than merely estimate it, and the exact-versus-estimated split between `ℓ1`/`ℓ∞` and `ℓ2` is the kind of statement every bound in our own account should carry about itself.
- **Warning (the one to keep):** the full-network constant here is a product of per-layer constants, which the authors explicitly say need not be the true constant of the composition — so the gap between reported and real steepness is guaranteed to exist and is never measured. Any number of this shape in our account must be labelled as a product bound and, wherever we can afford it, accompanied by an empirical constant estimated from input-output pairs, because the two differ by an unknown factor and only one of them is a property of the model's behaviour.
- **Second warning:** the `ℓ2` constraint being applied to a deliberate underestimate is a live failure mode, not a footnote — a bound that is computed with a truncated iterative method silently stops guaranteeing what it claims, and the trained networks here are the proof. For us: any iterative or sampled estimate of a bound is reported with its direction of error (conservative or optimistic) stated inline.
- **Framing:** this is the first place in the thread where the same scalar appears as *training objective*, *generalisation prior*, and *the quantity a verification paper later computes*, which supports run 647's hypothesis that the constant is the bridge variable between accounts — but the bridge is weaker than it looked, because the training side only ever pushes on the product bound and never checks whether the network it produced is actually as steep as claimed.
