# Where Tree Ensembles Decide: Structural Local Explanation with Decision Predicate Graphs

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.18382
- **Authors / organisation:** Sylvio Barbon Junior (University of Trieste, Department of Engineering and Architecture) and Luca Longo (University College Cork, School of Computer Science and Information Technology); single paper, journal submission to *Knowledge-Based Systems*
- **Date:** 2026-11-13
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.18382
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Feature attributions say how much each input mattered, counterfactuals say what input change would flip the answer, and structural explanations summarise the tree rules — but none of them names the *predicate* the model actually executed. The authors' question is narrower and more operational than either: for this frozen ensemble and this one input, which of the split thresholds the sample crossed is the one whose crossing would move the prediction most strongly toward a class of interest. They motivate it from explanatory debugging, where the goal is not to justify a prediction but to find out where it can be changed, and they want an answer that needs no external explainer, no search over input space, and no retraining. The artefact is a per-sample graph of the executed rules plus one selected threshold; the paper is as much an audit of that selection as a proposal.

## What they studied
Four tree-ensemble families of 100 estimators each — random forest, bagged trees, AdaBoost-SAMME, and gradient boosting — over 16 public numeric classification datasets (OpenML/UCI, 4 to 617 features, 2 to 26 classes), plus synthetic datasets with deliberately planted faults. The unit of analysis is the **executed predicate**: one internal-node test together with the branch the sample took, and the set of trees whose trace contains it. Nothing here is a neural network, so no neurons, features or directions are involved; the objects are thresholds, the rule-set of each tree, and the class support assembled from per-tree leaf contributions.

## How they did it
Each tree contributes its root-to-leaf route for the sample, which is a conjunction of executed tests; those routes are merged into one directed acyclic graph per sample (the local DPG) in which every node and edge carries the identity of the trees that traversed it. Class support is computed from the per-tree contributions through each family's own link function, not by counting routes per class, so the explanation's predicted class equals the model's by construction (their Proposition 1; they verify it on 342,040 samples with a maximum residual of order a rounding error). From the executed predicates they rank a **critical predicate** by an estimated gain: for each predicate label, cross that threshold in each of the trees that execute it, keep every other tree fixed, and add up how much the target class's support would rise. The estimate is explicitly first-order — it sums single-tree changes and ignores interactions between trees crossed together — and it is read off the stored traces with no input-space search. The selected threshold is then crossed in the *whole* model (one feature moved just past it, every tree re-evaluated) to see whether the prediction changes. Two target classes are used: the top competitor (where is the prediction contested?) and, for wrong predictions, the true class (can one crossing recover it?). Controls are a random executed predicate, the nearest threshold (smallest slack), the predicate shared by the most trees, and five explainer-guided predicates — TreeSHAP, LIME, Anchors, LORE and feature tweaking — each supplying the feature it ranks first, on which the nearest or the highest-gain predicate is taken, so the comparison is between predicates selected by different criteria and not between explainers. On top of that: an oracle that picks the executed predicate with the largest real whole-model change, a *gain-guided* arm in which every explainer's top feature is scored by the same gain rule, ten generated datasets with a planted faulty rule and a planted label-noise region, and a training-free repair experiment where localised predicates are edited (leaves neutralised, thresholds moved) and kept only if validation error on held-out data falls. Statistics are Wilcoxon signed-rank with Holm correction within each family and metric, plus Friedman/Nemenyi critical-difference comparisons over the controls defined on every sample.

## What counts as an explanation here
A verifiable record of the computation plus one named threshold. The local DPG is a graph (nodes = executed predicates with the trees that share them, sink nodes = classes) and is meant to be complete for one sample but *not* human-readable — the authors call it a record, not a summary, and the compact view they offer merely retains the shortest prefix of the ranked pivots that accounts for 80% of the positive estimated gain, which still needs 26 to 123 predicates. The deliverable to a person is the critical predicate: a feature, a threshold value, the sample's value and slack, the trees that execute it, and the projected effect of crossing it. It is a causal-adjacent, partial explanation — a proposal about where the prediction responds, with no minimality guarantee and no claim of completeness.

## How they judged success
Four research questions, each scored on measures that deliberately do not reuse the selection criterion. RQ1: **separation contrast** (among the 10 nearest training points with a different prediction versus the 10 nearest with the same one, the excess share lying on the other side of the threshold) and whether the counterfactual found by feature tweaking crosses the threshold; then the whole-model flip rate and change in competitor support. RQ2 (wrong predictions, 49,835 of them): **true-class recovery** under one crossing, plus the same separation measures taken against the true and predicted classes. RQ3: relative change in held-out test error after training-free edits. RQ4: runtime per sample and graph size. Baselines are the eight controls above, the oracle, and refitting. They also assess the artefact against a published twelve-property framework of explanation quality, marking four properties as not evaluated.

## What they found
The critical predicate separates differently-predicted neighbours from 0.262 to 0.336 (separation contrast) depending on family, against 0.105 to 0.157 for a random predicate and 0.038 to 0.144 for the nearest threshold, and it sits at the level of the TreeSHAP-guided predicate (0.344 to 0.358) without needing an attribution to be computed. The counterfactual from feature tweaking crosses the critical threshold in 31% to 40% of samples, against 6% to 8% for a random predicate. Whole-model flips: 0.181 (random forest) to 0.428 (bagging) of samples, against 0.052 to 0.129 for random, 0.007 to 0.052 for nearest, 0.115 to 0.356 for the most-shared predicate, and 0.010 to 0.106 for TreeSHAP-guided. When each explainer is given the same gain rule on its own top feature, the critical predicate still wins in 3 of 4 families over TreeSHAP-guided (0.147–0.372) and LIME-guided (0.121–0.316) — but the feature-tweaking arm matches it and beats it on AdaBoost (0.602 against 0.411). True-class recovery from one crossing is 46% to 69%, against 29% to 50% for contrastive TreeSHAP-guided and 0.388 to 0.777 for tweaking-guided (higher on AdaBoost). On planted faults the selection tests a faulty feature in 54% to 73% of the resulting errors and lands within 0.25 of the faulty threshold in 46% to 71%, against 6% to 21% at random; contrastive TreeSHAP points at the faulty feature more often (68% to 93%) but at the threshold less often, and the two combined reach 59% to 90%. The oracle trades magnitude for locality: it flips 46% to 75% of predictions, but through thresholds executed by 2 to 7 trees and a feature move of 1.62 to 2.13 standard deviations, where the critical predicate uses 8 to 27 trees and 0.88 to 1.34 standard deviations, and its separation contrast falls to 0.13–0.18. The estimated gain tracks the real change at a per-sample Spearman of 0.52 to 0.68. Training-free repair failed: 0.1 to 0.8 accepted edits per repetition, test error between −0.6% and +0.5%, none of 42 comparisons significant, while plain refitting moves test error by −11% to −15% (and +8.5% for AdaBoost). Cost is 87 to 461 ms per sample, comparable to TreeSHAP and LIME for the bagging families and up to 11 times slower for boosting; the graph averages 173 to 885 nodes.

## Limits and open problems they name
The method leans on thresholds being *shared* across trees, and sharing is thin exactly where one would want it: the average predicate is executed by 1.05 to 28.7 trees depending on dataset and family, and in random forests by only 1.05 to 2.37 outside one dataset; the correlation between sharing and flip rate is 0.05 to 0.47 across families, so reuse explains the effect only in part. On real data, recovery of the true class is not evidence that the predicate caused the error. The repair experiment is a null and the authors offer an untested explanation (too few errors to justify revising a threshold shared by many trees). The graph is large, the compact view still has tens to over a hundred predicates, the approach covers numeric-feature classification only (regression, categorical splits and histogram boosting are named as extensions), and every user-facing property — intelligibility, compactness, coherence across similar inputs — is left unevaluated.

## What we take from it
A **measurement discipline**, a **diagnostic**, and a **warning**. The discipline: the paper's whole comparative design rests on scoring a selector with metrics that do not contain the selector's own objective, and it goes one step further than most explanations papers by giving each competitor the *same* selection rule applied to *its* top feature — the gain-guided arm is what turns "our heuristic beats their heuristic" into "our search space beats their restriction of it". Our own probe and circuit rankings should be compared that way: same aggregation rule, different candidate sets, plus an oracle that shows what the best possible choice would buy. The diagnostic is reusable almost verbatim on a network: **the effect-size-versus-locality trade-off of the oracle**. Selecting by raw measured effect gave the largest flips but through components touched by few trees and inputs moved 1.6–2.1 SD from the sample, while the gain estimate preferred components many trees share and moves of under 1.4 SD — so an intervention that maximises the measured change is often an intervention on an atypical input, and an estimated, structural rule buys robustness by giving up magnitude. For a white-box predictor of response to intervention, that is a front-and-centre design choice, and the paper's separation and atypicality checks (does the flipped sample look like one the model ever saw?) are the two controls we should attach. The warning is sharper for us than for them: this is the folder's fourth decline of a structural-explanation paper on the same grounds, and this one earns its place only because it reports that locating the responsible threshold does **not** let you repair the model without refitting (−0.6% to +0.5% versus −11% to −15%) — naming where a prediction responds is a different claim from naming what is wrong with it, and only the second survives an intervention test.
