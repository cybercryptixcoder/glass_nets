- **Key:** arxiv-2603.21844

## What they wanted, and why
Constraint-based discovery — the PC family — prunes a complete graph by asking whether pairs of variables are conditionally independent given subsets of their neighbours. The number of such questions grows exponentially in the graph's maximum degree in the worst case, and the authors note this hurts twice: runtime and, because each question must be settled statistically on finite data, accuracy. They ask whether that test count can be improved without extra assumptions, and answer in a sharper currency than the field has used: instead of bounding by the maximum degree *d*, bound by the size *s* of the largest undirected clique in the essential graph (which satisfies s ≤ d+1 and is often far smaller). Their target is an algorithm whose test count matches a proved lower bound, plus an account of which assumptions its correctness actually needs.

## What they studied
No neural networks and no data-driven representation: the object is a partially directed graph (the essential graph of a Markov equivalence class), the resources are perfect oracles for conditional independence and dependence, and the unit of cost is one conditional-independence query. Empirical settings are linear-Gaussian structural equation models on Erdős–Rényi graphs (50 nodes with rising expected neighbourhood size, and rising node counts at fixed edge probability), Barabási–Albert scale-free graphs of 40 nodes, semi-synthetic single-cell expression from the SERGIO simulator's 100-gene 258-edge DS1 regulatory network with 2,700 cells, and a 6-variable Airfoil dataset.

## How they did it
The central move is to merge PC's adjacency search with PC's orientation phase, because orientation supplies *ancestral* information and ancestry is what makes adjacency tests cheap: given the full topological order, a single test settles the presence of any edge. They build on the prefix-node-set method of CCPG (Shiragur et al. 2024), which learns some ancestral relations in polynomially many tests and outputs only a coarse partition, and generalise its two definitions with a depth parameter *m*. Two families of tests are defined: a set of nodes shown to be downstream by exhibiting a pair that is independent under one conditioning set but becomes dependent when the candidate is added (their descendant set), and a set of nodes shown to have an ancestor outside the current prefix set because a dependence on a prefix node disappears once particular nodes are conditioned on. The algorithm grows a prefix set in rounds: for each depth ℓ it removes edges whose separation set lives inside the not-yet-placed nodes, computes the descendant and Meek-rule sets at that depth, deletes them from the candidate set, increments ℓ, and finally orients every surviving edge between different rounds forward. A stopping criterion follows from Dirac's result that minimal separators in a chordal graph are cliques, so the conditioning sets never need to be larger than the largest clique, bounding the maximum useful depth by *s*. The lower bound reuses a lemma from Zhang et al. (2024) that pins down exactly which conditioning sets can distinguish two DAGs differing by one undirected edge, together with a counting argument on the subsets of a maximum clique. Experiments compare against PC (stable variant), GSP at depths 4 and ∞, GRaSP², FCI and MARVEL where feasible, always re-using each baseline family's own CI tester (partial correlation with Fisher's z, significance 0.05 in the synthetic runs and 10⁻⁴ in the SERGIO and Airfoil runs) to keep the comparison fair across software packages.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not a representation of a model's internals but a *cost account of an identification procedure*: the explanation is an algorithm plus a theorem in the currency of queries, with an argument that no procedure in the same class can do better than exponentially in the clique size. It is partial in the usual causal-discovery sense — it returns the whole Markov equivalence class, not one DAG — and it is predictive about cost, not about behaviour: it says how many questions recover the class, not what any particular variable will do under intervention. The correctness claim is also weakened on purpose: they prove by a four-node linear-Gaussian counterexample that the algorithm can succeed where faithfulness fails, because the one test that would have exposed the violation is never scheduled once the collider is placed early.

## How they judged success
Three things: an upper bound on test count (p^O(s)) proved for infinite-sample distributions respecting a DAG, with a converse that p^O(d) was the prior state of the art and that d can be as large as Ω(p·s); a lower bound of 2^Ω(s) applying to *any* constraint-based algorithm, stated in an any-case form — for every collection of fewer than 2^s−s−1 tests there exists an indistinguishable equivalence class — which they note is strictly stronger than the earlier worst-case version; and empirical comparisons on normalised structural Hamming distance, skeleton precision/recall/F1, distinct-test counts and wall-clock, with runs averaged over 3, 5 or 10 seeds and shaded standard deviations. A variant, GAS+, replaces the final orientation loop with a quadratic number of extra tests and is reported as differing only when faithfulness is violated.

## What they found
The main theorem gives at most p^O(s) tests against the previous p^O(d); the lower bound gives 2^Ω(s) for any algorithm, so the gap is at most a logarithmic factor in the exponent. Empirically the algorithm is the fastest method in every experiment they run, and its accuracy profile is genuinely mixed rather than uniformly better: on 50-node Erdős–Rényi graphs its skeleton precision climbs from 0.42 to 0.93 as expected neighbourhood size rises from 15 to 45, but its recall falls from 0.92 to 0.75, while PC moves in the opposite direction (precision 0.83 down to 0.96, recall 0.29 down to 0.09 — PC's F1 is worse throughout but its recall collapses faster). On SERGIO, with a maximum out-degree of 76, PC and FCI are reported as computationally infeasible and only GRaSP² is a viable comparison; GAS+ is the most accurate while GAS is the fastest. On the Airfoil data GAS performs 46 distinct tests against PC's 104 and runs about twice as fast as GRaSP², but only PC identifies `attack` as exogenous — the authors trace this to a marginal independence between `frequency` and `chord` that becomes a dependence when conditioning on `attack`, which their ancestry rule reads as a collider, correctly under causal sufficiency but wrongly in the presence of a latent confounder. On scale-free graphs the accuracy order reverses sharply: at every tested connectivity PC's SHD is 105 to 305 while GAS and GAS+ are 283 to 477, i.e. the method is roughly 1.5 to 4 times worse on SHD there despite being 40 to 200 times faster. Their own sample-size table shows the same caution: on a dense 50-node graph GAS's skeleton SHD is essentially flat (585 at 50 samples, 605 at 100,000) while GAS+ falls from 598 to 476 and PC from 568 to 535.

## Limits and open problems they name
They separate efficiency from correctness and say the precise correctness conditions of their algorithm, and how they compare to other algorithms', remain unknown; finite-sample behaviour of those conditions is named as important future work. They flag that the method consumes both independence and dependence verdicts, so per-constraint significance levels and test families may need to differ, which they do not try. The Airfoil latent-confounder failure is reported rather than repaired (no FCI-style extension is run). Their lower bound is stated for the essential-graph recovery problem under the infinite-sample oracle, not for a budgeted finite-sample campaign.

## What we take from it
A measure and a warning. The measure: for a battery of probes, the natural resource is queries, and the right structural parameter may be much smaller than the obvious one — here the clique size of the equivalence class rather than the degree — and once you name the right parameter an exponent-optimal schedule exists. Concretely for us: **when we specify how expensive an identification campaign is, the cost parameter should be a property of the answer space (the largest local set of mutually competing accounts), not of the observation surface (how many sites the probe touches)**, and the lower bound tells us which queries are non-negotiable: the ones that cross every subset of the hardest local group. The warning is the accuracy flip: the method that asks the fewest questions is not the method that recovers the most structure, because it settles on a sparse, precise skeleton and misses edges; cost and recall trade off inside the same algorithm class, and the paper prints it rather than hiding it. The second transferable is the correctness argument by *never scheduling the fatal test*: the algorithm is right on a faithfulness-violating distribution because its ancestry-first order skips the one conditioning set that would have misled PC. That is a general and cheap pattern for us — an ordering that places well-supported parts of an account first can make later probes unnecessary and can immunise the account against a known confound it would otherwise trip on — and its price is equally general: the same shortcut is what makes the method accept a collider that a latent confounder would have explained.
