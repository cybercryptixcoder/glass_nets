- **Key:** arxiv-2611.20311

## What they wanted, and why
The authors work with urban planners who have to turn large street-level image collections and their captions into usable categories — "sidewalk in poor condition", "green walkability" — for which no label set exists and no classifier can be trained because nobody has defined the concept in machine-readable form. Their claim is that the obstacle is not retrieval quality but **record-keeping**: an exploratory analysis of a multimodal corpus moves through dozens of ad-hoc queries and set operations, and almost no tool preserves *why* a final collection of images was assembled, so the resulting concept cannot be defended to a stakeholder or transported to a second city. Reveal is a visual analytics system whose contribution is to make each step of that construction a first-class, inspectable object, and to treat the recorded chain as the reusable artefact. The larger goal is traceability for decisions made with models whose reasoning is otherwise invisible.

## What they studied
Two multimodal urban corpora: Project Sidewalk (crowdsourced sidewalk accessibility annotations over street-level imagery, with a broad but coarse tag taxonomy) and a set of geolocated Chicago street-level images enriched with captions generated by a language model. The unit of analysis is a **named set of records** — a node in an exploration graph holding images, tokens and metadata — not a neuron, feature or direction. The underlying representation is a frozen CLIP joint embedding, with UMAP used only for the 2D scatter views.

## How they did it
Preprocessing resizes and thumbnails the images, lemmatises the captions and keeps nouns, verbs, adjectives and adverbs, and maps image↔token relationships in both directions; CLIP then produces three vector families — image, token-text, and joint caption-image — and UMAP projects them to 2D, chosen over t-SNE and PCA for cluster legibility. Queries are of five kinds: text/image/joint search against the precomputed vectors by cosine similarity, selection inside an embedding scatter plot, filtering through the gallery and its top-20 token word cloud, polygon filtering on a map, and set union/intersection/difference between result sets. Every one of those operations writes a node into a dataflow diagram, coloured by type (search input, canvas refinement, set operation), numbered so that order is preserved for asymmetric operations like difference, with a red border marking the active parent. Sets can also be curated by hand into free-floating "buckets" that are not produced by any operator. A whole subgraph can be saved as a state — filters, similarity thresholds, node pipeline — and later replayed against a different corpus. The evaluation is two case studies run by one urban planner with more than eleven years of experience, followed by a semi-structured interview.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **recorded derivation**: a directed acyclic graph whose root is a seed query and whose every internal node is an operation with its parameters stored, so that any final collection can be re-derived step by step, inspected at an intermediate node, or transposed onto a new dataset. It is deliberately partial — it explains the *construction of a dataset*, never the model's judgement about an image, and nothing in the system predicts what a query will return before it is run. The framing that matters is what the diagram becomes on a second corpus: the demand for a shareable, replayable definition makes the first study's graph a specification, so the provenance record is promoted from a log of what happened into a description of what the concept *is*.

## How they judged success
No metric. Evidence is (i) that the workflow closes: the first study yielded eleven new categories and over 1,600 newly labelled images that the original taxonomy did not distinguish; (ii) that the blueprint **transports** — the same node sequence rebuilt on Chicago recovered spatially coherent and *different* patterns per category (South Chicago for eroded curb edges, transport corridors for compound degradation, construction nodes isolating temporary work zones, and a tree-intersection branch flipping the downtown concentration into two residential clusters); and (iii) one expert's self-report in a semi-structured interview over three themes. There is no baseline system, no second expert, no task timing and no inter-annotator agreement.

## What they found
The taxonomy-extension path is the substantive result: a seed text query, then a *difference* against hand-picked exemplars of well-maintained sidewalk to isolate damaged sidewalk, then word-cloud terms from that node as fresh text queries, then intersections between condition sets and feature sets (tactile pavement, curb ramp) to carve out combined conditions nobody had tagged, then an embedding-level split of the remaining node into "under construction", "slippery", "water accumulation". The expert reported that text queries were good at broad categories while image queries found patterns that are hard to name in keywords, and that the graph turned the analysis from a linear sequence into branchable work. Transposition is the second finding: reproducing the node sequence in Chicago is what made the two analyses comparable, since restarting from scratch on new data would have produced a differently-defined notion of walkability.

## Limits and open problems they name
The system leans on CLIP similarities and the authors flag the representation as the ceiling, naming a replacement of CLIP as future work. Provenance in this design is *operator-level and manual*: a node is born from an interface action, so an inference nobody wrote down — why these three exemplars were chosen as "well maintained" — is invisible in the graph, and the labels ultimately rest on the expert's visual judgement of a handful of images. There is no uncertainty anywhere, no measure of whether a node's contents are stable under a threshold change, and the evaluation is one expert on two datasets.

## What we take from it
Three things. (a) **A framework**: promote the derivation chain from a log to the published object. Our read-outs — a probe trained on this split, a circuit found by this ablation, a concept assembled from these activation exemplars — are exactly the case where the final number is defensible only through the sequence that produced it, and the numbered-node discipline (order preserved so difference is well-defined; the active parent marked) is the minimal bookkeeping that makes a composed read-out re-derivable and testable against a different corpus or model. (b) **A validation move worth copying**: the acceptance test for the record is *transposition* — does rebuilding the same operation sequence on a new dataset produce something coherent and correctly different — which is the analogue of a probe's transport test and is cheaper and more informative than re-scoring on the same inputs. (c) **A warning by omission**: the recorded chain here contains only the operations, so the judgements inside exemplar selection vanish; if our battery records provenance it must record the *seeds and thresholds* too, or the trace will look complete and be reproducible only up to the choices that mattered most.
