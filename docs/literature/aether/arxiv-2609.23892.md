# Circuit-Diff: Factual Edit-based Intervention Method for Localizing Knowledge in Attribution Graphs

- **Key:** arxiv-2609.23892
- **Authors / organisation:** Edward G. Friedman, Xiangchen Song; School of Computer Science / Machine Learning Department, Carnegie Mellon University, Pittsburgh, PA 15213
- **Date:** 2026-09-20
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.23892
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Attribution graphs built from cross-layer transcoders are the current standard way to approximate a circuit, but their nodes are unlabelled features: a practitioner prunes the graph and then works out by hand what each surviving node means, and the automatic descriptions that exist have been reported as causally unfaithful under intervention. The authors want to make the *selection* step — deciding which features deserve human attention at all — into a causal computation rather than a reading exercise. Their lever is knowledge editing: if a single fact is overwritten in the weights, then whatever moved inside the attribution graph is, by construction, the part of the model the fact ran through. The larger goal is a tool that turns "which of these thousands of features carry this fact" into a short ranked list with causal backing, for auditing and debugging rather than for describing everything.

## What they studied
Gemma-2-2B and the released 426k-feature cross-layer transcoder for it (Piotrowski and Hanna, 2025), which replaces the model's MLPs with transcoders whose decoders write to later layers. The unit of analysis is the *feature node* of an attribution graph — feature `i` at token position `t` — together with its edges. The intervention is a fact edit `(s, r, o → o*)` applied with MEMIT on the CounterFact dataset, at the layer band the authors' causal-tracing reproduction selects (layers 3–10), one fact per model throughout. Two further edits from the sweep serve as case studies (Taj Mahal, religion; Pentium II, developer).

## How they did it
Five steps. (1) Apply MEMIT to the base model to get an edited model that answers `o*` instead of `o` on the prompt. (2) Build two attribution graphs through the *same frozen* CLT — one on the base model, one on the edited model — so that node identities are comparable across the pair; unlike the standard attribution-graph computation, which seeds the influence computation with all top logits, they seed a single target logit at unit weight (`o`'s in the base graph, `o*`'s in the edited one) so that a node's influence is the share of that one logit's attribution flowing through it and is comparable across the two graphs. (3) For every node compute the change in activation, in influence, and the change in its outgoing edge weights, plus a normalized activation movement in [-1, 1]; nodes whose movement is maximal are "disappeared" (candidates for the old object) or "appeared" (candidates for the new one). (4) Because appeared/disappeared as defined cover most of the graph, add a **null calibration**: take, from the same edit's *neighbourhood* prompts — different subjects sharing the relation and object, which elicit unedited knowledge — the chosen percentile of raw activation movement, and keep only nodes that move further than that floor. The edit supplies its own null. (5) Rank each side by a heuristic weighting the normalized movement by the influence change, and compare that ranking against two method-free baselines over the same nodes (rank by activation, rank by influence) and against random draws count-matched at every rung. Verification is by constrained patching in the base model: clamp a node's value by a multiplier and add it to the MLP output over a window of layers, sweeping the window endpoint, and score the patch's effect on the target logit as a fraction of the edit's own effect on a centred-logit basis, so a score of one reproduces the edit. One more lens is trained — an additive delta over the frozen CLT, restricted to the slices the edit band most directly invalidates — so that a fine-tuned lens can be compared against the frozen one with the model held fixed, and that fine-tuned lens is also read back on the *unedited* model as a control corner for what fine-tuning alone does.

## What counts as an explanation here
A ranked, causally filtered list of features plus a diff report — which nodes and edges moved, by how much, on which side — attached to an existing attribution graph. It is explicitly partial: it does not label the features, and the paper's interpretive claims are read off the CLT's own released feature dashboards (tokens a feature promotes, contexts where it fires) rather than written by the authors or by a language model. Predictively it claims only a local statement: the selected set, patched in, reproduces a substantial share of the edit's effect on the target logit. The paper's own framing of the risk is the sharpest thing in it — a localization can be wrong and still look convincing, because a ranked list with readable dashboards invites belief in a circuit when what has been found is movement inside an approximation.

## How they judged success
Three families of measure. *Lens reliability*: completeness and replacement scores of the attribution graph, plus per-layer cosine activation similarity, compared across the edit, paraphrase and neighbourhood prompt buckets between the base and edited graphs, over 20 trials of one edit each. *Set size*: over 500 CounterFact edits, the share of each side of the graph covered under three readings of "changed" — direction, presence, and passing the null floor — and the share of the top-q per cent by influence that each reading captures, against chance. *Causal verification*: constrained-patching group ablations on up to 24 edits (12 for the six-score comparison), always count-matched against the influence and activation baselines and a random draw, reported with and without the null floor, with per-edit sign tests at a stated replicate-noise threshold. They also ablate five alternative ranking heuristics. A separate multi-edit sweep over a ladder of facts-per-model from one to ten thousand measures how the frozen lens degrades as edits accumulate.

## What they found
The frozen lens is damaged exactly where the edit lands and hardly anywhere else: on edited prompts the replacement score falls by about 0.3 at the median while the less strict completeness score falls only about 0.06, and on neighbourhood prompts neither moves appreciably. The authors read the gap as error nodes absorbing what the lens can no longer model. Fine-tuning the lens on the edited model recovers little, and their diagnostic for why is the interesting part: the parity stopping rule fires at the first evaluation for every one of the 20 trials, so one edit leaves almost nothing global for a fine-tune to correct — the adapted lens at this scale is a minimally perturbed frozen one. Raw appeared/disappeared is far too permissive on its own: over 500 edits the gained side covers 96.2 per cent of the graph by direction and 94.4 per cent by presence, the lost side 48.2 and 27.2 per cent; the null floor collapses these to 14.0 and 3.2 per cent. Causally, single nodes barely move the logit (as the CLT authors also report), and per node the whole-graph influence ranking is ahead of their heuristic on the suppress side at every rung and on the unfloored inject side. Patching the whole top-q per cent set together tells a better story on the inject side: at q = 5 per cent the selected set moves the target logit further than a count-matched influence ranking (9.98 against 5.90 centred logits unfloored, 8.16 against 4.94 floored), while on the suppress side the two are comparable unfloored (−42.4 against −41.3) and the baseline is ahead floored (−24.8 against −35.3). Four of the five alternative scores are indistinguishable from the chosen one, because they all multiply by a movement term that is saturated at its ceiling for nearly every node; the one edge-only score tracks random. The case study is the qualitative payoff: the top disappeared features read as French history, geography and Paris contexts, the top appeared features as Italy, Roman antiquity, Roman mythology and the papacy, and the moved nodes' geometry matches where the edit writes (a band across the edited layers at the subject's last token). Their recurrence count across the 25 pooled edits separates the two kinds of feature: one Paris feature recurs in exactly the other French facts, while three others recur across a dozen unrelated edits with unreadable dashboards and sit inside the edit band, which the authors read as tracking the write itself rather than the fact, and tell the reader to discount.

## Limits and open problems they name
The method rests on the edit having succeeded and on the frozen lens remaining usable afterwards, and the authors do not measure the first dependence directly. The second they call the more fundamental limitation: holding the lens fixed preserves the meaning its features had for the base model but does not guarantee that meaning still describes the edited one, and the damage bound is inferred from two aggregate scores. The null floor, which is what makes the output a short list at all, is not tuned systematically. The causal evidence is group patches over 24 edits — enough for a per-edit sign test, not for a tight effect estimate — and on the suppress side the advantage over simply reading off influence is small or absent. They inherit the known problems of causal tracing and MEMIT (layer choice, inconsistent application, side effects at scale) and of the CLT basis itself, whose mechanistic faithfulness has been questioned where ground truth exists. Grouping returned features by shared role rather than taking the top q per cent of a side whole is named as likely a sharper intervention unit and left to future work.

## What we take from it
A **method and a warning**, both directly usable. Method: an intervention that is *semantic* rather than an input corruption — change one stored fact, hold the prompt fixed, and the difference in the model's own attribution structure is a discovery signal without needing a task-specific clean/corrupted pair; and, more transferable, the **edit-as-its-own-null** construction: the intervention's collateral prompts define the distribution of movement that the intervention does *not* mean, so the floor is calibrated from the intervention itself instead of from a matched control the analyst has to invent. Warning, and the sharper one: a set-difference reading of "what changed" is nearly vacuous until floored — 96 per cent of the graph "appeared" — so any of our own changed-unit reports must print, beside the count, the share of the whole and the chance share at the same size, or the report is a graph-painting. Third, a **measure**: recurrence of a unit across unrelated interventions separates knowledge from mechanism (a feature that recurs in exactly the related facts versus one that recurs in a dozen unrelated ones and sits where the method writes), which is a cheap cross-run filter we can adopt as-is and which no single-run ranking can produce.
