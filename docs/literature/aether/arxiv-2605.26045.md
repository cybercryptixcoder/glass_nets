## What they wanted, and why
An activation oracle is a second language model trained to read another model's hidden state and say in words what is in it, and it is the read-out form our own work leans on whenever we want a natural-language description of a state. The practical problem is that its answer arrives as a sentence with no accompanying uncertainty: in an audit someone has to compare a score against a threshold to flag a model or release it, and an uncalibrated score puts that threshold in the wrong place, so a pipeline acts confidently on wrong answers and discards confident right ones. The authors — who note that the oracle's own authors listed missing calibration as a main limitation — ask a narrow, checkable question: does the oracle know when it is right? They treat the oracle as a *steered* model rather than an ordinary one, since while it answers, vectors copied from the target model are added to part of its own hidden state, and it is not obvious that calibration methods developed for unsteered models survive that intervention. The larger goal is to make a learned interpretability interface usable by a decision-maker, which is exactly the interface class our read-outs belong to.

## What they studied
Four activation oracles spanning two families and a 3.3× size range: Qwen3-8B and Gemma-2-9B (the released Karvonen et al. oracles), a community Gemma-3-27B oracle, and a Qwen3.6-27B oracle the authors train themselves — the first oracle for a hybrid Gated DeltaNet / Gated Attention architecture. The task is the secret-word game: 20 target models, each a LoRA adapter on the same base chat model as its oracle, fine-tuned to hint at one word without ever writing it, so every sample has an exact answer that can come only from the activations. Unit of analysis is the *answer-plus-confidence pair* the oracle emits under the steering hook; the objects compared are five confidence estimators, not model internals. Sample size is 6,000 per method and oracle (20 words × 100 context prompts × 3 verbalizer prompts), 19–21 method rows per oracle across temperature grids.

## How they did it
The injection is stated precisely: the target's last ten token hidden states are copied into ten placeholder positions of the oracle's prompt at its layer 1, each scaled to the norm of the oracle's own vector at that position, so the target contributes a direction and not a magnitude; the coefficient defaults to 1, and the paper explains the design choice — overwriting rather than adding had grown norms by about five orders of magnitude in the original training. Five estimators are then built on top of that hook. Log probability is the joint probability of the answer's tokens. The "bootstrap" samples twenty answers at temperature T and reports the modal word with its frequency. Direct numeric self report asks the oracle for a number from 0 to 100 and divides by 100. Constrained label scoring asks the same question but forces one of five labels and reads the softmax over them, with the expected value, P(very high) and P(high or very high) as three variants. Forced choice forces each of the twenty candidate words under the hook, sums its token log-probabilities, and normalises across the twenty. Evaluation reports accuracy, expected calibration error with ten equal-width bins, the Brier score, negative log likelihood, AUROC of confidence versus correctness, and selective-prediction curves, each with bootstrap standard errors over 1,000 resamples; a nested bootstrap over words, context prompts and verbalizer prompts re-checks that the method ordering is not an artefact of the twenty chosen words. Post hoc calibration (temperature, Platt, isotonic, beta) is fit on held-out halves and reported split by word as well as at random. Three further readouts built on the steering itself (power-sampling acceptance rate and agreement, and sensitivity to the injection coefficient) are reported in full as negatives.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not an explanation of the model at all — a *reliability statement about the explanation's producer*. The contribution is a decision rule over confidence estimators, and the thing being calibrated is a verbalised read-out of a hidden state, not a feature, direction or circuit. Its form is a conditional procedure: what an auditor should do depends on what the auditor possesses (a candidate list, a labelled set, neither). It is deliberately partial — it says nothing about whether the oracle's prose is *true* in general, only how confident one should be in a single-token answer on a task with an exact answer.

## How they judged success
Because a low calibration error can be bought by giving every answer the same number near the pooled accuracy, they always report ECE *and* AUROC together, and add selective-prediction curves for the auditor who acts only on the most confident answers; proper scoring rules (Brier, NLL) are reported alongside. Baselines are the five estimators against each other on every oracle, an ablation of the temperature grid, an ablation of the number of bootstrap samples (k = 3, 5, 10, 20), a held-out temperature-selection rule, and an activation-free floor of one correct answer in twenty, which every method clears on every oracle.

## What they found
The ordering is identical on all four oracles and survives both bootstraps (Table 6 reports 100% agreement across 1,000 resamples on the key comparisons). **Forced choice wins everything**: accuracy 0.646 / 0.533 / 0.590 / 0.550 against free-text log probability 0.414 / 0.234 / 0.217 / 0.433, AUROC 0.940 / 0.936 / 0.922 / 0.959, ECE 0.078 / 0.080 / 0.090 / 0.131. On the two lowest-accuracy oracles the accuracy more than doubles. The mechanism is diagnosed, not just measured: **98–100% of incorrect free-text answers name none of the twenty words** (they say "secret", "password", "yes", or nothing), and in 32–48% of exactly those samples forced choice recovers the secret word anyway — surface-form competition, where a generic string eats probability that belongs to the answer. Even where *all twenty* bootstrap samples miss the word, forced choice finds it in 23–30% of cases. **Direct numeric self report is the worst estimator on every oracle** (ECE 0.562–0.783, AUROC 0.404–0.531, and 0.500 ± 0.000 on Gemma-2-9B because it writes "100" essentially always); on Qwen3.6-27B the stated number is on average *higher for wrong answers*. Scoring five fixed labels with the logits restores a usable signal (AUROC 0.635–0.813), and on both 27B oracles P(very high) is among the best-calibrated confidence of all methods (ECE 0.069 and 0.088) — the information is in how probability spreads over labels, not in which label is most likely, which is "very high" almost always. For free text, the bootstrap at T = 1.0 is the safest label-free default (worst-oracle ECE 0.177, better than any other free-text method's worst case), ten of twenty samples carry most of the gain, and five labelled words are enough to tune the temperature near its optimum (Qwen3-8B 0.075, Qwen3.6-27B 0.088, Gemma-2-9B 0.069, Gemma-3-27B 0.124 against oracle-best 0.057 / 0.077 / 0.040 / 0.117). With labels, post hoc rescaling drives nearly every method's ECE toward zero (on Gemma-3-27B, direct self report goes from 0.230 to 0.009) — and that is the trap, because rescaling is monotone and cannot change AUROC, so AUROC is all that separates methods once labels exist. Per-word accuracy varies by an order of magnitude (Qwen3-8B: 0.827 for "moon" against 0.133 for "chair" on Gemma-2-9B). All three steering-derived readouts fail: acceptance rate AUROC 0.49–0.60, agreement at five times the cost with higher ECE, coefficient-sensitivity limited by having only five possible confidence values.

## Limits and open problems they name
All four oracles come from one training recipe, so the ordering is untested against a differently trained oracle (they name the conversational-data recipe as the natural next test). The task is single-token with a known twenty-word space, so the most important audit case — hunting a hidden objective you cannot list — is covered only by the label-free AUROC argument, not by a calibrated probability. Calibration is measured per answer word and not per claim. The authors add that calibration is not a guarantee: a pipeline that acts on calibrated scores still acts on confident wrong answers, and they flag the dual-use risk of the same tooling pointed at private fine-tunes.

## What we take from it
(1) **A control our own read-outs owe: report separation and calibration separately, and never let a post hoc rescaling stand in for information.** The demonstration is decisive and cheap to copy — fitting a calibrator on labelled data took the *worst* estimator (a number the oracle writes) from ECE 0.230 to 0.009 on Gemma-3-27B while leaving its AUROC at chance, because a monotone map cannot reorder answers. Any of our verbalised read-outs that reports "confidence is now calibrated" after fitting a temperature on held-out data has reported nothing about whether the confidence is informative. (2) **Constrained output beats free generation, and the mechanism generalises past oracles.** Surface-form competition is the same failure that makes a natural-language feature description score poorly against a candidate list: if a read-out is asked to name something out of an unbounded vocabulary, generic strings absorb the probability mass. The reusable instrument is forced choice over a known candidate set, and this paper prices it — up to more than double the accuracy, AUROC 0.92–0.96, with a *matched probe* that shows the gain is recovery of information the free-text arm already had but did not print (32–48% of the samples where free text said nothing). (3) **A self-report is not a read-out, and this is the cleanest published instance in the folder**: the same question asked twice — once as free text, once as five scored labels — gives no signal versus a usable one, and the failure is attributed to the chat-model habit of saying "very high" plus the written number's direction being unrelated to correctness. The structural lesson for us is the one the model already encodes internally: the uncertainty exists in the activations while the verbalisation of it does not track it, so *score the hypothesis, do not ask for the confidence*. (4) **A warned-about scope**: everything here is measured against an exact answer, and the estimated confidences are worth exactly their AUROC on the stratum we care about. Before our write-ups cite calibration numbers they should say which stratum produced them and whether that stratum resembles the deployment question.
