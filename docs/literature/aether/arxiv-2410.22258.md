# LipKernel: Lipschitz-Bounded Convolutional Neural Networks via Dissipative Layers

- **Key:** arxiv-2410.22258

## What they wanted, and why
Robust-by-construction networks with a guaranteed Lipschitz bound already exist, but the ones that hug the bound closely enough to be expressive pay for it at inference: convolution layers parameterised in the Fourier domain have to be evaluated by transform and inverse transform (or as full-image-size kernels), which is unacceptable in a real-time feedback loop. The authors want the confident side of that family - bounds proved by linear matrix inequalities, not by the crude product of layer norms - without the inference-time tax, and they want it to cover the layer zoo an actual CNN uses (pooling, stride, dilation, zero padding) rather than the Fourier methods' circular-padding, power-of-two-image corner. A secondary aim is architectural: give the same unconstrained-optimisation interface as the other direct parameterisations, so training is plain SGD on free variables. The motivating domain is control and robotics, where a bound on the map is a stability prerequisite rather than a robustness metric.

## What they studied
Convolutional classifiers, small (two convolutional plus two fully-connected layers, 16 and 32 channels, kernel sizes 4, on 32x32 MNIST; and a variant with average pooling), compared against a vanilla net, almost-orthogonal layers, Fourier-domain orthogonal layers and Fourier-domain "Sandwich" layers. The unit of analysis is the **layer as an operator plus its certificate**: each layer is parameterised so that an LMI holds by construction, and the end-to-end bound is a telescope sum over per-layer gain matrices. They also study single layers in isolation, timing forward evaluation across channel counts, image sizes and kernel sizes with randomly generated weights. The bound itself is generalised to weighted norms on input and output, so "Lipschitz constant 1" becomes a family of ellipsoidal reachability statements rather than a circle.

## How they did it
First they recast the statement "this network is (Q,R)-Lipschitz" as a chain of per-layer inequalities: an affine or convolutional layer followed by a slope-restricted activation is *incrementally dissipative* with respect to a supply rate that compares an input difference weighted by a positive-definite matrix X_prev against an output difference weighted by X, and the chain telescopes so that summing all layer inequalities leaves (input spread under R) minus (output spread under Q) non-negative. Then they observe that a 1-D or 2-D convolution can be written exactly as a linear state-space system of Roesser type - the kernel taps sit in the read-out matrices C and D, and the shift dynamics A and B are fixed - which converts the LMI condition into a block matrix inequality over (A,B,C,D) plus the activation multiplier. Since a general 2-D Lyapunov equation has no solution, they exploit the nilpotency and block-triangular structure of the FIR realisation: the vertical state block decouples, so P2 is solved first as an ordinary Lyapunov sum, the horizontal block is then solved with a Schur complement that absorbs P2, and the whole thing is made feasible by adding free terms H1,H2 and a small epsilon. With P fixed, the kernel matrices are produced by inverting the Cayley transform - the skew-symmetric free variable is mapped to a pair of matrices whose stacked product is an isometry - so the inequality closes algebraically rather than by projection. Fully-connected layers reuse a known bijective parameterisation, and the paper states that this parameterisation is not only sufficient but necessary for the LMI, i.e. on that layer type nothing is lost. Extensions replace the gain with a claimed one for average pooling (a scalar rescale) and restrict the gain to be diagonal (with a re-derived formula) when max pooling is present, because max pooling is not linear. Experiments: train the small CNNs for 20 epochs with three initialisations at four target bounds, report clean accuracy, the **empirical lower** Lipschitz bound, a certified robust accuracy from the margin divided by the bound, and PGD accuracy at three radii; separately time inference over the three parameterisations with the same random weights.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **reparameterisation plus a certificate**, and a deliberately partial one. The object that does the explaining is the LMI: it is a small set of matrix inequalities that every trained layer satisfies, and the claim is that satisfaction implies the property for the whole network. What it does *not* give is a statement about what any particular trained layer computes - the free variables (Y, Z, H1, H2, gamma, b) have no interpretation, and the paper never inverts its own map to say what a trained kernel looks like. The predictiveness is one-directional and reliable: given a checkpoint, the bound holds; given the bound, nothing follows about the weights beyond the residual gap between the claimed constant and the realised one. The paper does add a small prediction to the story - its 2-layer, 2-unit regression figure shows a dissipative parameterisation fitting a cosine where spectrally normalised weights cannot, because its layers are allowed spectral norm above one as long as the composition is contractive - which is the sharpest statement in the paper that "every layer contractive" is a strictly smaller class than "composition contractive".

## How they judged success
Inference wall-clock against two Fourier-domain baselines, averaged over ten initialisations, swept across channels (10-500), image size and kernel size, with the axis labels printed (the timings themselves appear only as Figure 4's log-scale panels, not as a table). Downstream: clean MNIST test accuracy, certified robust accuracy at three epsilons, PGD accuracy at three radii, and an **empirical lower** Lipschitz estimate plotted against the target bound. Controls are weak by the standards of the rest of this folder: no attack-strength sweep, no AutoAttack, no comparison at matched realised bound, three seeds, twenty epochs. The claim "more expressive than spectral bounds or orthogonal layers" is supported by one accuracy column on one dataset plus the two-dimensional regression cartoon, not by a bound-matched comparison.

## What they found
Inference is the headline and it is a clean two-to-three-order-of-magnitude win: LipKernel layers evaluate in well under a millisecond to about 100 ms across the whole sweep, while the Fourier methods run from roughly 10 ms to over 10 s and worsen steeply with channels and image size; kernel size barely matters for any of the three. Downstream the picture is much flatter. On the 2C2F architecture at claimed bound 1, Sandwich has the best clean accuracy (97.3%) and LipKernel second (96.6%) ahead of Orthogon (94.6%); at bound 2 and 4 Sandwich again leads clean accuracy while LipKernel and Orthogon sit close together. Against AOL on the pooling architecture, LipKernel is clearly ahead (91.7% vs 88.7% at bound 1). In robustness under PGD at the widest radius, Sandwich leads on every LipKernel row in the 2C2F table (72.0 vs 67.8 at bound 1; 54.0 vs 39.7 at bound 4), so the Fourier parameterisation that is being beaten on speed is not being beaten on robustness. Notably the empirical **lower** bound sometimes exceeds the claimed certificate - 3.110 against a target of 4 on 2C2F - which the paper does not comment on. The vanilla network has a lower-bound estimate of 221.7 (2C2F) and 148.0 (2CP2F) and certifies nothing, and its PGD accuracy at the largest radius is within a few points of the constrained nets (59.6 vs 67.8 at bound 1), which is the standard reminder that on this dataset the gradient attack is not where the bounds bite.

## Limits and open problems they name
The extended-supply-rate machinery (weighted input and output norms, residual and monotone variants) is introduced in the problem statement and then not used anywhere in the experiments - every trained model uses the plain bound. Strided and dilated convolutions are handled by citing another paper's state-space construction and are omitted for space, so the "wide variety of layers" in the abstract is a claim about feasibility, not a measured result. Max pooling forces the gain matrix diagonal, a restriction the authors inherit and cite rather than analyse. The paper also does not report training-time cost, which its own framing makes central: the parameterisation requires matrix inverses and Cholesky factorisations per layer per step, and unlike the inference claim this is never timed or compared. The empirical bound is reported as a lower estimate only, so whether the certificate is tight at a given checkpoint is inferable but never stated.

## What we take from it
A **framing** we can copy verbatim, and a **measure** we should adopt: "incremental dissipativity of every layer with respect to a weighted supply rate" is exactly the compositional form a predictive account wants, because it is a per-layer property that composes by telescoping with no global optimisation and produces an ellipsoidal region rather than a scalar bound - and the ellipsoid is the object our account would have to predict anyway. The mechanical lesson is the *state-space realisation as an interface*: converting a convolution into (A,B,C,D) with the kernel taps in the readout is what lets a control-theoretic certificate be applied at all, and the same conversion is what makes the layer readable - the certificate lives in a matrix whose entries are the weights, not in a property of a reshaped tensor. A **warning** too: this paper's speed claim is measured and its expressivity claim is asserted, and the two appear in the same abstract at the same volume. Two more concrete leads for us: the necessity/sufficiency remark on the fully-connected parameterisation means that, on that layer type, the LMI class *is* the Lipschitz class - a rare exact equivalence between a constrained family and a semantic property, and worth having on record; and the paper's own citation trail (the 1-D Cayley-plus-Gramian paper, the Roesser state-space realisations, and the "unified algebraic perspective" whose diagonal-dominance lemma is used in the proof) points at a control-systems literature that has been doing weight-level guarantees for CNNs in parallel with the interpretability one, largely uncited across the boundary.
