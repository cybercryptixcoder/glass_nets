# Static analysis of ReLU neural networks with tropical polyhedra

- **Key:** arxiv-2108.00893
- **Authors / organisation:** Eric Goubault, Sébastien Palumby, Sriram Sankaranarayanan, Sylvie Putot, Louis Rustenholz; LIX, École Polytechnique / CNRS / Institut Polytechnique de Paris (Sankaranarayanan: University of Colorado at Boulder)
- **Date:** 2021-08-23 (v2; v1 2021-07-30)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.00893
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (v2 HTML, sections 1–7 plus the section-4 embedding theorems and Appendices 0.A–0.I)
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why

The paper asks how to bound the outputs of a ReLU feedforward network given a box of inputs, and it treats this range estimate as the primitive behind robustness checking, spec compliance and reachability analysis of learned controllers. The authors' complaint is that the two standard families both disappoint on ReLUs: derivative-based bounds do not apply to a piecewise-linear function, enumerating activation patterns explodes even on small nets, and convex relaxations are coarse precisely because the function they relax is not convex. Their answer is to import a technique from program verification that was built for exactly this difficulty — abstract interpretation over a domain that can hold disjunctions — and their larger goal is a hierarchy of approximations to the function a ReLU net computes, of which this paper is the bottom rung. The framing that makes the paper interesting outside verification is stated almost in passing: a ReLU network is a rational map in the tropical (max-plus) semiring, and the abstract domain it suggests is therefore *shaped like the function* rather than like the geometry around it.

## What they studied

Feedforward ReLU multilayer perceptrons, in practice small: the experiments use hand-built nets of 4 to 13 neurons, learned controllers from the Sherlock benchmark distribution, and randomly generated single-hidden-layer nets with weights drawn between −2 and 2, scaled up to 100 inputs and 100 hidden neurons. The unit of analysis is not a neuron or a feature but a **relation over all variables at once** — the graph of each layer's affine map, the reachable set at each layer, and the input-to-output relation of the whole network, all held as sets in a tropical space with one coordinate per neuron.

## How they did it

The key structural observation is that the abstraction is asymmetric between the two halves of a layer. The ReLU is *exact* in tropical algebra, so applying it to a set costs nothing: it is applied coordinatewise to the generators. The affine map is the hard half, because an ordinary linear map is not tropical-linear; the authors handle it by first computing the tightest **zone** (a difference-bound-matrix domain) containing the graph of the map over an input box, then converting that zone into a tropical polyhedron. Both conversions are given as theorems with matching internal (extreme-point) and external (max-of-affine-inequality) descriptions, plus a theorem showing a zone on n variables is exactly the tropical convex hull of n+1 specific generators. An octagon version is then built on the same pattern by lifting the variables into positive and negative copies and intersecting the resulting tropical polyhedron with a linear manifold, which buys sum-type constraints that zones cannot express. For multilayering, the algorithm computes the relation for each consecutive layer pair, embeds it into the full-dimension space, intersects it with the embedded previous abstraction, and projects back out — either onto input-and-output only, or onto all layers. A separate theorem gives the non-obvious part of the internal route: embedding into a larger space naively costs a factor of two per added coordinate, and the extreme points of the embedded object are the original generators at the lower bound plus only those generators that are *not* absorbable (in the max-plus sense) by any other generator, at the upper bound. Property checking then proceeds by taking the smallest zone containing the final polyhedron and solving a linear program for the minimum of the property's affine function over that zone — or, when the property needs the polyhedron itself, its minimum is named as a non-convex problem they leave to future work. Precision is improved by subdividing the input box and taking tropical unions, with closed-form external descriptions given for the scalar case and a sound, stronger-than-the-base set of extra constraints given for the general affine case.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **sound over-approximation of a set**, with two interchangeable presentations of the same object. It is not a description of the network's internals and it does not attribute anything; the account of layer k is a polyhedron relating the input coordinates to layer k's coordinates, and the account of the whole network is the projection of that object onto input-output. Completeness is explicitly partial: the abstraction is exact for ReLU and inexact for affine maps, and the paper is honest that the residual conservatism is the price of representing a tropical *rational* function by a tropical *linear* one. It does predict — it will tell you that a region of inputs cannot reach a specified output region — but it predicts only membership properties, and it says nothing about which parts of the network are responsible.

## How they judged success

Three measures, all qualitative rather than benchmarked. (a) **Soundness proofs** for each construction — both directions of the representation equivalences, the zone-to-tropical conversion, the octagon embedding, the affine abstraction in both descriptions, the embedding theorem, and the subdivision constraints. (b) **Exactness on worked examples**: whether the returned interval equals the true interval on a tiny network, with the paper's own `multi` example reporting exact ranges for 4 of 8 outputs and conservatism for the rest. (c) **Wall-clock time** for computing the internal representation versus running the full double-description route, on twelve sample networks. No comparison against other verifiers on a shared benchmark, no certification-rate table, no baselines beyond citing what other tools achieve on the same toy nets.

## What they found

The constructive results are the content: every abstraction has both an internal and an external description, the conversion between them is exact, and the tropical domain is exact on the ReLU. On the worked two-input net, the tropical abstraction returns the exact graph of the output as a function of the input, which is enough to reject a property that "all classically convex abstractions, whatever their intricacies" cannot reject — the paper's central claim by example, since the exact range of the output in the relevant projection still intersects the unsafe region once you convexify it. The projected abstraction onto output coordinates is *not* exact there: its area is 2.5 times the exact range before subdivision, 1.5 times after splitting the input into two halves, and 1.25 times after four. On the random single-hidden-layer sweep, a 100-input, 100-hidden-neuron net runs the full pipeline including the linear-programming check in about 35 seconds, of which the tropical analysis itself is 6 seconds, with the internal-description cost growing cubically in the number of neurons. In the cost table, the C++ internal route takes between 0.005 and 0.2 seconds on most of the Sherlock controllers (0.75 s on the 105-neuron one) and 88.8 and 104.75 seconds on the two 506-neuron car controllers, while the polymake-based double-description route takes from 1.83 seconds on the running example to 1682 s on a five-hidden-layer net — three to four orders of magnitude, which the authors attribute partly to the interpreted Perl backend and exact rational arithmetic rather than to the method. The one-hidden-layer `multi` example is the paper's own negative control: seven outputs whose true range is the unit interval come back as [0,6], [0,4], [0,4], [0,2], [0,4], [0,2], [0,2] (the eighth is exactly zero), so the domain represents differences of neuron values faithfully and sums badly.

## Limits and open problems they name

Whether the double-description route is tractable at all is unresolved — the paper concludes that the approximations are usable when *either* the internal *or* the external representation is used, "and not both at the same time", and says so plainly. The property check implemented is only the one that reduces to a linear program over the enclosing zone; checking against the polyhedron itself is non-convex in both the tropical and the classical sense and is deferred. The internal description applies to one-layer networks and can subdivide and project but cannot intersect; the external description handles multiple layers but has no simple projection algorithm, which is why the two must be stitched. Constraints that are sums (their `krelu` comparison shows z1 + z2 ≤ 2 is reachable by the multi-neuron ReLU abstraction of others) are outside the domain except through the octagon lift, which the experiments do not actually exercise. And the whole study is called preliminary: a prototype, small nets, one machine.

## What we take from it

A **framing** first: if the object a network computes lives in a particular algebra, the natural abstract domain is the one that algebra is linear over, and the choice of domain can then be argued from the function rather than from general geometric convenience. This paper's whole advantage comes from that — the activation function becomes free and all the conservatism is concentrated in the one operation that is genuinely not linear in the target algebra. A **warning** second, and it is the sharper lesson: the constraint language of the domain decides which questions are answerable, and the paper states it without disguising it — a domain built on differences answers "is it the same/higher/lower", a domain that can also say "sum of these two" answers a different question, and no amount of care inside the first recovers the second. For our account of a network this is the same failure as an interpretability object that can name directions but not conjunctions of them: the representable predicates bound the claims, not the search effort. A **method** third: the two-presentation discipline (extreme points for unions and projections, inequalities for intersections, and a printed theorem that the two describe the same set) is exactly the bookkeeping we need if our account is to be composed across layers and then queried — and the cost asymmetry they measure, four orders of magnitude between the two routes on the same object, is a concrete reason to commit to one presentation per query type rather than mixing them. Cross-reference run 616's containment-terminated iteration and run 614's answer-equivalence: all three say the account is scored by what it can decide, and this replaces the decision procedure with the domain's constraint language as the thing that sets the ceiling.
