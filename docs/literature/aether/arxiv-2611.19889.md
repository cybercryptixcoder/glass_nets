# Illusion of Explainability: A Sanity Check for Spectral Band Attribution in Satellite Time-Series Crop Classification

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.19889
- **Authors / organisation:** Sumit Tahiliani, Sandarbh Agrawal, Rakshith Sathish, Ashwathi Nambiar — SatSure Analytics India; Avi Mathur — Shiv Nadar University
- **Date:** 2026-11-15
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.19889
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Attribution maps get validated in practice by two cheap checks: several methods agree with each other, and the features they highlight match what domain experts already believe. The authors ask whether either check actually licenses the downstream use people make of the ranking — here, deciding which spectral channels a sensor should carry. Their claim is that both checks test *plausibility of a frozen model's response*, not *necessity of a feature for the task*, and that in an input space with strong redundancy a plausible and apparently faithful ranking can be flatly uninformative about which features to keep. The larger goal is a discipline for explaining models whose inputs are correlated by construction, which is the common case for sensors, embeddings and residual streams. Their contribution is diagnostic only: no new attribution method is proposed and the vegetation-band finding is not disputed.

## What they studied
Sentinel-2 multispectral time series on the PASTIS crop-segmentation benchmark (2,433 patches of 128×128 over four French regions, ten spectral bands, 38–61 acquisitions each, 18 classes plus a background that is ignored). Model: U-TAE, a U-Net encoder with a temporal self-attention bottleneck, trained with focal loss and AdamW; two analysed settings — the full 18-class task (fixed-split mIoU 0.674 ± 0.012, five-fold 0.680 ± 0.010 against the reference implementation's 0.631) and a reduced seven-class task (mIoU 0.913) restricted to seven well-separated crops so that attributions are not driven by classes the model cannot segment. Unit of analysis: **bands**, ranked per class and aggregated into one global ordering of length ten.

## How they did it
Three attribution methods produce one score per class and band: GradientSHAP (expected gradients over a background distribution of real training time series, 50 Monte Carlo samples per patch and class, summed over timesteps), Shapley sampling (Monte-Carlo permutation estimator with antithetic pairs, 30 orderings per patch, bands added one at a time to an all-zero input), and Occlusion (each band zeroed across the whole series, averaged over ground-truth pixels). Per-class rankings are compared pairwise with Spearman ρ, then aggregated four ways. Faithfulness is measured with deletion and insertion AUC in ranked order, each against a random-ordering control and each repeated under three removal baselines — zero-fill, the per-band dataset mean, and a *marginal* baseline that swaps in the same band from another patch so the distribution is preserved but dependence on retained bands is broken — plus a reversal arm that flips the order. Then the arm that matters: U-TAE is **retrained from scratch** on the five highest- and five lowest-ranked bands, on random five-band sets, on top/bottom-k for k = 1, 2, 3, and on all ten — five seeds per five-band arm, three for smaller ones, every compared pair holding two 10 m bands so resolution does not confound — with a rotated test fold, twenty further random five-band subsets (23 subsets at matched seed), and the whole comparison repeated in the 18-class label space. Finally a redundancy analysis: participation ratio PR = (Σλ)²/Σλ² of the k×k band correlation matrix and variance coverage VC of all ten bands, computed for all 252 five-band subsets and for every subset size, with gradient-boosted-tree versions of both to cover nonlinear dependence and a gradient-boosted pixel classifier as a task probe. Statistics: Welch's t and exact permutation tests at seed level, paired t and sign-flip tests at fold level, Holm correction over six pairwise comparisons, and TOST equivalence against a margin of twice the pooled seed SD (0.008 mIoU).

## What counts as an explanation here
A ranked list of input features with numerical importance scores — a post-hoc, model-specific, partial account of a stream of input channels. It is explicitly not a claim about the computation: the authors say a ranking "describes the solution this model happened to find" and separates three grades of evidence the explanation can be held to — plausibility, faithfulness, and selection utility after retraining — arguing only the third tests necessity. The explanation is not expected to predict anything about a differently trained model, and the paper's central demonstration is that two equally accurate models' rankings disagree precisely in the part a practitioner would act on.

## How they judged success
Five checks, in increasing strictness: (i) all three methods concentrate on red, red-edge, NIR and SWIR; (ii) per-class cross-method Spearman ρ and top-band agreement rate; (iii) deletion/insertion AUC against a random-order control under three removal baselines, and the same curves reversed; (iv) retrained mIoU/macro-F1 of top-5 versus bottom-5 versus random-5, with seed and fold variance separated and Holm-corrected p-values; (v) ranking stability across two equally accurate runs and two extra seeded models, plus the retraining verdict recomputed on the second run's ranking and on a magnitude aggregation. Redundancy is measured by PR and VC, and predictive validity of the redundancy measures by their rank correlation with retrained accuracy across the 23 subsets.

## What they found
Every plausibility and faithfulness check passes. Top bands converge on B07, B11, B05, B08, B06 and the three methods broadly agree in aggregate; Shapley and Occlusion beat their random controls on deletion and insertion under zero-fill (deletion AUC 0.074–0.079 against 0.245 random) and their advantage survives the mean and marginal baselines, though it shrinks (Shapley's deletion gain over random falls from 0.171 to 0.118 then 0.074), while GradientSHAP does not beat its own control under zero-filling (0.260 against 0.245). Agreement is fragile underneath the aggregate: mean per-class ρ runs from 0.09 to 0.79, Shapley–Occlusion agreeing far better (0.78) than either with GradientSHAP (0.16, 0.09), and decorrelating the bands by PCA raises GradientSHAP's agreement to 0.61 and 0.57 — so the disagreement is mostly a property of the correlated input, not of the methods. The global ranking also depends on aggregation: B07 under signed averaging, B08 under magnitude averaging, B04 under per-class normalisation. Retraining fails the ranking even more sharply: in the seven-class setting Run A's top-5 reaches 0.8893 ± 0.0044 mIoU against bottom-5 0.9047 ± 0.0027 (Δ = −0.0155, permutation p = 0.008, Holm 0.048) and random-5 0.9021; the top-5 is the worst of all 23 retrained subsets at matched seed; bottom-5 beats it on four of five folds and ties on the fifth. Yet Run B's ranking — same code, same seed, same accuracy 0.9137, differing only by exchanging B12 for B05 in the top five — retrains in the opposite direction (0.9053 vs 0.8982, p = 0.010, higher on every seed). The 18-class setting shows no significant top/bottom difference either way (top slightly ahead by 0.005–0.006, p = 0.064/0.098) and every five-band subset loses 0.026–0.032 mIoU against all ten. Rankings across four models share only B04, B07 and B08 in the Shapley top five. The redundancy explanation is quantitative: PR spans 1.35–2.62 across all 252 five-band subsets (1.08–1.45 on unfiltered pixels), well below 5, and the leading three principal components explain 98.4% of spectral variance; a PCA-10 model matches the raw-band model (0.9144 vs 0.9131). A pixel-level classifier saturates after about four bands and, unlike U-TAE, prefers the Shapley top-5 (92nd percentile), and no redundancy measure predicts U-TAE retrained accuracy (|ρ| ≤ 0.30).

## Limits and open problems they name
One backbone (U-TAE) on one dataset (PASTIS), so generalisation to other architectures and to other spectral-correlation regimes is untested. Rankings come from a ten-band model and are used to choose five-band retrains, which need not transfer. The seven-class setting favours well-separated classes (the 18-class arm partially checks this), and Run A's ranking is computed on the same test fold that evaluates its fixed-split retraining — a bias that can only help the top-ranked arm. Arms use at most five seeds, non-zero baselines are used for faithfulness only and not for the retraining arms, and temporal and spatial attribution are not studied at all.

## What we take from it
A **measure and a warning**, in the exact form our battery is missing. The measure: retrain-from-scratch on the selected subset is the only check here that tests necessity, and its verdict can invert between two equally accurate models whose rankings differ by one item — so any "these units carry the task" claim we make from an attribution or a probe ranking should carry a top-k-versus-bottom-k retrain, or at minimum a statement that it does not, because masking-based faithfulness and cross-method agreement are both compatible with a ranking that points away from the best subset. The warning is the near-duplicate pair: two runs identical except for GPU nondeterminism, equal to four decimal places in accuracy, produce opposite retraining verdicts and different top bands, which is the strongest available argument that a single checkpoint's ranking is not a property of the task. Two cheap habits transfer directly. Report attribution rankings under **two aggregations** (signed and magnitude, or normalised and pooled) and from **two training runs**, because here either choice alone reverses the conclusion. And print an **effective-dimensionality number** for the unit axis — participation ratio on the correlation matrix is one line, needs no labels and no trained model — since PR 1.35–2.62 over five units is the signal that many subsets suffice and that fine-grained ranking is not identifiable; the companion caution is that the same PR did not predict which subset retrains best, so it is a warning light rather than a selection criterion. Finally, their marginal removal baseline (swap in the same feature from another example) is a better off-manifold control than zero-fill or the mean and costs nothing.
