## What they wanted, and why

A model that generates code and then judges whether that code is right is the cheapest available verifier, but it is wrong often enough that its own "yes" cannot be taken at face value. The expensive fix is to run the tests or to let the model reason for a thousand tokens; the cheap fix is a predictor that reads a signal from the judging pass itself and says when to stop trusting the verdict so the expensive check can be spent only there. The authors ask whether such predictors keep working when the deployment moves to an unfamiliar benchmark or an unfamiliar programming language, and they deliberately split the usual single target into two: is the *program* correct, and is the *model's verdict about it* correct. The second target is the one that matters for triage, and it is the one the causal-interpretability literature has claimed to solve under shift in question answering. This paper is the test of whether that claim carries over to code.

## What they studied

Two open-weight models that both generate and judge: Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B-Instruct and gpt-oss-20b (the latter pre-filled into its final channel so it answers a single token without reasoning, because reasoning would move the read position and cost roughly 1,100 tokens per candidate). Data is LiveCodeBench (release v6) and BigCodeBench, plus a language-shift set of 454 LiveCodeBench problems rendered in Python, C++ and Java. The unit of analysis is the hidden state at the last prompt token of the judging pass — a whole layer state for the probes, a learned six-dimensional subspace of it for the causal methods. Each problem gets ten sampled candidates at temperature one, labels come from executing the benchmark's tests, and problems are split 60/20/20 with all of a problem's candidates kept on one side.

## How they did it

Six execution-free predictors from four families are compared on identical rows. (1) *Confidence*: the probability of the verdict token, or of "Yes" when the program is the target; temperature is its only free parameter. (2) *EigenScore*: the log-determinant of the regularised covariance of last-layer generation states over several sampled candidates, a label-free measure of how much the samples agree. (3) *Internal features*: a logistic probe on one layer's state, and LAT, which takes the first principal component of passing-minus-failing state differences on problems that have both. (4) *Internal causal features*: distributed alignment search learns, per layer, a k=6 rotation R such that replacing the coordinates inside that subspace of a base example with those of a source example makes the base answer like the source; training uses only candidates the model judged correctly, so on those the verdict and the truth coincide. Two read-outs of the learned subspace follow — counterfactual simulation, which copies a candidate's subspace state into sixteen fixed base examples and averages the resulting probabilities, and a value probe, which just fits a logistic classifier on the candidate's own six projection coordinates and needs no extra forward pass. Layer (or temperature) is selected by cross-fitting over problems in distribution and then read on the shifted set, so no shifted row enters selection; three problem partitions with disjoint test sets are averaged, and comparisons use a paired, problem-clustered bootstrap of 2,000 resamples with |Δ| > 1.96 SE as the criterion.

## What counts as an explanation here

A low-dimensional causal subspace — a rotation identifying six directions whose coordinates are claimed to carry the model's internal assessment of whether a candidate is correct — plus derived scalar predictors (probes, intervention-averaged scores) that read that subspace. The claim is localisation, not completeness: the authors do not claim the six dimensions exhaust the computation, and they explicitly test whether the *intervention* or merely the *subspace* is doing the work. Prediction is the whole point of the artifact, so this is framed as an explanation that must pay rent out of distribution.

## How they judged success

AUROC at candidate level, pooled over candidates, with a paired problem-clustered bootstrap (2,000 resamples, six language pairs and three partitions averaged with shared resamples) and rerun noise of up to 0.02 AUROC treated as the floor below which no cell difference is interpreted. Two decisive contrasts run alongside: pooled AUROC versus *within-problem* AUROC restricted to problems with mixed outcomes, which separates "spots the hard problems" from "ranks the candidates of one problem"; and the interchange intervention accuracy (IIA) of the learned subspace, reported beside its predictive AUROC. The central control is three random six-dimensional subspaces at the same layer, used as the null for the claim that the *learned* directions rather than the dimensionality carry the shift robustness. Baselines include label-free methods (confidence, EigenScore), which cannot lose accuracy by construction under shift since nothing was fitted.

## What they found

Both models are mediocre judges: balanced accuracy of their verdicts against the tests runs 57.2% to 74.4% across the six evaluation sets (Qwen3-Coder 74.4 on LiveCodeBench, 58.3 on BigCodeBench; gpt-oss 62.8 and 61.7). In distribution, hidden-state methods reach 0.69-0.83 AUROC for verdict correctness while confidence is weak for Qwen3-Coder (0.64-0.67) and near chance for gpt-oss (0.54-0.57). Under language shift on Qwen3-Coder the full-state probe falls from 0.761 to 0.668 while the value probe and counterfactual simulation hold at 0.728, a lead of 0.042 ± 0.021 over the best non-causal method in each pair, and the paper states this holds in all three partitions; on gpt-oss no method is significantly ahead and all trained methods lose 0.067-0.116. Benchmark shift is worse than language shift for everyone: LCB-to-BCB drops Qwen3-Coder's trained verdict predictors to 0.453-0.485, below confidence's 0.552. The advantage is concentrated in problem identification — the within-problem AUROCs are far lower across the board. The learned subspace, not the intervention, carries the gain: the value probe matches counterfactual simulation's robustness, while random six-dimensional subspaces at the same layer land at 0.48-0.61, no better than the full-state probe. IIA does not predict usefulness — on gpt-oss, layers where DAS nearly always swaps the verdict (IIA 0.91-0.95) give a subspace no better than random, while partly converged layers (IIA 0.54-0.64) transfer better than random ones. For program correctness the causal advantage disappears: the full-state probe loses 0.088 AUROC on Qwen3-Coder and 0.166 on gpt-oss under language shift, and confidence barely moves. Program-correctness predictors do transfer between benchmarks in both directions (0.59-0.82). EigenScore offers no consistent gain over a single pass — at chance on LiveCodeBench for Qwen3-Coder (0.504) and on BigCodeBench for gpt-oss (0.518). The most probing result: a probe read on the model's own generation, at the last token of the code, matches or beats the judging pass (0.895 against 0.886 pooled for Qwen3-Coder), and read at the last *prompt* token — before any code exists — still reaches 0.891 in distribution, so much of what all these predictors capture is the problem's difficulty rather than the candidate's quality.

## Limits and open problems they name

Correctness labels are the benchmarks' tests, which can miss errors; gpt-oss judges only moderately better when allowed to reason (balanced accuracy 62.4% to 67.8% on 498 language-set candidates, costing a median 1,101 tokens). The trained methods choose among unequal numbers of layers (25-49 for probes, 4-7 for DAS, since each subspace costs GPU hours), which could favour the probes in distribution and may not help under shift. Two models, competitive-programming plus library-call tasks, no repository-level work, no seeds reported for training besides the three partitions. The authors name the central open obstacle: whether the contrastive/causal margin survives shift, and validating any subspace on held-out data before deployment. They also note that no verdict predictor transfers reliably across benchmarks at all.

## What we take from it

A *measure* and a *warning*. The measure: report pooled AUROC beside within-problem AUROC, because the pooled number is largely a problem-difficulty classifier, and a probe that has seen only the problem matches the full judging-pass probe in distribution (0.891 against 0.886). Any accuracy our white-box model claims for a per-item prediction needs the "problem-only" probe as a floor in the same table. The warning: interchange-intervention accuracy is the wrong certificate — it certifies that the subspace controls the decision, not that reading it predicts anything out of distribution, and this paper has a clean case where IIA 0.95 and predictive value near random coexist on the same layers. The reusable control is the random-subspace arm: three random k-dimensional rotations at the same layer, which separate "small dimensionality transfers well" from "this direction transfers well" — a comparison our own reports should make mandatory, since it costs nothing once the probe is fitted.
