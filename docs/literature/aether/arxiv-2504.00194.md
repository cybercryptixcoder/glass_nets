# Identifying Sparsely Active Circuits Through Local Loss Landscape Decomposition

- **Key:** arxiv-2504.00194
- **Authors / organisation:** Brianna Chrisman (Independent); Lucius Bushnaq, Lee Sharkey (Apollo Research)
- **Date:** 2025-03-31
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.00194
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

The dominant decomposition of the field eats activation space: it finds features, not the machinery that produced them, and it stumbles on non-linear features, feature manifolds and some forms of superposition. The authors' premise is that the parameters are where the machinery lives, because they are the objects training actually moved, so a decomposition of *parameter* space could yield sparsely active subnetworks — units of computation each responsible for a subset of the samples — rather than units of representation. They also want the account to be intervention-ready: if a subnetwork is real, moving the weights along its direction should change the predictions of the samples that use it and leave others alone. The larger goal is the field-level one, a decomposition that is simultaneously complete (reconstructs the object decomposed), sparse (few components per sample) and interpretable.

## What they studied

Toy models by construction first: a toy model of superposition (five features, two hidden dimensions, sparsity 0.05), a toy model of circuit superposition (an X ↦ AX map where subnetworks share parameters and are non-orthogonal), a higher-rank variant (thirty inputs grouped into six correlated blocks of five, so the true circuits are rank five), and a GeLU network learning five independent squarings, chosen because its loss landscape is not nearly linear. Then two real blocks: attention block 7 of **tiny-stories-8M** decomposed into 100 subnetworks, and convolutional block 4 of **mobilenet-v3-small** likewise. The unit of analysis is a direction in parameter space — a low-rank tensor per weight, assembled into a subnetwork.

## How they did it

For a sample they compute the gradient, with respect to all parameters at the trained point, of a divergence between the sample's own output and the output of a *randomly chosen other sample* used as reference (normalised MSE for regression toys, KL for the probability-output models). That gradient per sample is the object being decomposed, not the weights themselves and not the activations. Two transforms are then learned — one taking the parameter gradient into subnetwork space, one back out — with the reconstruction restricted to the top-k magnitudes over both components and samples per batch (batchTopK, always k = 0.1 here), so each sample's gradient is rebuilt from a small set of directions. Each direction is stored as a low-rank tensor per weight matrix via a Tucker factorisation, which is both the compute saving and the assumption that real circuits are lower-rank than the whole model. The loss is the normalised L2 reconstruction error of the gradient. To interpret and probe a subnetwork, they define an impact score: the magnitude of the sample's gradient component along that direction, averaged over many reference outputs, which ranks samples by how much the subnetwork matters to them, and they also report the logits with the largest gradient under a small step along the direction. Intervention is a plain parameter move, evaluating the model at weights plus a scalar times the unit direction.

## What counts as an explanation here

A set of named parameter-space directions that (a) reconstruct the per-sample loss gradient from a few components, (b) are meant to correspond to one computational job each, and (c) are certified by selective intervention — the direction matters for a small, identifiable subset of samples. It is meant to be a decomposition of the mechanism rather than of the representation, and it is explicitly partial: the reconstruction error is reported as an unexplained remainder, and the authors list the definition of a circuit among the unresolved problems. It predicts behaviour in one clear sense: perturb along a subnetwork and the samples it claims should move are the ones that move.

## How they judged success

Four instruments, all printed: normalised gradient reconstruction error (per toy model and per real block); recovery of the ground-truth structure, by checking that the learned directions line up with the *known* encoder/decoder of each feature (drawn as direction plots) and by tracing the weights through the subnetwork's highest-norm path to re-derive the coefficients of the toy model's A matrix and correlate the derived values against the true ones; selectivity of intervention, measured by perturbing along one direction with magnitude δ and recording the change in output for 1,000 sampled inputs, looking at whether change concentrates on the relevant output index and how that concentration degrades as δ grows; and, on the real models, qualitative annotation of each subnetwork's top samples and top affected logits. Dead subnetworks are tracked by the fraction of inputs for which a subnetwork entered the top-k, and excess subnetwork slots were given deliberately (8 for 6 expected) as a training aid.

## What they found

On the superposition toy model the decomposition split cleanly into one encoder/decoder pair per feature, with the learned encoder directions nearly parallel to the original embeddings, at 19% reconstruction error. On circuit superposition, ten rank-1 directions covering 10 inputs, 5 hidden layers and 10 outputs, reconstruction error 6.4%, and re-deriving the mixing coefficients from the subnetworks by tracing the path gave r² = 0.92 against the truth. On the higher-rank model, six block-level circuits were found plus dead slots, and — reported as a mild surprise — rank-1 directions represented that model nearly as well as rank-5 ones did; training with no spare slots frequently stalled in local minima. On the squaring model, the five per-index circuits were found at 32% reconstruction error, which the authors attribute largely to holding k fixed at 0.1 so that fewer than one subnetwork is used per sample on average. Interventions were selective in every toy model: perturbing along one direction changed the outputs for the matching index and little else, and while pushing δ far enough eventually moved other samples, the ratio of relevant to irrelevant change stayed high. On the real blocks, reconstruction error was 40% for the transformer and 23% for the CNN, with 40% of the CNN subnetworks never active; interpretable-looking circuits appeared in both — phrase and word-pair detectors, subject-tracking, category detectors — but the CNN's circuits read as less human-legible than the transformer's at a first pass.

## Limits and open problems they name

The whole method rides on the claim that local first-order attributions approximate the global loss landscape well enough; the authors defend it by pointing to overparameterisation and wideness of basins and concede it might fail in a future training regime with different degeneracies or with circuits that are not sparsely active. Hyperparameters were held nearly constant across very different models (they name the k = 0.1 choice as the likely cause of the squaring model's high error, and the ranks were picked somewhat arbitrarily), and the real-model results were run with minimal compute and little tuning, so they are presented as qualitative promise rather than demonstration. The definition of "subnetwork" is flagged as unsettled: whether a scaffolding direction relevant to every sample belongs in the decomposition, and whether a circuit described only by its end results is more informative than the features it computes, are left as open questions for the field. Reconstruction error is never given a floor or an extrapolation argument, and there is no control arm where the decomposition is fitted to shuffled or randomly paired gradients.

## What we take from it

Three things we can use. First, the *object*: a per-sample loss gradient with respect to parameters, defined against a randomly drawn reference sample so that noise from non-convexity is averaged out, is a cheap and self-contained quantity that needs no forward-pass bookkeeping, and grading it against a reference output rather than against a fixed target is what makes it behavioural rather than descriptive. We can borrow the reference-sampling trick directly in any probe we fit. Second, the *selectivity instrument*: rank samples by the magnitude of their gradient component along a claimed direction, then perturb along it and check that the change lands on the same samples — this is a prediction-under-intervention test we could run on any parameter-space claim, and it converts "this direction means X" into a prediction about which inputs should be affected. Third, the *caution*: they compare reconstruction error and interpretability across two deployed blocks and the ranking inverts — lower reconstruction error (23% against 40%) came with circuits that read as *less* interpretable — which is the same warning as elsewhere in this folder, that a fit score is not an account. Note also the deliberately printed weirdness: giving the decomposition more slots than the ground truth needs was necessary for stable training, exactly as dead latents help sparse dictionaries, and rank-1 matched rank-5, so the rank of a subnetwork is a statement to be argued for and not read off the truth.
