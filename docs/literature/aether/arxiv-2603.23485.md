# Failure of contextual invariance in large language models

- **Key:** arxiv-2603.23485

## What they wanted, and why
Benchmarking of model bias almost always presents a model with an isolated prompt and varies the prompt's wording, order or formatting while assuming that dropping the same sentence into ordinary surrounding discourse changes nothing that matters. The authors want to test that assumption directly in its strictest form: hold the target sentence fixed, vary only the discourse around it, and check whether the inference the model makes about the target survives. Gender inference from an occupation sentence is their chosen case because the task has a well-defined answer (no gender information is given about the referent, so an unbiased model should split evenly) and because a whole literature reads models' occupational gender skew as evidence that they encode cultural stereotypes. If that skew evaporates once a neighbouring sentence is present, then the stereotype correlation was an artefact of decontextualised measurement, not a property of the model. The larger goal is an evaluation protocol that can distinguish a stable disposition from something assembled afresh at each mention.

## What they studied
Five instruction-tuned models under 25B run locally: gpt-oss-20b (22B), Phi-4 (14B), gemma-3-12b-it (12B), Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (8B) and Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct (7.6B), all but Gemma at 4-bit quantisation. The stimulus set is the 360 WinoPron sentence templates, each with an occupational and a participant role and one unambiguous pronoun of undetermined gender, converted to a fill-in task by replacing the pronoun with a blank. The measurement object is the probability of the feminine pronoun for each template in each of five context settings, estimated from repeated forced choices. Surrounding discourse is deliberately minimal: a priming sentence built from the same role pair whose pronoun refers to the *other* referent — so the target inference needs nothing from it — plus two pronoun-free null primes with no semantic relation to the target. Unit of analysis: the template, the template pair (same template in two context settings), and the distribution over 360 templates.

## How they did it
Each model is prompted to replace a single blank with one of two pronouns, one token, with the option order counterbalanced across runs so that each pronoun appears in each slot equally often. The five settings are the bare template, the same template preceded by a version of the sentence in which the other referent's pronoun is feminine, the same with it masculine, and two unrelated null prime sentences. Every (template, setting, ordering) cell is sampled repeatedly at temperature 0.5, top-k 40, six new tokens, with a strict output format, and non-conforming responses dropped — roughly 70–181 thousand valid measurements per model per setting, above 99.5% acceptance for all models in all settings. Per template and setting they take the empirical frequency of the feminine pronoun, compare it against the unprimed frequency with a Bernoulli KL divergence, and average over templates. They correlate per-template femininity ratings for the target's antecedent (from a published seven-language norming study, matched for 279 of 360 templates) against those frequencies in each setting, which is the standard stereotype-alignment test. They then run a mutual-information regression of the chosen pronoun on five prompt features — priming pronoun gender, role type, cultural stereotype, grammatical case, option order — to see which feature carries the most information in each setting. Finally, because a distributional shift cannot separate the prime's own effect from shared latent factors, they apply the Contextuality-by-Default framework to paired templates in both orderings: a positive contextuality value means the dependence between response probabilities in the two orderings cannot be explained by any model in which each measurement has a well-defined distribution and is merely signalled (i.e. shifted) by the surrounding context — so repetition of the prime is subtracted out by construction. Settling for the CbD variant rather than a sheaf-theoretic treatment is explicitly to avoid the marginal-selectivity problem. Robustness: the whole analysis for gpt-oss-20b is repeated at temperatures 0.3 and 0.7, and a comprehension check (three metaprompted questions on anaphora, part of speech, and the other referent's gender) is run so that a failure to track referents can be diagnosed rather than assumed.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not a mechanistic explanation but a *measurement-theoretic* one: the paper's explanatory object is the relationship between a prompt and its embedding context, expressed as an algebraic criterion (contextuality) that rules out a whole class of simple accounts of the model's behaviour. What it delivers is a diagnosis of a measurement assumption, plus an inference about what kind of quantity a "bias" is — either a fixed prior that context shifts around, or a dependence constructed per discourse. It is deliberately partial: nothing is said about which internal machinery produces the dependence, and the authors offer no circuit or representation-level account. Its predictions are about evaluation: which measurements will replicate, which will not, and which pairs of items cannot be given a joint probability space at all.

## How they judged success
Metrics are KL divergence between each primed setting's response distribution and the unprimed one (bits, averaged over templates), Spearman correlations between per-template response frequencies and published femininity norms with significance stars, k-nearest-neighbour mutual information in bits between the chosen pronoun and each of the five features (with standard error), and the CbD contextuality statistic ΔC with its strict inequality. Controls: two null prime settings that must shift less than the discourse-relevant ones; counterbalanced option order, with the ordering reported separately because the contextuality rate is wildly order-dependent; a five-way feature regression so that stereotype evidence can be compared against the trivial syntactic predictors; temperature sweep; comprehension metaprompts as a competence floor. No significance test is applied to the contextuality counts themselves, and no confidence intervals are reported on any rate.

## What they found
Context moves the answer in every model, and the movement is model-specific in direction and size. Baseline skews differ — gpt-oss-20b is near 0.5, Phi-4, Gemma and Qwen lean masculine, Llama leans slightly feminine — and priming reverses or amplifies these. A feminine prime shifts the distribution more than a masculine one in every model, in both magnitude of KL and depth of shift at the template level; the null primes shift noticeably less than the discourse primes in every model except Llama. The stereotype story collapses: significant correlations between femininity ratings and the unprimed feminine-pronoun probability appear for four of five models, and they weaken or vanish once a prime is present — the lone survivor is Llama under the masculine prime, weaker and less significant. In the mutual-information regression the cultural-stereotype feature contributes some information in the unprimed setting, generally less than mere option order, and once a prime is present the prime's own gender becomes the most informative feature in most models (most strongly in gpt-oss-20b, and even for Llama it beats stereotypes), while order's informativeness falls. The contextuality analysis is the paper's sharpest number: 52% of the 180 template pairs in Phi-4, 39% in Gemma, 27% in gpt-oss-20b, 22% in Llama, 19% in Qwen — the 19–52% range in the abstract — show irreducible dependence on the unrelated referent's pronoun gender even after all marginal effects of context, including straightforward repetition, are subtracted. That rate is strongly order-dependent: gpt-oss-20b is above a quarter of pairs in one option ordering and 3% in the other, with Qwen showing the reverse pattern. Overlap in *which* templates are contextual is high for Phi-4 but below 40% for most model pairs, so the phenomenon is widespread while its location is model-specific. The comprehension checks show the models are not simply confused about whom the blank refers to — anaphora accuracy ranges 0.45 (Qwen) to 0.74 (Phi-4 and Gemma) with part-of-speech near ceiling, and gender tracking of the other referent 0.36 (Qwen) to 0.96 (Llama) — so in a substantial band the models mis-locate the referent, which the authors read as the behavioural trace of the same conflation the contextuality result formalises. Raising temperature increases the number of contextual pairs while their identity stays stable, and correlations with stereotypes weaken further at higher temperature.

## Limits and open problems they name
The discourse manipulation is minimal and synthetic; whether the failure generalises to realistic context is stated as open. Only English templates, one task type (pronoun selection in a gender binary), one norming source for stereotypes. No human baseline is collected, so it is unknown whether people are invariant under the same manipulation. The authors' own reading of the cause — that models conflate discourse referents — is offered as an interpretation of the contextuality result, not a demonstrated mechanism, and they name as open whether this is a limitation in referent tracking or a more general failure to separate informative from uninformative context. Ethical note: the binary pronoun restriction is a methodological choice for contextual equivalence, not a normative claim, stated by a genderqueer-led team. Reader-side limits worth naming: the contextuality rate is an unadjusted pair count with no null distribution, no interval, and a known sensitivity to option ordering that the paper reports but does not resolve; the paper runs the CbD inequality on a *single measurement per condition* per prompt variant in the "steering" form, which removes sampling noise from the calculation but also means an estimated probability near the boundary can flip the sign of a pair; several effects are described only as figures and are therefore not quotable; and stereotype norms were matched for 279 of 360 templates with no analysis of what the unmatched 81 do to the correlations.

## What we take from it
**A measure, and the one to import.** Contextuality-by-Default is the first instrument in this folder that can ask "is this difference a shift of a stable quantity, or is the quantity not well-defined outside its context" and answer with an algebraic criterion rather than another correlation. The rule it enforces — subtract all marginal/signalling effects first, and only then ask whether dependence remains — is exactly the discipline our read-outs need when we claim that a probe measures a stable property: a direction that predicts behaviour under prompt A but whose marginal under prompt B has moved has not been shown to be context-invariant, and the CbD difference is a cheap, distribution-free way to say so. **A warning about our own stimuli.** The paper's central dissociations are all of the form "the correlation that the decontextualised measurement reports disappears once the stimulus sits among other tokens": stereotype correlation to zero, while an irrelevant local feature becomes the best single predictor. That is the strongest available evidence that an isolated-prompt probe battery can rank features in an order that inverts under realistic formatting, and it means any feature ranking our white-box model emits should be re-measured with irrelevant-but-adjacent context inserted, with the ranking correlation across conditions reported. **A framing.** Bias, and by extension any behaviour we attribute, may be better modelled as a per-context construction than as a parameter of the model, which is a direct argument against treating a single scalar per model as the target of explanation — and against reporting a pooled statistic over contexts without the per-context breakdown. **A shortcut worth stealing:** the counterbalanced option order plus separate reporting of every rate by ordering caught an effect that in one ordering looked like a quarter of items and in the other like 3%; if we report an effect size without having swapped the order of our own alternatives, we have not measured it.
