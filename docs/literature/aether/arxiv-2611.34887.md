# Interpretable and Accurate Hybrid Decision Trees with Selective Case-Based Splits

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.34887
- **Authors / organisation:** Cristiano Landi, Alessio Cascione & Riccardo Guidotti; University of Pisa and ISTI-CNR (Pisa, Italy)
- **Date:** 2026-11-25 (arXiv v1; published version in the 2025 IEEE BigData proceedings, doi 10.1109/BigData66926.2025.11402029)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.34887
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 1 (background)

## What they wanted, and why
Decision trees are the standard transparent model for tabular decisions, and the split rule at each internal node is what makes them both expressive and readable. The literature has grown several families of split — axis-parallel, oblique, and nearest-exemplar ("pivot") comparisons — but each tree uses one family throughout, which the authors argue fixes an a priori geometry for the whole decision boundary. They want to lift that restriction in the same greedy framework everyone already uses: let every node choose its own split family, and let an exemplar-based split look at only a small set of features rather than all of them. The larger goal is a single shallow tree that keeps the transparency of a tree while competing with tree ensembles on accuracy.

## What they studied
Tabular classification only. Twenty-five public datasets spanning 4 to 34 features, 2 to 13 classes, and training sets from 120 to 24,000 instances, split into a "small" and a "large" group by an 80-20 stratified hold-out. The unit of analysis is a single internal node's split function and, above it, the whole tree's depth and node count. Two proposed models: a hybrid tree that carries a mix of split families, and a selective-pivot tree whose exemplar comparisons are computed on a learned binary subset of features. Baselines are homogeneous trees of each family plus eight tree ensembles (random forest, AdaBoost, extremely randomised trees, three gradient-boosting variants, DART, and the explainable boosting classifier).

## How they did it
Every split family in the paper is written as one indicator function with a coefficient vector and a threshold, so the greedy learner can treat them uniformly. At each node the hybrid tree asks every family in the allowed set for its best split under Gini gain, then keeps the single best one across families; the recursion is standard CART otherwise. The selective-pivot tree constrains that coefficient vector to be binary with exactly h non-zero entries, so a pivot comparison is made on h features only — the authors describe this as borrowing the shapelet idea (a discriminative sub-pattern) into tabular data. Because enumerating every feature subset is infeasible, they implement four subset-generation strategies (exhaustive fixed size, two importance-ranked variants that pick h by an elbow or by the steepest importance drop, and a greedy nested one) and three pivot-candidate strategies (all instances, random k, and k-medoids clustering per subset), with instance subsampling at a fraction v. Case-based splits are additionally tried under no scaling, min-max and standard scaling, and the scaling that yields the best gain at that node is the one used. Hyperparameters come from stratified five-fold cross-validation on the training set, the reported score is macro F1 on the held-out test set, complexity is the node count, and depth is swept from 1 to 10 plus unlimited. Model comparisons use critical-difference plots with the Nemenyi test at alpha 0.1, and an ablation removes individual split families from the hybrid's candidate pool.

## What counts as an explanation here
A tree, i.e. a program: each root-to-leaf path is a conjunction of readable tests, and a test is either a threshold on one feature, a linear combination of at most two features compared to a threshold, or a distance/relative-distance comparison against one or two training exemplars measured over a named subset of features. The authors also prove (informally) that the hybrid's search space strictly contains each homogeneous family's, so its per-node best gain can never be lower — they present this as a rationale for the design rather than as a result, and they say plainly that a higher gain per split does not imply higher accuracy. Completeness: a decision path fully accounts for a single prediction; nothing is claimed about why the data has that structure.

## How they judged success
Macro F1 on a held-out test set, rank across 25 datasets with the Nemenyi critical-difference test at alpha 0.1, node count for interpretability, F1 as a function of maximum depth, and wall-clock training and inference time. Ablation over the allowed split set, a hyperparameter sensitivity study on the small datasets at depth 3, and a count of how often each split family is actually selected, at what average depth, with what impurity reduction and what gain.

## What they found
The hybrid and selective variants beat homogeneous trees on macro F1 and are statistically indistinguishable from the tree ensembles, while producing fewer nodes; the selective family is also among the most compact. With case-based splits excluded entirely, the hybrid still beats every standalone homogeneous tree and does so at depths of five or six. In the per-family usage analysis the selective distance split is chosen most often and contributes the highest gain, the classic pivot split is second, and the plain axis-parallel split is third. At the root of the tree the hybrid achieves a mean information gain of 0.20 against 0.18 for a univariate tree on the small datasets, and 0.23 against 0.21 on the large ones. Removing the selective families from the hybrid's pool costs both accuracy and compactness. The headline cost is time: average training takes 390 s for the hybrid, 170 s for the selective tree and 435 s for its proximity variant, against 0.15 s for a univariate tree; inference is 0.32 s, 0.52 s and 0.91 s against 0.0001 s. The sensitivity study found that sampling 10% of instances for split search was the best accuracy-speed compromise, that the four feature-subset strategies performed comparably (so the efficient importance-with-elbow variant was kept), and that no scaling beat the others clearly — raw unscaled distances were kept partly because they read better.

## Limits and open problems they name
Speed: the search over split types and feature subsets is orders of magnitude slower to train and to serve than a standard tree, and the authors propose bagging and per-node feature reduction as future fixes. No non-tabular data — the shapelet inspiration is imported but not tested on sequences. Case-based splits are sensitive to preprocessing and the choice is made per node by gain, which the sensitivity study could not settle. Comparisons are against greedy trees; optimal and generative tree learners are named as missing baselines, and the optimal learner was run only on the ten smallest datasets, which they judged too few for a statistical test. Human-subject validation of whether these trees are actually easier to read is left for future work.

## What we take from it
A framing, from the interpretable-model literature rather than the interpretability-of-networks one: a **heterogeneous split set scored by one common criterion** (here Gini gain) gives a monotone improvement over any single family at the level of the criterion, and the authors still warn it buys no guarantee at the level of the outcome. That split between the criterion and the outcome is the useful borrow. Two smaller ones: they report how often each split family is *selected* at nodes alongside how much each contributes, which is a cheap way to check that a component is used for the reason its authors claim; and their own result shows the selection preference going to the split family with the most parameters — which is the overfitting risk they name, and a standing reason not to read "most frequently chosen split type" as "most meaningful test". Everything here is supervised with labels on small tabular data, so nothing about reading weights, probing a network, or predicting under intervention transfers — the entry is filed as background and should not be cited as support for any method we propose on neural networks.
