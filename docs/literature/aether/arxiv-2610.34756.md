# Mechanistic Insights into LoRA: Layer Sparsity for Adaptive Fine-Tuning via Path Patching

- **Key:** arxiv-2610.34756
- **Authors / organisation:** Fei Zuo, Yizhou Huang, Kezhi Wang; East China Normal University, Brunel University of London
- **Date:** 2026-10-26
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.34756
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Standard LoRA adapts every transformer layer at the same rank, which implicitly assumes layers contribute equally to the adaptation. The authors ask two things: is the causal contribution of LoRA to a task spread uniformly over layers and component types, and are the layers that matter the same across tasks? If contributions are skewed and task-dependent, then adaptation capacity is being wasted, and — more importantly for our purposes — the pattern of skew is a fact about where a fine-tune writes into the network. The larger goal is a calibration-driven method that picks layers before training starts, using only interventions on the frozen base model, so that the interpretability analysis is not just commentary but the allocator.

## What they studied
Base models: Qwen2.5-0.5B (24 layers) for the mechanistic study, Qwen2.5-1.5B (28 layers) for the main downstream comparison, and six models spanning 0.5B–32B (Qwen2.5, Qwen3, LLaMA-3) for generalisation. Adapters on the query/key/value projections, rank 8 for the analysis and rank 4–32 downstream, trained on four instruction corpora (Alpaca-ZH, Alpaca-EN, Code-Alpaca, Dolly) and evaluated on eight commonsense and three maths benchmarks. Unit of analysis: the **layer block** (attention output and MLP output separately), with a secondary decomposition of attention into Q, K and V projections; the object of study is the *adaptation effect*, not a behaviour.

## How they did it
Two measurements, deliberately kept apart. First, with a trained adapter in place, a **contribution score** for component c is the absolute change in the probability of the adapted model's own top token when c is zero-ablated — Equation 1 — aggregated over a calibration set (Equation 2). Ablating the MLP output while the adapter lives on Q/K/V is what lets the MLP appear to "mediate" adaptation: the adapter shifts the residual stream, and a downstream MLP pass it through. Second, and this is the part the method actually uses, the **calibration score** is computed on the **frozen base model only**, with no adapter trained: for each input take the base model's predicted token, zero each layer's attention output and its MLP output in separate forward passes, sum the two absolute probability drops per layer, and average over inputs. Top-k layers are then selected and only those carry adapters; unselected layers stay frozen. The calibration is gradient-free, costs O(N·L) forward passes (20–62 s on one A100), and defaults to 30 inputs. Controls: random, first-k (sequential) and last-k (reverse) selection at matched layer budget; a sample-size sweep; repeated calibration draws to measure selection stability; a cross-distribution calibration arm (calibrate on one corpus, train on another); and a labelled-reference arm replacing the predicted token with the gold token.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **ranking of layers by causal necessity under zero ablation**, plus a two-level share decomposition (MLP versus attention; within attention, Q versus K versus V). It is explicitly partial — a layer is a coarse object, the paper says so in its own limitations and suggests pushing to heads or projections — and the thing it claims to predict is narrowly defined: which layers are worth giving capacity to. The paper is careful in one place that matters: it never claims the ranking is the mechanism of LoRA. It claims the ranking agrees with a trained-adapter measurement and that using it improves fine-tuning.

## How they judged success
Three separable yardsticks, and they are separable in the results too. (a) Agreement between the frozen-base score and the trained-adapter contribution: Spearman ρ and top-k intersection (recovery) over five adapter configurations; Pearson on raw scores. (b) Stability of the selection itself: Jaccard between top-k sets drawn from independent calibration samples, and from calibration sets of different sizes. (c) Downstream: commonsense and maths accuracy against full-layer LoRA, LoRA+ and DoRA at matched rank, with layer budget as the controlled variable, in a single-seed main table plus a three-run supplementary table with paired per-seed gains; a perplexity-versus-layer-budget curve against the three selection controls; and a subset of the architecture families. Random/sequential/reverse at matched budget is the control that carries the layer-selection claim; there is no randomised-label or shuffled-score arm, and no arm where adapters are placed by an *uninformative but equally deep* selection.

## What they found
The adaptation effect is non-uniform: MLP blocks account for 58.4% against 41.6% for attention, and the ratio is remarkably flat across four very different corpora (57.3–59.5%), while within attention the value projection takes about 46% against roughly 27% each for Q and K (44.3–47.7% per corpus). An independent output-replacement experiment reproduces the MLP majority in two adapter configurations and, notably, the **no-LoRA reference already shows 56–60% MLP share**, so part of the "MLP dominance" is a property of the model rather than of the adapter — the adapter's own increment is +3.0 to +6.3 points. Layer importance across depth is concentrated at the front with task-specific middle and late layers; across tasks the top-7 selections overlap at a mean pairwise Jaccard of only **39.5%** (min 27.3%, max 75.0% between the two Alpaca languages), with only layers 0–2 selected by all four tasks. Frozen-base scores agree with trained-adapter contributions at Spearman 0.630–0.893 (higher for the Q/V configurations) and top-7 recovery 5/7 throughout. Selection stability is moderate, not high: Jaccard 0.617–0.747 across five draws at N = 30 over six models, rising from 0.524 at N = 10 to 0.685 at N = 100. Downstream, adapting 8 of 28 layers beats full-layer LoRA at every rank tested: commonsense 59.1 vs 56.9 at r16, 62.2 vs 60.4 at r32, with LoRA+ and DoRA in between; on the perplexity-versus-budget curve the largest relative gain over random selection is 25.0% at 12 of 36 layers (12.32 vs 16.42), with sequential at 14.06 and reverse at 15.00, and all four strategies collapse to the identical 5.75 when the budget covers every layer. Cross-model, the 30% budget gives +1.2 to +2.2 points on four small models, a tie at 8B and −0.2 at 32B; three-run means are positive everywhere but shrink to +0.17 ± 0.15 at 8B and +0.23 ± 0.38 at 32B. Cross-distribution calibration retains usable accuracy (72.8–73.5% against 75.2% matched and 70.2% random) at selection Jaccard 0.45–0.51.

## Limits and open problems they name
Layer granularity only, though their own findings (MLP majority, V dominance) argue for finer selection; a calibration cost that grows from O(L) to O(L·C) if that is pursued. Task coverage is reasoning benchmarks — generative and long-context tasks may place importance differently. No theory for why early layers dominate or why task-specific patterns sit at particular depths. Selection is fixed before training, with re-calibration during training left open. Visible but unstated: the main downstream table is seed 42 alone, and the selection stability of roughly 0.62–0.75 means two calibration draws agree on only about two-thirds of the chosen layers, which bounds how task-specific the selection can really be; the 30%-budget advantage is a clear win at small scale and has effectively vanished by 32B.

## What we take from it
Three things, one of which is not the paper's own claim.

**A method we can use directly:** a frozen-base zero-ablation score is a legitimately cheap layer-importance instrument — no adapter, no gradients, O(N·L) forward passes — and the paper's own validation of it is the right shape (rank agreement against a trained-adapter measurement *and* stability across draws *and* a matched-budget downstream control, three separate numbers rather than one). For our battery, this is the template for "cheap score, then check the score against the expensive object it is standing in for."

**A measure worth stealing as a diagnostic:** report **selection Jaccard across independent draws** for any layer- or component-ranking method, and report the downstream effect *as a function of that stability*. A ranking at Jaccard 0.65 can still be useful, as here, but the honest reading is "the score identifies a region, not a set," and that is the same conclusion our folder reached from the Circuit Oracle pass-to-pass Jaccard of 0.51 — a reader or scorer that lands in the same region while naming different components.

**A warning about share decompositions:** the MLP-versus-attention split of 58/42 is reproduced by the frozen base model *without any adapter* at 56–60%, so the headline share is mostly a property of the network, and only the few-point gap is attributable to the adaptation. Any of our entries that decomposes an effect into "component type X carries N%" needs the same no-intervention reference in the same table, otherwise the number describes the model's general routing and gets read as a description of the treatment.
