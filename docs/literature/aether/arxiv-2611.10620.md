## What they wanted, and why

Multi-agent deployments create a risk a single model does not: a hidden subset of agents can quietly push a group decision toward an outcome nobody approved, while every individual message reads as ordinary. Text-only monitoring can in principle always be defeated, because agents can encode information in innocent-looking prose; looking at the agents' internal activations instead looked like a solved problem, with published monitors reporting near-perfect separation. The authors ask whether those numbers measure what they claim — specifically, whether an activation probe reads the agent's *decision* to collude or merely the fact that the agent was *told* to. Their answer is that the field's standard evaluation cannot distinguish the two, and they build a benchmark plus a checklist of controls that can.

## What they studied

Groups of LLM agents (3–8 members) that discuss in public for four rounds and then vote between a fair option and a target option. The unit of analysis is the **(agent, round) activation vector** at the last token of a public message, read at three middle layers (16, 18, 20 of Qwen3-14B's 40), from which a logistic-regression probe direction is extracted and averaged across layers; the monitor then aggregates over rounds and over agents. All agents are played by Qwen3-14B (4-bit AWQ, vLLM, temperature 0.7, 512 tokens per message), with replications on Qwen3-32B and Mistral-Small-24B. Two tasks: a committee task reusing NARCBench's 50 scenarios (2,500 episodes) and a code-review board (1,476 episodes).

## How they did it

The benchmark's design idea is to surround the colluders with **look-alike families that would trip a shortcut**: benign coordination (same brief and private channel, honest motive), briefed decline (the identical collusion brief and private invitation plus one sentence saying the agent has declined), tacit preference and cost pressure (the target is pushed in the open, legitimately), solo deceivers, a control family, and two free-choice families where the same prompt is given to everyone and the agent decides. Every differing text exists in five wordings, and test episodes use scenarios and wordings never seen in training. Three probes are built identically and differ only in labels: the published recipe (colluders vs honest), a hard-negative probe (colluders vs honest plus every look-alike family), and a **decision probe** (free-choice joiners vs refusers, so the prompt is constant). The central intervention is a *context cut*: re-score the same messages with the brief and private messages removed, so only the agents' own replies differ. A pre-registered no-brief test implants the collusive goal as a QLoRA adapter trained on acted colluders' replies with the brief stripped, then compares coalition seats against an honest adapter and the base model on held-out scenarios, scoring also on messages written before any member argues for the target in public. Baselines: a vote rule, TF-IDF and fine-tuned ModernBERT text classifiers, two LLM judges, a re-implementation of a published divergence detector, a metadata-only regression on length and speaking position, a stance oracle built from per-message stance labels, and scan statistics for coalition-agnostic group alarms. Outcomes are reported as recall at fixed false-alarm rates (1% and 0.1%) before the vote, on all attempts, with bootstrap intervals.

## What counts as an explanation here

A **direction plus a trained linear readout**, treated as a monitor rather than as a description of mechanism. The paper's explanatory claim is a scope statement about what that readout is measuring — that the probe's high score tracks *holding an active collusive goal that has not been refused*, whatever the source of the goal (instruction or weights), and only secondarily the decision to act. It is explicitly partial, and the authors flag the interpretative claim as their reading rather than something directly tested. There is one causal arm: erasing the probe direction from colluders' states during generation reduces collusion success, which supports "the model uses this direction" without supporting "this direction is sufficient".

## How they judged success

Recall at 1% and 0.1% false-alarm rates (with the number of available negatives stated for each), AUROC, 95% bootstrap intervals (2,000 resamples stratified by class), forced-choice accuracy on matched collusion/decline pairs, Jaccard overlap between named suspects and the true coalition, live-alarm delay in rounds and its realised false-alarm rate, and pre-registered success criteria for the added experiments (with the predictions and the regeneration amendment committed under a verifiable date). Controls are pre-specified: score before the outcome, negatives that hold the instruction but decline, brief removal, same-prompt contrasts, a metadata baseline, the public-record upper bound, and calibration on unseen wordings and new tasks.

## What they found

Outcome leakage alone is worth nearly a perfect score: a rule that only checks whether anyone voted for the target reaches AUROC 0.990 after the vote and recall 0.00 before it, and the two LLM judges fall from AUROC 0.97 to 0.67. Before the vote, the published recipe reaches recall 0.06 at 1% false alarms and the hard-negative probe 0.86 (AUROC 0.99) — but removing the brief drops colluder-vs-decliner agent AUROC from 1.00 to 0.61 (code review 1.000 → 0.746; Mistral-Small-24B 0.797; Qwen3-32B 0.774), the published detector separates colluders from decliners only 0.568 at its final layer, and a metadata-only regression reaches 0.883 against controls. The public-record ceiling is low because in 57% of committee attempts no colluder argues for the target in public before the vote; the stance oracle catches 0.05 at 1% false alarms against the probe's 0.86, and on matched pairs the probe picks the collusion episode in 1.00 of pairs against the oracle's 0.67. With the brief gone, a decision probe trained on free choices keeps 0.73 against decliners and separates joiners from refusers at 0.98 on held-out scenarios, but catches only 0.45 of attempts at 1% false alarms, and a pre-registered learning curve is flat, so more free-choice data does not help. In the no-brief test the implanted seats vote for the target in 0.961 of pilot cases against 0.000 for both controls, both probes separate them (instruction-level 0.995, decision 0.928 against the honest adapter) and still separate on messages written before any public argument (0.991 and 0.891), though under the strictest cut the decision probe falls to 0.668. The live alarm fires in the round the brief arrives (median delay 0, recall 0.93, realised false-alarm rate 0.008), and grading the probe's direction out of colluders' states lowers collusion success from 0.41 to 0.26 while a random direction leaves it at 0.43 — but pushing the direction into decliners does not make them collude.

## Limits and open problems they name

Collusion is instructed (or fine-tuned from instructed replies); on released episodes of emergent open collusion the probe is at chance (AUROC 0.50) while a bag-of-words classifier reaches 0.89. The no-brief test covers one adapter fold and 10 scenarios, cannot rule out a trace of the training replies in the adapter, and its strictest cut rests on 133 messages. Both tasks were authored by one person; agents are 4-bit quantised, with generation and extraction in different 4-bit formats; monitors need white-box access, excluding API-only systems; monitor-aware agents are prompted to evade but never trained to. Labels come from a generator plus an automatic audit (15.4% of collusion runs flagged as not acted on, 23.8% of decline runs flagged as siding with the target), unchecked by humans. They name agents trained to beat the probe and collusion arising without instruction as the next steps.

## What we take from it

**A warning and a measurement discipline, both directly usable.** (1) The *look-alike-negative* construction is the general form of the control our own battery keeps missing: to show a readout tracks a decision rather than a cue, you need negatives that carry the cue and lack the decision, not random negatives. (2) The **context cut** — re-score the same states with the eliciting text removed — is the cheapest test for cue-versus-state in any probe, and the paper shows the cut alone is not enough (the probe is then off-distribution), so it must be paired with a probe trained where the cue cannot help. (3) Fixed-budget metrics belong in every monitor table: AUROC averages recall over all budgets, and here the same probes that rank well (0.93 at agent level) catch 0.45 at a 1% alarm rate. (4) The **public-record ceiling** is a pattern worth generalising: before claiming an internal readout beats a text-only reader, compute the best possible text-only reader, because the gap may be evidence that the transcript is silent rather than that the internal signal is rich. (5) The threshold does not transfer across tasks or wordings even when the ranking does, so a monitor's calibration is a separate object from its direction — recalibrate on unlabelled honest traffic. (6) Finally, the ablation asymmetry is a clean template for our intervention arms: grading the direction out reduces the behaviour while adding it does not induce it, which is what "used but not sufficient" looks like when measured rather than asserted.
