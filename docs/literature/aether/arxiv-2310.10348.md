# Attribution Patching Outperforms Automated Circuit Discovery

- **Key:** arxiv-2310.10348
- **Authors / organisation:** Aaquib Syed (University of Maryland, College Park), Can Rager (independent), Arthur Conmy (independent)
- **Date:** 2023-11 (v2 2023-11-20; NeurIPS 2023 ATTRIB Workshop)
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10348
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why
Circuit discovery by activation patching is expensive: each edge's importance costs its own forward pass, and the recursion over the graph makes the total grow quickly with model size, which is why automated circuit work had been confined to small models. The authors asked whether a first-order Taylor approximation of the same intervention can stand in for the measurement and get the ranking "for free", since one backward pass through a single forward graph yields the derivative at every edge at once. The larger goal is interpretability that scales to deployed models, and the specific obstacle is that the field's benchmark — recovering a circuit humans already found — treats the recovered subgraph as the target rather than the behaviour. This paper is therefore as much a study of *when an approximation is allowed to replace a causal measurement* as it is a new circuit method, which is why it is central for us rather than incidental.

## What they studied
The unit is the **edge** of a transformer's computational graph: a directed dependency between a writing component (attention head or MLP) and a reading one, including the direct edge from each component to the output, under the residual-stream framing in which every component reads from and writes to one channel. Models are the small ones the original circuit work used — GPT-2 small for IOI and Greater-Than, and the small model of the docstring task — so nothing here is about large-model behaviour directly. Three tasks, each with a previously published human-found circuit: Indirect Object Identification, Docstring completion, and Greater-Than. Data are clean/corrupted prompt pairs of the kind each task's original circuit paper defined.

## How they did it
Two scores are defined side by side. **Activation patching** replaces one edge's activation on the clean prompt with its value from the corrupted prompt and scores the absolute change in the task metric — the paper uses do-notation to insist this is an intervention. **Attribution patching** replaces that measurement with the first-order term of the same quantity: the dot product of the activation difference (corrupted minus clean) with the gradient of the metric with respect to the clean activation. Both signs reduce to the same ranking instrument, the absolute value of that product, averaged over the clean/corrupted pairs. **Edge Attribution Patching (EAP)** then does two steps: compute the absolute attribution score for every edge in the graph from two forward passes and one backward pass, then sort and keep the top *k*. Evaluation is a ROC curve swept over all *k*, with the published circuit as the ground truth and area under the curve as the summary, comparing EAP against ACDC under both of ACDC's metric choices (task-specific metric and KL divergence, the latter being the originally recommended one) and, on the docstring task only, against repeated activation patching. Two further experiments inspect the approximation rather than the ranking: a scatter of attribution against activation scores for the same edges with a fitted line, and a sweep that interpolates an edge's activation from clean to corrupted in steps and compares the true curve with the linear prediction. Finally they run ACDC on the subgraph EAP already pruned, sweeping both pruning thresholds, and compare true- and false-positive rates.

## What counts as an explanation here
A ranked, pruned subgraph of the computational graph — a circuit — with an explicit selection rule (top *k* by absolute attribution score) and no claim about *how* the surviving components compute anything. The account is partial and, notably, *unvalidated by the authors' own intervention*: the paper's central finding is that the scores which select the subgraph are only weakly related to the causal effect of the edges they rank, so the subgraph is justified by its recovery score, not by the mechanism it is taken to name. There is no prediction of behaviour under new interventions; the only prediction in the paper is that a circuit selected this way will rank edges like a human's circuit does.

## How they judged success
Area under the ROC curve of the circuit-recovery ranking, taking the previously published human circuit as ground truth, computed for IOI, Greater-Than and Docstring and compared against ACDC (both metric variants) and against repeated activation patching on Docstring. The approximation is judged separately by R² between attribution and activation scores on the same edges and by the slope of the fitted line, plus the shape of the clean-to-corrupted interpolation curve for selected outlier edges. The combined method is judged by true-positive rate against false-positive rate at swept thresholds. There is no ablation of the metric choice, no random or permuted-edge ranking baseline, and no statistics beyond the plotted curves.

## What they found
EAP has the highest AUC on IOI and on Greater-Than, while ACDC with the KL-divergence metric beats it on Docstring, so the headline "outperforms" is a majority-of-tasks result rather than a clean sweep, and ACDC itself had already beaten the heuristic and gradient-based baselines it was compared with. Cost is the unambiguous part: a constant number of passes against ACDC's exponential-in-nodes forward passes.

The approximation check is where the entry earns its keep. Attribution scores correlate with activation scores at **R² = 0.27**, and the fitted line has slope **0.531** — i.e. the linear estimate is roughly twice the real effect on average. The interpolation sweep shows a concave true curve, so the first-order term taken at the clean endpoint necessarily overestimates; the outlier edges studied all share this shape. The score distribution is nonetheless informative in the right direction: circuit edges spread further from zero than non-circuit edges, and on IOI only a handful of edges outside a narrow band around zero are not in the circuit. Because the error is systematic, the authors propose running ACDC on EAP's pruned subgraph, and the combined method improves true- and false-positive rate over EAP alone.

## Limits and open problems they name
The approximation fails outright where the metric's gradient vanishes: KL divergence, ACDC's recommended metric, has a global minimum at the unablated point, so every attribution score is zero — which is why they switch to task-specific metrics, meaning the method is not metric-agnostic. The R² of 0.27 and the slope of 0.531 are stated as evidence that the success is *not* due to accuracy, and no explanation is offered for why an inaccurate score still ranks well. The benchmark itself is criticised in the closing paragraph: recovering a circuit humans found cannot evaluate understanding of tasks we do not yet understand, and human circuits are themselves imperfect, which adds noise to every number. Scope is three small-model tasks, one model per task, no random baseline, and no attempt at large models despite the scaling motivation.

## What we take from it
Three things. **A method, cheap and directly usable:** for any edge- or component-level attribution we publish, the one-backward-pass version is available at essentially no cost, and the paper's recipe — absolute value, mean over clean/corrupted pairs, sort, sweep *k* — is the concrete form of the ranking rows our account tables have been asking for. **A warning about the metric, which is the sharper lesson:** attribution scores can rank well while being badly calibrated (R² 0.27) and biased by a factor of two, so a good area metric does not license reading any single score as an effect size. The rule for us is that a ranking score and an effect estimate are two different rows and must be reported as such, with the calibration scatter (attribution against measured intervention) as the diagnostic — a row none of our accounts has ever printed. **A framing for a whole literature:** the benchmark is recovery of a previously published circuit, so every comparison in this line measures agreement with a human account, not fidelity to the model; the paper says so itself. That is exactly the caveat needed when quoting EAP's poor showing on the BlackboxNLP 2025 harness, where the same method scored far worse than random on one of the two official metrics — agreement with humans and agreement with the whole model's preferences are different targets, and EAP was tuned for the first.
