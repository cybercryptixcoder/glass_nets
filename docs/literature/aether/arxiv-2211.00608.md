# ReachLipBnB: A branch-and-bound method for reachability analysis of neural autonomous systems using Lipschitz bounds

- **Key:** arxiv-2211.00608
- **Authors / organisation:** Taha Entesari, Sina Sharifi, Mahyar Fazlyab; Johns Hopkins University (ECE)
- **Date:** 2022-11-01
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.00608
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Reachability analysis of a network — and of a closed loop where a network is the controller — is usually done either by exact but explosive enumeration over activation patterns or by a convex relaxation whose bound is loose. The authors want a method that terminates at a user-chosen accuracy rather than at the solver's patience, and that does not need any per-layer bound propagation. Their lever is a scalar quantity they can compute once and reuse everywhere: a certified upper bound on the directional Lipschitz constant of the objective being optimised. The larger goal is safety certification for learning-enabled control at horizons and initial sets where wrapping-error blow-up has defeated cheaper methods.

## What they studied
Feed-forward networks with slope-restricted activations (a sector constraint that covers ReLU, sigmoid and tanh as parameter choices) — no restriction to piecewise-linear nets, and bias terms are declared irrelevant to the method. Objective: a linear functional of the network output, so the network plus a final linear readout is itself treated as one scalar-valued network. Measured units: the value of that scalar over an input rectangle; for the closed-loop case, the state space under a linear-plus-network-controller map at each time step. Networks in the experiments are small: a 2-input/2-output single-hidden-layer net with 50 units for a robotic-arm reach problem; a 6-input/3-output net with two hidden layers of 32 for a quadrotor; an unspecified MPC-approximating policy for a 2-D double integrator.

## How they did it
The solver is a textbook branch-and-bound shell with three subroutines, and the novelty is entirely in the bounding one. Upper bound on a rectangle is simply the objective evaluated at the rectangle's centre; the authors note PGD would also be valid and cheaper bounds are not the point. Lower bound is a Lipschitz-ball argument: the objective at the centre minus half the certified Lipschitz constant times the rectangle's diameter, and each child's lower bound is maxed with its parent's so the tree cannot regress. The Lipschitz constant is obtained offline by casting the scalar network as an SDP in the LipSDP family — a single LMI whose feasibility certifies the constant, with the slope-restriction sector as the free variable that makes the relaxation tight. Branching picks the k_b rectangles with the lowest lower bounds (a lower bound that improves globally by construction) and splits the longest side, the max-min-length axis, into k_d equal slices; k_b = 512 and k_d = 4 throughout the experiments. Pruning discards rectangles whose lower bound exceeds the incumbent upper bound. Convergence is not re-proved: they show the shell meets Boyd and Mattingley's two conditions and inherit that proof, with the diameter threshold 2ε/L stated as the one that guarantees ε-accuracy. For verification they add the standard early exits — stop the moment the best lower bound is non-negative (verified) or the best upper bound is negative (counterexample). The closed-loop extension changes the shape of the set rather than the solver: at each step they compute an orthonormal change of basis from PCA on a set of sample trajectories, and branch in the rotated coordinates, so the state set is a rotated box; the Lipschitz SDP is re-derived for the composed map that includes the rotation and the affine plant, and a proposition shows the rotated-box recursion over-approximates the true image as long as each rotated direction's min and max are solved to a bound.

## What counts as an explanation here
Not an explanation of the network at all — a certified enclosure of a quantity. The form is a pair of numbers (best lower and best upper bound on the objective's optimum) plus, in the closed-loop case, a sequence of rotated polytopal sets that provably contain the reachable states. The Lipschitz constant is the compact, reusable summary that stands in for the network: it is the only object carried from one rectangle, and one time step, to the next. Nothing here predicts behaviour; it certifies a region, and the certification is exact in the sense that matters to them — a wrong answer is impossible, only a loose one.

## How they judged success
Absolute suboptimality of the bracketing (upper minus lower bound ≤ ε, with ε set to 0.1, 0.01 and 0.001 in the timing table), wall-clock run time averaged over 100 runs with variance reported, and visual comparison of reachable-set shapes against two named baselines — Reach-LP (their own re-run from the public repo, configured with a specific partitioner and propagator) and Reach-SDP. No verification-rate table and no adversarial benchmark; the evaluations are three control-flavoured tasks. The only formal guarantees offered are soundness of the enclosure and the inherited convergence statement.

## What they found
On the 2-D robotic arm the method's run time grows roughly with the accuracy demanded — about half a second at the loosest tolerance, under two seconds at the middle one, under five at the tightest — while Reach-LP's single reported configuration sits at about 0.7 s and Reach-SDP at about 1.0 s, so at comparable setting the Lipschitz bounding is competitive without any propagation. The authors' strongest qualitative claim is the absence of forward/backward bound propagation as a structural difference from prior branch-and-bound verifiers. In the closed-loop cases they report that rotated boxes follow the reachable set far better than axis-aligned ones on the double integrator and the 6-D quadrotor (twelve steps, 1.2 s of continuous time, from a narrow initial rectangle), and that branching in PCA coordinates is what makes the set representation usable inside their solver at all. The conclusion states the dependency plainly: the algorithm is efficient when the network's Lipschitz constant is small, and the named future work is training-time heuristics that reduce that constant.

## Limits and open problems they name
Efficiency is a function of a property of the model, not only of the method — a network with a large certified constant gets a wide ball and needs more branching, and the authors say so in the conclusion and point at Lipschitz regularisation and dropout as unrelated-seeming levers. The method targets low-dimensional inputs with large input sets; that is stated as the scope of the paper, with ReLU-splitting named as the better regime for high-dimensional inputs. No bound-propagation means no cheap way to tighten individual rectangles, so the only way to buy precision is the constant or the partition — no per-neuron refinement at all. The certification of the rotation requires the direction min/max problems to be solved, so the PCA heuristic shapes the search without being required for soundness; it is a performance device with no guarantee attached. No comparison against exact/SMT verifiers on verification tasks, and no ablation of k_b, k_d or the sample count p.

## What we take from it
A reusable instrument: **one certified scalar per (network, direction) can replace the whole per-layer propagation machinery**, and it is exactly the kind of object our white-box account should be built to carry, because it is a number that composes across rectangles, across time steps, and across a plant. A discipline: the constant is computed offline and never recomputed, which is sound only because a constant valid on a superset is valid on every subrectangle — so a reader can check the whole tree's soundness from one certificate and one diameter inequality. A warning that generalises: this paper's efficiency, like run 626's, is dominated by a regularity constant of the described object rather than by the instrument, and the two papers from different subfields converge on the same shape. A framing worth stealing: the state set is not described in coordinates chosen by the analyst but in coordinates chosen by samples, with soundness carried separately in the certificate — data choosing the representation, theory certifying it. And a concrete shortcut for our own account: the max-with-parent rule is the cheapest way to keep a hierarchical decomposition from getting worse as it deepens, and it costs one comparison per node.
