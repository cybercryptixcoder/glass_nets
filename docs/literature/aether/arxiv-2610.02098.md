## What they wanted, and why
Circuit discovery is usually treated as a search problem: better attribution methods, more data and better optimisation should return a better account of what the model computes. The authors point out the premise such progress rests on — that the score used to *choose among* candidate circuits can recognise the better one when it is handed to it. Their worry is specific and mechanical. To isolate a circuit you replace every signal outside it with a donor activation, a mean or a zero, and the components you kept still compute, but now on inputs they never saw during normal inference; call that change *context distortion*. If two equally sized circuits react differently to the distorted environment, a faithfulness score can prefer the one that is merely more compatible with the replacement signals, even though it reproduces the intact model's behaviour worse. They call the resulting reversal an *objective-level recovery gap* and set out to show it is a property of the evaluation rather than of any particular search algorithm — because if it is, then no amount of better search fixes it. The larger goal is to establish that circuit explanations should be judged against behaviour on held-out inputs, not only against an intervention-defined score.

## What they studied
Two suites of tasks, all with a size-matched behavioural yardstick. The *Human* suite is four circuits from published analyses in pretrained language models — IOI (963 edges, GPT-2 small), Greater-Than (235 edges, GPT-2 small), Docstring (24 edges, a four-layer attention-only model of width 512), and Acronym (8 heads, GPT-2 small). *InterpBench* supplies ten semi-synthetic compiled transformers whose computation is known by construction (reference sizes 72–587 executable edges). The unit of analysis is a **circuit candidate**, represented as an edge mask (attention-head masks for Acronym) so that two candidates with the same components but different wiring are distinguishable, and the objects compared are *pairs* of candidates of exactly equal executable size. Behaviour is measured on a held-out split, not on the split used to score faithfulness, and discovery itself receives a third, separate set of prompts.

## How they did it
Three experiments, staged so that discovery cannot be blamed for the first one. **Controlled edits:** from a reference circuit they remove *k* components and add *k* from outside, keeping total size fixed, for one swap and for replacements of 5, 10, 20, 50 and 75 per cent (30 seeds per distinct attainable count), generate the pool *before* scoring it, and then simply rank it. **Discovery:** they run EAP, EAP-IG, ACDC and Edge-SP, 30 seeds per task, 100 shared discovery prompts per seed, capped at 30 minutes and 300 updates, and extract each returned circuit at the reference's exact size using the method's own ordering rather than any validation score — 1,680 outputs across fourteen tasks. **Diagnosis:** from the discovered pool they select 100 persistent misrankings (deficit at least 0.5 points, strict reversal on *both* validation and held-out), then search for a *partial context restoration*: a subset of the excluded connections whose recipient-intact signal is put back in place of the donor/mean/zero, circuit masks untouched, selected on validation at five fractions (80, 40, 20, 10, 5 per cent, ten draws each) and confirmed only if the same subset also repairs the ranking on held-out prompts. Three faithfulness metrics are computed from the identical executions — negative KL between intact and intervened output distributions, a logit-difference margin, and a probability-difference margin — so switching metric changes the score and not the candidates. Behaviour, `Q`, is answer agreement with the intact model under a *fixed ordinary resampling* protocol (semantic completion accuracy for Greater-Than), scored on the intact model's own answers including its mistakes.

## What counts as an explanation here
Nothing new is offered as an explanation, and that is the paper's methodological point: it is an audit of the *selection criterion* that every circuit explanation implicitly uses. The artefact under test is a scoring function, and the thing being claimed is a statement about that function's validity — specifically a pairwise one, that for a demonstrable fraction of equally sized candidate pairs the score's preference and the held-out behavioural preference disagree. It is meta-explanatory but predictive in the useful sense: given two circuits, it says when the score you would normally use can be expected to pick the wrong one (any intervention that supplies signals the retained computation did not receive intact), and it supplies a measurement, `ΔQ`, of how much behaviour that wrong choice costs. The authors are explicit that they do not test mechanism identity, only behavioural reproduction.

## How they judged success
The core statistic is the **pairwise misranking rate**: over all distinct size-matched pairs (reference–candidate and candidate–candidate), the fraction where held-out behaviour prefers B while validation faithfulness prefers W, with ties — behavioural *and* score — kept in the denominator and a scale-aware tolerance used to define a tie. Each rate is accompanied by the mean **behavioural deficit** `ΔQ` of the preferred-but-worse circuit, and the family is reported as a distribution (median and interquartile range, and a count of failures losing five points or more) because a frequent near-tie and a rare catastrophe are different failure modes. Paired bootstrap intervals resample prompts while keeping each pair's two circuits together. Size matching is the load-bearing control: every comparison is at equal executable edge count, which removes "the smaller circuit is worse" as an explanation. The restoration study adds a two-split control — subsets are searched on validation and only counted if they also repair held-out ranking — and the InterpBench conversion is validated by checking that the converted edge closure reproduces native reference execution (answers identical at every scored position, largest logit discrepancy below 8e-13).

## What they found
Reversals are ubiquitous and cheap to produce without any discovery algorithm: under ordinary resampling, candidate–candidate misranking appears in all fourteen tasks, at 4.4–7.8 per cent in the Human suite and 0.7–16.3 per cent in InterpBench, and reversals appear after a *single* component swap. The intervention matters enormously and the pattern is not monotone in edit size — on Greater-Than, zero replacement raises the candidate–candidate rate from 4.4 to 53.4 per cent, while on IOI reference–candidate failures are more frequent under mean (33.9 per cent) than zero (3.9 per cent), and Docstring's zero-replacement reference failures fall from 23/30 at one edit to none at 75 per cent. The costs can be severe: under zero replacement the preferred-but-worse candidate averages about 25 points lower `Q` on Greater-Than and on Acronym, and 61.29 points on InterpBench task 93 in a reference comparison. Among real discovery outputs at fixed method and size, ordinary-resampling KL misranks 13.2–24.4 per cent of Human candidate pairs (Edge-SP 13.2, EAP 15.9, ACDC 20.6, EAP-IG 24.4) and 2.5–16.2 per cent in InterpBench, and pooled across methods the Human suite is worse than the semi-synthetic one (18.0 / 27.6 / 41.2 per cent for resampling / mean / zero against 8.7 / 12.0 / 28.9). The problem is not the formula: logit and probability difference misrank too, and all three metrics agree on the wrong circuit in 1,354 Human and 3,019 InterpBench discovery comparisons, with 2,378 and 6,851 shared controlled-edit failures losing at least five points. The diagnosis holds: restoring selected intact-model signals reverses 96 of the 100 persistent misrankings (49/50 Human, 47/50 InterpBench), including 49/50 of those originally scored under resampling; a window of 95 cases is already repaired by the first four restoration levels, and at the 5 per cent level a median of one receiving-node group and 17 excluded connections is enough to flip 60 cases. A candidate can also score above a benchmark's known-mechanism reference while reproducing much less of its behaviour, and a score can rank the reference correctly yet still prefer the worse of two alternatives (InterpBench task 97 has no reference–candidate reversal at all under any intervention, and 18.2 per cent candidate–candidate misranking under zero).

## Limits and open problems they name
The behavioural criterion tests agreement with the intact model, so a circuit could match outputs without recovering the mechanism, and the paper therefore establishes a *ranking* failure relative to behaviour rather than mechanism identity. Context restoration is a diagnostic, not a replacement faithfulness score — restored signals may hand the circuit information it would not compute itself — and it does not solve the underlying problem: how to isolate a circuit's effect without distorting its context remains open. The restoration search looks for *a* sufficient repair, not a minimal or location-specific one, and no matched random-restoration control is run. Misranking rates describe evaluated candidate pools in which one circuit can appear in many pairs, so the pooled counts are descriptive rather than independent observations. InterpBench's semi-synthetic models are simpler than pretrained ones, which is why both suites are reported. The uninterrupted-chain assumption of the whole exercise is that held-out behaviour under one fixed resampling protocol is the right target, and that choice is asserted rather than argued.

## What we take from it
**A validation measure we should copy into every battery.** For any candidate explanation we produce — a circuit, a feature set, a direction, a subspace — the right question is not "how faithful is it under our intervention" but "does our score rank it correctly against an equally sized rival, judged by behaviour on inputs we never scored it on". That is a design constraint with a cheap implementation: build pairs of equal-size candidates, compute the intervention-defined score on one split, compute behaviour on a disjoint split under one fixed protocol, and report the *pairwise* reversal rate plus the distribution of behavioural deficits. A single score comparison across many candidates hides exactly the failure this exposes.
**A warning that binds our own interventions.** Any time we ablate outside a component, the retained components recompute on off-manifold inputs; the evidence here is that this distortion is biased, not merely noisy — it systematically rewards circuits that co-operate with the replacement signal, and it can do so severely enough (25 points, 61 points) that a high faithfulness number is compatible with an explanation that reproduces almost none of the model's behaviour. Our read-outs should therefore carry the gap between the highest-faithfulness candidate and the highest-behaviour candidate as a reported number.
**A framing worth internalising: the score and the search are separate objects, and an audit of the score needs no discovery algorithm at all.** Editing a published circuit by one component is enough to produce a reversal, which makes this the cheapest instrument-validity experiment in the folder — no retraining, no new method, a few dozen forward passes. Coreference `arxiv-2606.06267` (input variation and evaluation granularity as the reason circuit rankings move), `openinterp-2026-cosine-causal-gap-crosscoder` (a causal gap between what is measured and what is claimed), and `lesswrong-causal-scrubbing-a-method-for-rigorously-testing` (an intervention-based test used as the ground truth itself).
