- **Key:** arxiv-2611.07753

## What they wanted, and why

Prior neural-collapse theory says the clean geometry — every class mean equal to its classifier vector, all class means on a maximally separated equiangular tight frame — is what the loss minimum produces, but only when the training set is class-balanced. On an imbalanced set, or when some classes are declared in the head but have no training data at all (the open-set / out-of-distribution case), the same losses are known to give distorted or collapsed geometry, and practitioners have been rebalancing by heuristic (resampling, logit adjustment, fixed-ETF classifiers) without a theory saying which rebalancing restores which geometry. The authors want the collapse story to survive imbalance and empty classes, and they want the fix to fall out of the loss rather than out of a bolt-on regulariser. The larger goal is the one the collapsing literature generally has: make the last-layer geometry a statement you can *derive* from the objective, so that a geometry measured on a trained checkpoint can be read back as a property of how it was trained.

## What they studied

Small supervised image classifiers trained from scratch: ResNet18 (512-d features) and ViT-Tiny (192-d), on CIFAR10 and CIFAR100, and their long-tailed versions built with standard imbalance factors of 1, 10, 50 and 100. The unit of analysis is the *last-layer* pair — sample feature vectors and classifier weight vectors, plus classifier biases — summarised as pairwise cosine similarities (feature-feature, weight-feature, weight-weight) and the norms of class and global feature centres. No projection head and no terminal ReLU, deliberately, so the geometry under test is the one the theorems describe. They also study supervised contrastive feature extractors (Softmax-based and Sigmoid-based losses) on the same long-tailed data.

## How they did it

The theorems are stated for an unconstrained-feature model: features are free vectors on the unit sphere, the classifier is linear with a scale factor, and one minimises the loss over features, weights and biases jointly. The central move is to replace the usual globally averaged loss — one mean over all samples, which lets a majority class dominate — with a **class-wise averaged loss**: average within each class, then average over classes, which is algebraically exactly per-class weighting by inverse frequency. Theorem 1 asserts that at a minimum of that class-wise objective, features equal their class mean, class means equal their own classifier vector, and both weight and centre gram matrices take the equiangular-tight-frame value with uniform off-diagonal −1/(K−1); the only thing that distinguishes cross-entropy from binary cross-entropy is the bias, which is free for CE and a unique root of an explicit quadratic for BCE. The open-set version adds K−K₁ classes with zero samples but a real classifier vector each: the bound on the loss (Eq. 13 / 15) is computed, and at the bound the K₁ known classes keep their tight frame while every empty class's vector points along minus the global feature mean and they all coincide; the paper also shows that the global mean norm shrinks toward zero as the loss approaches its minimum, which is what restores ordinary collapse. The contrastive section defines Softmax and Sigmoid contrastive losses normalised by the number of positive and negative pairs and proves the same frame result. Empirically they train only the class-wise-averaged variants (800 epochs supervised, 1000 open-set, 5000 contrastive, no weight decay, no augmentation, no warmup), report mean ± standard deviation of each similarity over classes, and print the theoretical value in the same table next to the measured one. There is no comparison against an unmodified baseline on an external metric — the globally averaged loss appears only as a violin-plot contrast in one figure — and no accuracy, AUROC or detection number anywhere in the paper.

## What counts as an explanation here

A closed-form description of the geometry that the optimiser is driven to, conditional on the loss. The explanation is a theorem plus a bias-value formula: given the loss and the class counts, you can write down the angles every feature mean, class mean and weight vector will take at the minimum, and you can say exactly which structural relations survive imbalance (within-class collapse, frame-ness) and which do not (maximality of the angles, alignment of centre to weight when the global mean is non-zero). It is complete in the sense that the prediction is per-coordinate and falsifiable; it is partial in that it describes only the terminal phase at the exact minimum of an idealised feature model, and says nothing about rates, finite steps, or what the geometry does for any downstream behaviour.

## How they judged success

Agreement between measured similarity statistics and the theorem's predicted constants, judged by inspection of mean and standard deviation across classes: the tight-frame off-diagonals (−1/9 for CIFAR10, −1/99 for CIFAR100, −1/49 and −1/29 in the K₁ subsections), the on-diagonal alignments (1.0), and the BCE bias roots (≈4.6698 for K=10, ≈6.3437 for K=100). The open-set tables add the empty-class statistics — similarity among empty classifier vectors (expected 1), between empty and known weights (expected 0), and the global-mean norm — plus an epoch sweep so the reader can watch the empty vectors coincide. Imbalance is swept over four factors as a robustness check rather than with a formal test; there is no held-out behavioural benchmark, no baseline method, and no ablation of the class-wise averaging itself on a task metric.

## What they found

The class-wise-averaged objective does deliver the tight frame at every imbalance factor tested, and the numbers sit close to the predicted constants: on CIFAR10-LT the positive weight-feature similarity is about 0.985–0.990 across factors 10 to 100 and the negative is about −0.109 against a predicted −0.111, with standard deviations at or below roughly 0.01; CIFAR100-LT lands near −0.0101 against −0.0101. The BCE biases are the sharper result — measured 4.663–4.669 on CIFAR10 across all imbalance factors against a predicted 4.6698, and 6.328–6.332 on CIFAR100 against 6.3437, which is the kind of agreement a free parameter cannot accidentally produce. The globally averaged loss is the contrast: at factor 100 its similarity matrices span roughly 0.4 in off-diagonal value and drift from about −0.3 in the head classes to about 0.1 in the tail, showing class-frequency structure in the angles, while the class-wise version stays inside a band under 0.024 wide. In the open-set tables the empty-class vectors behave as predicted — near-perfect mutual agreement (about 0.999–1.000) and near-orthogonality to the known weights — and the epoch sweep shows the empty vectors collapsing to a common direction already by epoch 50, before the known classes have settled. The known-class geometry is recovered only as the global-mean norm falls: with K₁=90 of 100 classes the mean norm is 0.0186 and every statistic is essentially at the tight-frame value, whereas with K₁=30 it is 0.1544 and the feature centres no longer align with their weights (0.9871 against 0.9985) even though each frame is still internally equiangular. One prediction they report failing: the angle between the empty-class vector and the global mean should be exactly 180°, but as the mean norm goes to zero the measured value wanders (−0.6181 for BCE at 600 epochs, −0.2504, −0.0492 and −0.0442 across stages for CE), which they attribute to the direction of a near-zero vector being numerically arbitrary.

## Limits and open problems they name

The paper has no Limitations section. The limits it concedes in passing are the ones that matter: the theorems are for the unconstrained-feature model at the exact minimum, so the guarantee is asymptotic, and the experiments show several structures that are only approximate off the minimum (the K₁=30 rows); the empty-class direction prediction fails for the reason above; and the Sigmoid contrastive version needs a weighting parameter in front of the negative terms because its inverse-pair-count normalisation shrinks the inter-class term as K grows, which they flag with a practical recommendation rather than a fix. What is not named and is visible to a reader: every experiment is on a 10- or 100-class CIFAR with two small architectures, the imbalance factor moves class counts but the class set never changes within a table, there is no measurement of accuracy or of any out-of-distribution behaviour despite the "open-set" framing, and the b-shaped root of the CE vs BCE bias difference is derived but never shown to matter for anything downstream.

## What we take from it

A **framing** and a **warning**, and one measurement recipe. The framing: the loss's normalisation is part of the geometry, not an implementation detail — averaging per class rather than per sample is exactly inverse-frequency weighting, and the paper's whole result is that this single change converts a distorted geometry into the standard one. For a white-box model of probing, that is a claim about the readout layer that we can test locally: when we report that a checkpoint's class prototypes behave well or poorly, we should report which normalisation its loss had, because two models with identical collapse metrics can owe them to different losses. The measurement recipe is the honest-table shape — every cell carries the measured mean and standard deviation with the theoretical constant printed in the same column — and it is the cheapest thing in the paper to copy. The warning is the open-set section read carefully: **an identically-defined, never-trained classifier vector is not idle.** It converges to a specific direction with the same confidence and the same small variance as a trained one, so a head slot with no data behind it still produces a maximally confident, perfectly stable-looking prototype. Read against the folder's run-731/732/733 arc on geometry-derived reliability scores, this suggests the vector of a class with no support looks exactly like the vector of a class with support once norms are normalised, which is the failure mode a prototype-based OOD score would inherit. Mechanical rule for us: **when we read a classifier or prototype out of a model and treat a small or zero-support direction as "unused", we first measure the gram matrix among such directions and the angle to the global feature mean — because the theory here says empty directions collapse onto one another and look as crisp as trained ones, and a crispness statistic computed without checking for that collapse cannot distinguish a real class from a placeholder.**
