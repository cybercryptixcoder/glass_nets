# GRACE: Parallel Best-of-N Multi-Agent Analysis of Closed-Form Questions over Dataframes with Guardrail Selection and Adaptive Retry

- **Key:** arxiv-2611.20983
- **Authors / organisation:** Fabio Picariello, Salvatore Cipolla, Susanna Bonura, Vito Morreale (Engineering Ingegneria Informatica S.p.A., Roma)
- **Date:** 2026-11-16
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20983
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
An agent that answers a closed-form question about a dataframe writes one Python script, runs it, and returns whatever came out. The authors argue that this commits twice: to a single reading of an often under-specified question, and to that reading being right, since nothing in the pipeline can say "no candidate here is defensible". Their target is the setting where the usual selection signals are unavailable — there is no held-out metric and no test suite for a single analytical question, and the candidate artefact is a named table rather than a scalar that answer-voting could count. The larger goal is a selector for that label-free regime which judges what was executed, and which is allowed to refuse the whole pool instead of emitting a confident wrong table. Multi-agent best-of-N is the established inference-time accuracy lever they adapt, not something they claim to invent.

## What they studied
No model is trained. The unit of analysis is a **prompted pipeline over a fixed backbone**: Qwen3.6-35B-A3B (35B total, 3B active per token, mixture-of-experts, 262,144-token context, BF16) serves every role, with Qwen3.5-9B dense as a downscaling probe. Benchmarks are DAEval from InfiAgent-DABench (246 questions with machine-checkable gold answers, drawn from 257 with malformed or missing values dropped uniformly) and DataBench (a fixed 400-question subset of 1,829, stratified by file, seed 12345, answer types near-balanced across boolean, category, number and the two list types). The metric is answer match — all expected values correct under 1% relative / 0.01 absolute tolerance and case-insensitive string comparison.

## How they did it
A description agent previews twenty rows and writes one authoritative per-column schema, reused by every downstream prompt. Then **N** code agents run concurrently, each generating and executing a script in a sandbox, with an internal self-repair attempt on execution errors so that only runnable, contract-satisfying candidates ever reach judging. The contract has three clauses: a single-entry dictionary whose value is a pandas DataFrame (no scalars, lists, empty frames or multiple tables), chart artefacts stored outside the result frame with provenance links, and a user-meaningful frame name. Diversity comes from four fixed persona preambles — agent 1 carries none and is a neutral anchor identical to the N=1 pipeline, so the model's most likely answer is always in the pool — plus a one-step persona rotation at each retry and a two-regime temperature schedule (0.65 on the first attempt, 1.0 thereafter). For N ≥ 3 a single-elimination pairwise tournament runs first, each match comparing code plus a preview of the executed frame and required to enumerate concrete defects in both candidates before naming a winner, halting at **two** survivors rather than one so both finalists keep an advice channel. A guardrail then sees at most two candidates, jointly as program and artefact, and returns one of two shapes: pick the least-defective candidate with a business-language narrative stripped of any mention of the selection process, or reject the entire pool with a global root-cause advice and a per-agent critique list. Rejection triggers a pool-level retry (r rounds) that re-diversifies everything at once — temperature step, persona rotation, global advice into every prompt, critique only to its own agent, and each eliminated agent receiving the loser-critique from the match that knocked it out — and every retry must add a new explicit invariant, assertion or state variable rather than a cosmetic patch. If the budget is exhausted, a failure-analysis agent returns a diagnostic narrative instead of a result.

## What counts as an explanation here
A **selector over executed artefacts**, not a mechanism. The "explanation" is the JSON verdict — winner, reason, and a one-to-two-sentence critique per loser — plus, on rejection, a root-cause narrative and per-agent advice. It is deliberately partial and behavioural: nothing is said about why the backbone misread a question, only which of several attempts is less defective and what would have to change. Its one genuinely predictive content is the deployment claim that agents and retries are substitutes on this benchmark and backbone, which the paper states as a cost–accuracy operating point rather than a law.

## How they judged success
Answer match at question level, with a paired bootstrap over the common question set (10,000 resamples, percentile intervals), Benjamini–Hochberg control at q = 0.1, and equivalence tested by two one-sided tests for the four plateau comparisons — margin set to the benchmark's median measurement resolution, ±5.7 pp on DAEval and ±3.75 pp on DataBench, so that a non-significant difference cannot be read as equality. Token usage and wall-clock time are normalised to the median of the single-rollout baseline. A separate instrumented run grades every candidate against gold to separate **coverage** from **selection**, holding the candidate pool fixed and comparing the tournament-plus-guardrail against answer-agreement voting and a random pick on the same 263 decisions. Four limits are stated rather than only found: the baseline and multi-agent cells are not compute-matched, so the end-to-end gain is not evidence of superiority over equal-budget resampling or self-refinement; no per-component ablation isolates personas, rotation, temperature, invariant forcing or the tournament; answer match scores all errors alike; and DAEval supplies a correct upstream table.

## What they found
On DAEval with the 35B backbone answer match rises from **39%** at the cheapest cell (N=1, r=0) to **71%** at the best (N=2, r=4), and question-level it keeps 95 of 96 previously correct answers while recovering 79 of 150 wrong ones. Without retries, agents alone climb to about 62% at N=4; a retry budget helps the single-agent configuration most and pulls every cell into a narrow band. The 9B probe starts at a comparable 37.8% but tops out at **63%** (N=4, r=4), keeps only 82 of 93 correct answers, and unlike the 35B continues to gain from more parallel agents even with retries available. DataBench reproduces the plateau from a much higher base, 69.8% → **85.0%**, with equivalence established for only two of the four plateau comparisons. On the same 263 pools the selector is correct on 66.9% against an oracle ceiling of 77.6% and beats voting (63.1%, +3.8 pp, CI [+0.3, +7.3], p = 0.03) and random (62.9%, +4.0 pp); voting itself is indistinguishable from random (+0.2 pp, p = 0.66) because in 63.9% of decisions no two candidates agree at all and a majority cluster of size two or more exists in only 34.2%. The split inside that number is the paper's sharpest result: given a pool containing a correct candidate the selector takes it in **86.3%** of cases, but when every candidate is wrong the guardrail rejects the pool in only **10.2%** of cases. Cost: one agent with a moderate retry budget approaches the best accuracy at about 1.1× the baseline token cost, two agents at about 2×, four agents cost substantially more for nothing, and latency stays near baseline because agents run concurrently.

## Limits and open problems they name
Abstention is named the weak link: the guardrail discriminates good from bad candidates but rarely recognises an all-wrong pool, so all-wrong pools are usually false-accepted, and since a wrong rejection is often recovered on retry while an all-wrong pool rarely turns around, abstention — not more generators — is where the next gain lies. The database is a fixed, authoritative input, so deployment with a fallible upstream table compounds an error rate this benchmark does not exercise. Compute is not matched between arms, and the same-pool comparison controls only the selection half. The diversity levers and the tournament are adopted from prior work on the strength of other papers' findings and are not separately ablated. Answer match cannot separate interpretation, computation, formatting and abstention failures. One cross-benchmark caveat is printed against their own claim: DataBench's high baseline is partly a floor effect of typed answers, driven by its categorical questions rather than its booleans, which are among the hardest types — so it overestimates the true difficulty gap.

## What we take from it
Three instruments, one warning. (1) **The same-pool selector comparison** is the control our own read-out comparisons should copy verbatim: hold the generated candidates fixed, swap only the selector, and report the oracle ceiling beside the realised score — 66.9% as 86% of the way to 77.6% is a statement about selection quality that no end-to-end accuracy number can make. (2) **Conditioning the confusion on whether the answer was present**: splitting decisions by pool-contains-correct turns one number into two capabilities, and shows they are unequal — strong ranking (86.3%), weak refusal (10.2%). For a battery that reports an aggregate hit rate this is the missing split: our read-outs are typically good at ordering candidates that contain the right one and poor at saying "none of these". (3) **Unanimity as a validation signal, and its absence as a reason to change instrument**: voting is no better than random here because 63.9% of pools show no agreement at all, which is the honest reading of any ensemble whose members disagree — a consensus score computed over a population that never reaches consensus carries no information and must be replaced by a judge of the artefact. The warning is the paper's own: a gain measured against a cheaper baseline that also gets more compute does not attribute the gain, and its four unablated levers are four reasons to distrust the attribution — exactly the reporting discipline our entries keep demanding and our own intervention studies keep skipping. Background transfer: the abstain-with-diagnosis option is the one capability the single-rollout and voting baselines lack structurally, and the same gap exists in any probe that must return a direction whether or not one is there.
