## What they wanted, and why
Most experiment-design work assumes the system can be drawn as an acyclic graph, but feedback loops are the normal case in biological regulation, equilibrium models, and any dynamical system observed too slowly to resolve its own dynamics. In that setting the inputs to classical discovery break in a specific way: with observational data alone the skeleton is not even recoverable, and unlike the acyclic case an intervention on a node does not necessarily orient anything. The authors ask how much interventional data is needed to pin a cyclic linear non-Gaussian model down exactly, and which variables are worth perturbing. The contribution they aim at is a design rule rather than a discovery algorithm — they take the observational estimate as given and ask where to spend a small number of single-node experiments. The larger goal is to make design tractable in a hypothesis class where the equivalence class is a combinatorial object rather than a partially directed graph.

## What they studied
Linear structural causal models of the usual form, with a weight matrix whose support defines the graph, allowed to contain cycles; noise components are jointly independent and at most one is Gaussian, and self-loops are excluded without loss by reparametrisation. Interventions are perfect and single-node: one row of the weight matrix is zeroed and its noise replaced. The unit of uncertainty is the *equivalence class of graphs* that produce the same observational distribution; the unit of action is a choice of which node to perturb next. Data are synthetic: Erdős–Rényi digraphs with edge probability scaled as a constant over node count (so expected degree is fixed as size grows), plus a dense regime with a fixed inclusion probability, sparse runs up to forty nodes and dense runs up to sixty. The matrices are recovered by FastICA in the realistic arm and handed over by an oracle in the idealised arm.

## How they did it
First they characterise what observational data leaves unresolved. Working from the ICA solution, equivalences are shown to be exactly row permutations of the recovered matrix, and two graphs are distribution equivalent precisely when their adjacency-plus-identity matrices differ by such a permutation — under non-Gaussian noise the two textbook notions of equivalence, over parameterised models and over graph structures, collapse into one. They then show a *cycle reversion*: permuting the rows around a cycle reverses that cycle's direction and reroutes external parents to the predecessor. From this they get a strong invariant — the strongly connected components, and hence the condensation DAG, are identical across an equivalence class — so acyclic graphs are fully identified from observational data alone (the LiNGAM fact falls out), and only cyclic components carry residual ambiguity.

The combinatorial handle is a bipartite graph: left nodes are rows of the recovered matrix, right nodes are column indices, and an edge exists wherever that entry is nonzero. Each admissible permutation is now a perfect matching, so the equivalence class becomes the set of perfect matchings of a small graph, and intervening on a variable resolves one column — it reveals the true edge incident to that column and deletes every matching that does not use it. Design is then posed as adaptive stochastic optimisation over matchings under a uniform prior over candidate graphs, with reward equal to the number of graphs eliminated. The reward is proved adaptively monotone and adaptively submodular, so the greedy policy that each round intervenes on the column whose observed edge is least predictable carries the standard near-optimal guarantee for the budget spent.

Since the residual matchings are exponentially many, the reward — a sum over the candidate edges of the column of each probability times one minus itself, scaled by the surviving class size — is estimated by sampling matchings and counting. A uniform sampler exists in principle from a classical approximate-counting algorithm; in practice they use a minimum-degree greedy sampler that is known to produce a valid but non-uniform matching. A concentration argument bounds the estimation error of that sum of products at roughly root-log-over-samples, so a few hundred matchings per candidate suffice. The finite-sample variant adds two pieces of engineering: a threshold on the recovered matrix, relaxed only until the bipartite graph admits at least one perfect matching, and a *safe* row-acceptance step that ranks candidate matches by similarity but accepts only one that leaves the remainder of the graph still perfectly matchable — a constraint-satisfaction guard against a confidently wrong ICA row.

## What counts as an explanation here
A design policy plus an ordering invariant: the account of the system is the equivalence class of matchings, the structural part that observational data settles is the condensation graph, and the resolution procedure is "one experiment removes one column from the bijection". The object is deliberately partial and completeable: partial because nothing beyond the SCC structure is claimed before intervening, completeable because the policy terminates exactly when the bipartite graph has a unique perfect matching, at which point the graph is identified with no residual class. It predicts something concrete — the size of the class after any proposed intervention — which is what makes the greedy selection meaningful; it does not predict anything about the mechanism's behaviour beyond the support of the weight matrix.

## How they judged success
The headline measure is the number of single-node interventions needed for full identification, averaged over sixty random graph instances per size, with standard deviation bars. Three arms are compared: the adaptive policy (in an exact mode that enumerates matchings and a sampled mode that uses the greedy sampler), a random target arm, and a maximum-degree arm. The theoretical reference is the feedback vertex set of the true graph — the smallest set of nodes whose removal breaks all cycles — which they compute exactly and treat as a lower bound on the experiments any strategy needs, noting that computing it is hard in general. Two regimes are run: an ideal-ICA oracle that isolates policy quality from estimation error, and a finite-sample FastICA regime that reports the same curve plus a table of relative Frobenius error between the recovered and true weight matrices. Dense graphs at fixed edge probability up to sixty nodes are run as a stress test where the lower bound itself is infeasible to compute.

## What they found
Observational data alone pins down the strongly connected components and therefore the condensation DAG, with the acyclic case fully identified. In both matching modes the adaptive policy beats the random and maximum-degree arms across the size ranges tested and tracks the feedback-vertex-set lower bound closely despite never seeing the true graph; the same qualitative ordering survives when the oracle is replaced by FastICA on finite samples, where the standard-deviation bars widen but the ranking holds, and in the dense regime where the bound cannot be computed, the adaptive arm still leads the two heuristics. On recovery quality, the table of relative errors across all finite-sample runs is bimodal: the large majority of runs land under a few percent error, a smaller middle group falls between five and fifteen percent, and a substantial tail of runs exceeds twenty-five percent — so the authors describe high-fidelity recovery as the typical outcome rather than a universal one. The reward estimator's error shrinks as roughly the square root of log samples over samples, which is what licenses the sampled mode. Extensions are argued rather than measured: multi-node interventions localise ambiguity to the intervened set and are exact when the induced subgraph is acyclic, so experiments can be parallelised; imperfect interventions remain informative whenever the perturbed row is distinguishable from the observational rows after scaling; and the Jacobian-sparsity route in nonlinear ICA is offered as the natural generalisation.

## Limits and open problems they name
Everything is linear and non-Gaussian with independent noise, single-row perfect interventions as the core case, and a uniform prior over candidate graphs — so the reward is an expected class reduction and no posterior weighting is used. The reward is sampled, not computed: the sampler is explicitly non-uniform in practice, and the analysis assumes an ideal uniform sampler that the paper does not implement. Estimation error is handled by thresholds and a safe-matching guard rather than by being propagated into the guarantee — the guarantee is about the idealised oracle arm, and the finite-sample arm is evidence of robustness, not a theorem. Cycles are admitted but only as strongly connected components whose internal direction cannot be resolved without interventions; the residual error tail in the recovery table is reported without a diagnosis of which runs fail or why. Multi-node and imperfect interventions, and the nonlinear extension, are sketched in an appendix rather than tested.

## What we take from it
Three transferables. First, a **shape for a residual uncertainty**: when our account search leaves a permutation ambiguity inside a block, represent the residue as a matching problem on a small bipartite graph, so that the question "which probe buys most" becomes "which column is least predictable" and the answering probe becomes "resolve that column". This is exactly the kind of internal object a probing campaign needs between global identification and per-site tests, and it comes with an invariant — the components that are already settled — that can be reported as free structure. Second, a **selective acceptance rule**: rank candidate resolutions by score but accept only those that leave the rest of the problem still satisfiable, which is the cheap way to stop one over-confident estimate from poisoning every later step; our batteries should adopt this guard whenever a probe result is used to prune a hypothesis class. Third, a **declared lower bound plus the admission of what it is not**: the feedback vertex set is a bound on *cycles*, used as a stand-in for the number of experiments, and the paper is clear enough that the reader can see the two are not the same quantity — our own write-ups should state which quantity a bound is a bound on rather than letting a convenient proxy stand for the cost measure. The warning is the threshold: the matrix threshold is relaxed until the graph is matchable, i.e. until the algorithm can proceed, which is a decision made by the solver's needs rather than by the data — a thumb on the scale that our own preprocessing must not adopt silently.
