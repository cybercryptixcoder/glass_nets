# Experimental Design for Causal Effect Identification

- **Key:** arxiv-2205.02232
- **Authors / organisation:** Sina Akbari, Jalal Etesami, Negar Kiyavash; EPFL, Lausanne.
- **Date:** 2022-05-04 (v1); v3 read, 2023-08-17
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.02232
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text (HTML v3; abstract, §1–§7 complete, Appendices A, B, D, E and G read for content, Appendix C read for result statements only)
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
When an effect is not identifiable from passive data, the only route left is to run experiments — and experiments cost money, may be impossible, and may be forbidden on some variables. The authors ask which *collection* of interventional distributions, each obtained by forcing a set of variables to values, buys identifiability of one specific effect at the smallest total price. This is the same decision a lab makes when it decides where to point its instruments, so the answer they want is not a score but a bill of materials. The aim is to turn "we need interventions to settle this" into "these interventions, on these variables, and nothing else, suffice". They note that existing work either prices interventions uniformly, or assumes any variable may be intervened on, or designs a set that renders *every* effect identifiable, which is more than any particular question needs.

## What they studied
Semi-Markovian structural causal models with a directed graph plus bidirected edges for shared hidden causes, under the assumption that the observed joint distribution and the graph are given. The unit of analysis is an *intervention set* (the variables forced) and a *collection* of such sets, priced by a cost function on variables; non-intervenable variables are modelled by infinite cost. The query is a joint interventional distribution of a subset after intervention on all the rest, which they argue is general enough, since any treatment–outcome query reduces to this form.

## How they did it
They define the design problem as choosing the cheapest collection of intervention sets from which the query is computable, then simplify the search space before proving it hard. For queries whose outcome set forms one connected component of hidden confounding, they show that any admissible collection can be replaced by a single set — the union — without increasing cost, so the search collapses from collections of sets to single sets. Then they reduce weighted vertex cover, and more generally weighted hitting set, into problem instances, which makes the problem NP-complete and, through the known inapproximability of hitting set, rules out any approximation better than logarithmic in the number of variables. For algorithms, they first show that the obstruction is a set of non-identifiability witnesses called hedges, so the design problem is exactly a minimum weight hitting set over the hedges. Instead of enumerating all hedges — which is exponential and is the bottleneck — they alternate between discovering one new hedge and re-solving the hitting set over the hedges found so far, removing the cheapest remaining vertex of the current hedge candidate each time, and stop when the surviving hitting set is verified identifiable. A greedy hitting-set solver in the same loop gives a logarithmic approximation; a user-supplied time budget gives an "anytime" answer whose distance from optimal is bounded by the cost of the residual hull, which is itself cheaply computable. Two polynomial heuristics approximate the problem as a minimum-weight vertex cut on an undirected or directed auxiliary graph and solve it by max-flow. When the outcome set has several confounding components, they partition the components and reduce to one instance per group: an exact version enumerates set-covers over groups, and an approximation sends flow through a small network whose edges are priced by cost per component and guarantees a factor equal to the number of components. For multi-variable sets they show the singleton reduction survives as long as the outcome variables themselves may not be intervened on, and give a small example where it fails otherwise. Finally they relax additive costs to merely non-decreasing costs and check which results survive.

## What counts as an explanation here
A priced intervention plan: a list of variable sets to force, a proof via witness-hitting that the plan certifies the query, and a cost next to it. It is meant to be both sufficient and as cheap as tractably possible, and its prediction is narrow and exact — that the queried effect becomes uniquely computable from the resulting distributions, not that any particular estimator will estimate it well.

## How they judged success
Complexity results (NP-completeness by reduction, and an inapproximability corollary via the logarithmic hardness of hitting set), optimality proofs for the exact algorithm, an approximation ratio for the greedy and flow variants, a regret bound for the anytime version, and expected-cost bounds in a random-graph generative model. Empirically: runtime and normalised regret (cost minus optimal, over optimal) against the exact algorithm on random graphs of ten to two hundred vertices with fixed edge probabilities and costs drawn from a small integer range, averaged over forty trials per point; hedge discovery counts against total hedge count; call counts for the multi-component algorithms; and a benchmark set of named published causal graph structures with latent confounders added at several densities, each with random costs.

## What they found
The design problem is NP-complete even with equal costs, and no approximation can beat a logarithmic factor unless NP has quasi-polynomial algorithms; the hardness therefore comes from the problem, not from exotic cost functions. The search space does shrink in two steps that matter practically: to single sets for single-component queries, and to a bounded region — the mandatory confounding parents of the outcome are always in any solution, and no solution reaches outside the union of all witnesses, which they compute in cubic time. The exact algorithm discovers only a small fraction of the existing witnesses before its answer is already optimal, which is the empirical claim the authors lean on most: the exponential worst case is not the typical case. The two flow-based heuristics ran much faster than the exact algorithm and reached near-zero regret in most settings on the random graphs. In a random-graph model their expected cost is bounded by the inverse edge probability times the optimum for the undirected variant and similarly for the directed one, and taking the better of the two improves the constant. The authors also observe, and repeated in the appendix, that regret is not monotone in graph size — it depends on where the outcome set sits and on the cost draw — and that denser graphs can become *easier*, because the mandatory confounding-parent set grows and the remaining region shrinks.

## Limits and open problems they name
The target is identifiability, not estimation: the plan guarantees that a formula exists, and nothing about how much data each experiment needs or how noisy the resulting number is, which is the only thing the experimenter ultimately cares about. The cost model is additive by design, and the hardness results are stated not to transfer once costs are arbitrary set functions, since that alone makes the input exponential. The exact algorithm is exponential in the worst case, the approximations all give multiplicative factors on cost rather than guarantees in absolute currency, and the single-set reduction genuinely fails when the outcome variables themselves may be intervened on. The anytime guarantee can be vacuous when the residual region is expensive.

## What we take from it
Four things. (1) *A framing:* our probing batteries are already budgeted, but they report a budget *spent*, never a budget *priced* — this is the paper that makes the design choice the object of study, and its shape ("the plan, the witness it hits, and the bill") is directly copyable. (2) *A measure:* worst-case cost ratio to the optimum, plus regret as a function of problem size, is a more meaningful report than an accuracy number when the thing being evaluated is an instrument. (3) *A warning with teeth:* the objective is always a multiplicative factor on cost, so an approximation result here is a weaker species of claim than a correctness theorem, and the folder's usual discipline of printing the guarantee beside the number applies — a cheap plan is not a sufficient plan. (4) *A concrete insight:* the mandatory part of any plan is computable in advance and the reachable region is bounded, so a battery can always publish "this much of the budget is forced, this much is our choice" before any intervention is run — which is the cheapest honest statement about a probing budget we have seen.
