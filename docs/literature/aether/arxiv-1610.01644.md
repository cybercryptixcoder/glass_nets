- **Key:** arxiv-1610.01644
- **Authors / organisation:** Guillaume Alain, Yoshua Bengio — both Mila, University of Montreal (per-author affiliation lines in the v4 HTML; correspondence guillaume.alain.umontreal@gmail.com)
- **Date:** 2016-10-05 (v1); this reading uses v4, 2018-11-22
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/1610.01644
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 3 (central)

## What they wanted, and why

The stated starting point is that deep networks still carry their reputation as black boxes, and the authors want a measurement that does not depend on interpreting a picture or a heat map. Their proposal is to read every layer with the simplest read-out that could possibly work — a linear classifier over that layer's features — fit it without letting it influence the network, and treat the resulting per-layer error as the quantity of interest. The motivating metaphor is explicit: probes are thermometers placed at many points in the same system, read simultaneously. The larger question behind it is what the layers are *for*: whether depth refines one representation gradually or switches between representations, and whether a layer's features already carry the label even when the network's final prediction looks fine. They present two contributions in their own framing — the tool itself, and the empirical regularity they were surprised by, which is that linear separability climbs almost without interruption as one moves deeper.

## What they studied

Convolutional image classifiers at three scales: the small TensorFlow MNIST convolutional example (two convolutions followed by two dense layers), the Keras pretrained ResNet-50, and Inception v3 after about two weeks of ImageNet training (minibatch 308,230). The unit of analysis is the **layer**, or more precisely any chosen insertion point in the forward graph — probe insertion points include the two sides of every convolution, activation and pooling in the MNIST model, the `add_*` merge points of ResNet-50's residual blocks, and Inception's inception modules plus its auxiliary classification head. No features, neurons or circuits are named; the layer output *is* the object being measured.

## How they did it

A probe for layer k is a softmax over `W h_k + b`, fit by ordinary cross-entropy on the layer's features against the task labels, with the gradient into the model blocked so the network never sees the probe's loss (they note `tf.stop_gradient` as one way). Because the probe's problem is convex, there is no local-minimum worry and "the best linear read-out available at this layer" is a well-defined quantity rather than a lucky draw. At every layer they fit the probe, then report the probe's held-out error — usually top-1 classification error rather than cross-entropy, top-5 mentioned as an alternative — and they prefer validation error over training error but say tracking validation is not always practical. Probes are trained with early stopping on a 10,000-element validation split so that they cannot simply memorise the features. For models whose layers have millions of features, a single probe would cost gigabytes, so they choose among three reductions: keep a fixed random subset of features, learn a low-dimensional projection (which they warn can cost more storage than the probe itself), or average-pool over the spatial axes to leave one value per channel — the last used for ResNet-50, the first for Inception v3, where each probe kept 1,000 randomly chosen features. They also warn that on very wide features a probe can fit random labels, which would make the reading meaningless, and that dimensionality reduction is partly a defence against this.

The experiments run in three directions. First, a baseline on MNIST at initialisation and after ten epochs, to see what random features and trained features each read as. Second, the depth sweep on ResNet-50 and Inception v3: probe validation error as a function of insertion point. Third, a deliberately broken training run on MNIST: 128 fully-connected layers of 128 hidden units — far too deep to train under normal stochastic gradient descent — plus one very long skip connection carrying the input past the first half. They then watch the probes over the course of training (500 and 2,000 minibatches shown) to see which segments of the network actually carry signal. Appendix B adds the practical shortcut of extracting features to disk once for a finished model, since forward passes at about 100 samples per second were replaced by about 6,000 per second read off local storage, making repeated probe passes cheap.

## What counts as an explanation here

A per-layer scalar curve — how linearly decodable the class label is at each depth — offered as partial, diagnostic understanding rather than a mechanism. The claim form is "information about this factor is present and linearly accessible at this point", and the authors are careful that this is *not* a claim about what the layer computes or why. Their sharpest statement of the scope is the information-theory passage: since new information is not created as one moves forward, the data-processing inequality guarantees each layer holds no more label information than its parent, so a falling probe error is not the network gaining information but the representation becoming easier to use. Their conclusion is that networks build computationally convenient representations rather than preserving informational content, which they illustrate with a picture of a savannah lion as a hex dump (more information, useless) versus a human-readable image (less, decisive).

## How they judged success

Top-1 (and discussable top-5) classification error of the probe on validation/test data, compared across depth within a model and against the trained network's own error; on MNIST also at initialisation versus after training. Controls are of the "is this the instrument or the model" kind: probes are prevented from feeding back, early stopping blocks probe overfitting, the untrained-model readings establish that random features already carry a usable amount of decodability, and the skip-connection experiment is treated as a case where the model's own loss looks unremarkable while the probes show a dead segment. There is no ablation of the probe itself and no comparison with a random or nonlinear read-out; the monotonicity claim rests on the smoothness of the curves, not on a statistical test.

## What they found

On MNIST the untrained model's random features still drive probe test error from 8% down to 2%, with the largest single step coming from the first ReLU — so a meaningful fraction of the decodability is architecture, not learning. After ten epochs, error improves at every layer with one small exception at the pre-activation of the first dense layer. On ResNet-50, validation error at the merge points falls almost perfectly monotonically along depth, from 0.99 at the raw input and 0.94 at the first merge through 0.62, 0.57, 0.51 in the 14×14 stage, to 0.41, 0.39 and 0.31 at the last three merges — a narrow, steady gain at each block rather than a jump at the three places where spatial resolution halves and channel count doubles. In Inception v3 the colour-coded sweep over insertion points is likewise nearly monotonic, and the auxiliary head's probes read slightly *better* than the main head's, which they interpret as the auxiliary branch leading training, though they say the gap is small and may not persist. In the pathological 128-layer model, the probes show the first half never contributing: the skip connection made the model trainable by routing around it, leaving a segment with non-zero weights but no useful signal, while the baseline loss and error looked acceptable.

## Limits and open problems they name

Their own list: the depth sweep is limited to these two architectures (they name ResNet-101, ResNet-151, VGG-16/19 and RNNs as the obvious extensions); the feature-reduction choices give some layers an unfair advantage, and they acknowledge there was no simple fair solution — 13 of 17 Inception probes had over 100,000 features and 11 had over 200,000, which they call relatively comparable rather than controlled. Multi-layer probes are set aside because stacking them costs the convexity that makes the single-layer probe well-defined, and a regulariser that would actively discourage a layer's linear decodability is raised as a future direction that would make the probe non-innocuous. Most openly, monotonicity itself is unexplained: they say it warrants a deeper investigation and suspect it connects to the optimizer rather than to anything the loss asks for. A reader should add what the paper leaves implicit — nowhere is a probe shown to be *necessary*, so a low probe error is never evidence that the network uses that information.

## What we take from it

The origin paper for the instrument the whole folder's direction and probe accounts rest on, and worth having for four specific things. First, the **negative-information baseline as a routine row**: probe the untrained model, because a read-out that already works at initialisation is measuring the architecture, and the trained model's reading must be priced against that number (here 8%→2% before any learning). Second, the **data-processing-inequality framing** as the correct ceiling for any decodability claim: a per-layer probe curve cannot go up in information terms, only in usability, so a claim that a layer "has" a fact must be stated as linear accessibility, not as information gain. Third, the **diagnostic use as the model of why we probe at all** — the 128-layer dead segment is a case where the loss was fine and the probes found the network not doing what it was built to do, which is exactly the class of prediction our white-box account must make and the training loss cannot. Fourth, a warning we should carry forward as a difference rather than an inheritance: this paper's probe is a *classifier on the task label* at every depth, convex, and never checked against a nonlinear or random read-out, so the monotonicity it reports is a claim about one architecture family on vision data — the transformer-side entries in this folder (`arxiv-2305.01610`, `arxiv-2410.10811`, `arxiv-2408.05451`) each re-derive both the plateau and the decodable-but-unused problem the vision probe cannot see.
