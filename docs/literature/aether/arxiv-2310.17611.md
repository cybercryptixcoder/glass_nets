# Uncovering Meanings of Embeddings via Partial Orthogonality

- **Key:** arxiv-2310.17611
- **Authors / organisation:** Yibo Jiang (Department of Computer Science, University of Chicago); Bryon Aragam (Booth School of Business, University of Chicago); Victor Veitch (Department of Statistics and Data Science Institute, University of Chicago)
- **Date:** 2023-10-26
- **Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17611
- **Kind:** paper
- **Read from:** full text
- **Relevance to us:** 2 (useful)

## What they wanted, and why
Embeddings demonstrably carry meaning, and people keep discovering that the meaning behaves algebraically — analogies subtract, concepts add. The authors want to say precisely *which* algebraic relation carries semantic structure, rather than observing that vectors happen to add nicely in examples. Their candidate is conditional independence: "eggplant" and "tomato" are similar in general but unrelated once you fix "vegetable", and adding "purple" leaves little more to say. The obstacle is that "independent given" is informal for words, so they take the observation that any sane notion of independence should obey the standard independence-model axioms (symmetry, decomposition, weak union, contraction, and optionally intersection and composition) and look for the vector-space relation that satisfies them. That relation is partial orthogonality — orthogonal residuals after projecting out a conditioning set. The larger goal is a link between the probability theory of graphical models and the geometry of representation spaces, so that a conditional-independence structure can be read off vectors.

## What they studied
CLIP text embeddings, one normalised vector per word, over the 49,815 words of the Brown corpus, with WordNet hyponymy and Wu-Palmer similarity as the external semantic yardstick. The unit of analysis is *not* a neuron, direction or latent inside a network but the embedding of a whole word, plus the linear subspace spanned by a small set of such embeddings. A secondary theory section studies abstract random variables and embedding maps between a distribution and R^d.

## How they did it
First a definitional move: partial orthogonality (a ⟂ b | C iff the residuals of a and b after projecting out span(C) have zero inner product) is shown to be a semi-graphoid satisfying composition, and a compositional graphoid when the vectors are linearly independent. Next they import Markov blankets and boundaries from graphical models: a boundary of a vector is a minimal set of vectors whose residuals make the target conditionally orthogonal to everything else. Because embeddings live in a space with far fewer dimensions than there are words, the intersection axiom fails, so boundaries are not unique; they prove that distinct boundaries nevertheless project the target to the same point, and that when the target lies in the span of the others, that projection is the target itself — an uncomfortably strict consequence. They therefore relax orthogonality from an exact inner product to the average cosine of residuals against all other embeddings, calling the result a generalised Markov boundary, and give a sampling algorithm: draw many random subspaces, keep the vectors whose residuals stay most correlated with the target across those projections, then search subsets of those candidates for the one whose average residual cosine is lowest. Experiments test three things — that conditioning on a category word reduces intra-category similarity more than inter-category similarity, that the random-subspace ranking surfaces more closely related words than plain cosine similarity, and that spanned boundaries align with human-meaningful subspaces (measured by smallest principal angle against a ChatGPT-written description sentence's embedding). A theory section then asks whether any embedding can preserve a distribution's independence structure at all.

## What counts as an explanation here
A geometric/graphical structure rather than a description or a dictionary: the explanation of a word is the subspace spanned by its boundary vectors, plus the independence model (which words are conditionally orthogonal given which sets) induced by partial orthogonality. It is meant to be partial and local — one target word, one small boundary — and it is predictive in a narrow sense: the structure licenses statements of the form "given these, this is unrelated", and the theory supplies the conditions under which a compressed embedding provably keeps them. Nothing here explains a model's computation; it explains the geometry of a pre-readout vector space.

## How they judged success
Three empirical measures: normalised reduction in average pairwise cosine similarity within versus across nine hyponymy categories when conditioning on the category word (with 10,000 sampled embeddings giving the mean and standard deviation used for normalisation); average Wu-Palmer similarity of the top-10 correlated words before and after random-subspace projection, on 1,000 random words and on 300 common nouns; and the smallest principal angle between the span of an estimated boundary and the span of selected description embeddings, compared against randomly chosen subspaces, with standard errors over 50 examples. Theory is judged by proof: existence of an independence-preserving embedding via a perturbed-adjacency-matrix construction, and a Johnson-Lindenstrauss-style bound on the reduced dimension needed to keep residual orthogonality within epsilon, depending on the maximum boundary size. One control appears throughout: words with cosine similarity at or above 0.9 to the target, or Wu-Palmer similarity near 1, are filtered out so that synonyms cannot supply the wins.

## What they found
Conditioning on a category word reduces intra-category cosine similarity more than inter-category similarity, with one stated exception they explain by WordNet polysemy — conditioning on "food" also depresses "beverage" pairs because one sense of "food" covers nutrients. The random-subspace projection step improves semantic quality measurably: average Wu-Palmer similarity of the top-10 neighbours rises from 0.223 to 0.245 on random words and from 0.343 to 0.422 on common nouns, both with standard errors around 0.006-0.008; examples include "eggplant" gaining "purple", "purple-black" and "plum" after projection, and "poem" replacing punctuation marks with "poetry's", "sonnet" and "rhyming". The average residual cosine of an estimated boundary falls quickly with size — 0.345 at one vector, 0.128 at three, 0.054 at five, 0.015 at eight, 0.008 at ten over 1,000 sampled words — which they read as evidence that boundaries are small. Boundary spans sit at smaller principal angles to semantic description subspaces than random subspaces do. The construction guarantees a representation that exactly preserves a graph's independence structure when the dimension equals the number of variables, and random projection keeps the structure within epsilon at a dimension that depends mainly on the largest boundary size and the conditioning of the Gram matrix.

## Limits and open problems they name
They state explicitly that exact orthogonality is unrealistic for learned embeddings because of limited dimension and training noise, and that inner products in embedding space do not faithfully respect meaning — which is why the relaxation exists at all. The faithful construction is worst-case dimension-hungry (mutually independent variables force as many dimensions as variables), and the compressed version preserves structure only approximately. Empirically the whole study rests on one embedding family (CLIP text) and on WordNet plus a ChatGPT-supplied description set as the notion of meaning; the closed-form construction is validated as theory, not fitted to a real model. They name as the obvious next question whether real text embeddings preserve the independence structure of their training distribution — i.e. whether the objects they analyse are actually instances of the class they define.

## What we take from it
Three things, all usable. (1) A *framing*: conditional independence is the right target for a white-box account of a representation space, and it comes with a ready-made axiom list, so any claim of the form "these two directions mean independent things" can be tested against whether the relation it induces is at least a semi-graphoid — the composition axiom failing is a legitimate, checkable complaint. (2) A *method worth copying for our own probes*: recovering a basis by projecting out random subspaces and keeping what stays correlated is a cheap way to separate "similar" from "redundant", and it beat plain cosine similarity on the semantic yardstick while needing no labels. (3) A *warning about the exact version*: requiring every residual to be exactly orthogonal forces the explanation to reconstruct the target vector exactly out of its own neighbours, which in an overcomplete vector set is nearly vacuous — so a reconstruction-exact account must be reported as such, and a distributional relaxation has to be declared because it changes what is being claimed. The paper also belongs to the same family as `arxiv-2406.01506`: both say the algebraic property that makes a concept readable is orthogonality *after* removing a conditioning set, and both imply that a single direction is the wrong object when a parent concept is present.
